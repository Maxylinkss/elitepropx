What to Do After Passing Prop Firm Challenge · reviewed October 2026

What to Do After Passing Prop Firm Challenge

The pass settles one question and opens several others. The funded account has a different rulebook, a payout schedule you have not yet used, a balance that will be measured against a floor you have not traded against, and a handful of decisions that are far easier to make now than after a losing week. This page sets them out in the order they arrive: what to confirm in the first 72 hours, how to read the funded agreement, how to reset the risk unit, how to plan the payout rhythm, when to scale, and what to keep on record.

Key takeaways

  • Read the funded agreement before the first funded trade. It differs from the evaluation rulebook in a small number of places that matter.
  • Recalculate the risk unit from the funded account's own floor, balance and daily stop rather than carrying the evaluation size across.
  • Plan the first payout before the first funded trade, including the qualifying period and the balance that must remain afterwards.
  • Withdraw on every eligible cycle and keep a fixed buffer. Profit left inside the account is profit inside the floor's reach.
  • Scale only after two or three clean payouts, and increase gradually rather than in one step.
  • Keep the payout statements, the funded agreement and the support replies. The first payout teaches the firm's rhythm; the records prove it if a later one is disputed.
  • A second account is a diversification decision, not a growth decision — and it only helps if the two accounts are not running the same trades.

The first 72 hours

The transition from evaluation to funded account is administrative before it is tactical. Most of the avoidable losses in the first month come from assumptions made in the first three days, so it is worth treating the handover as a checklist rather than a victory lap.

StepWhat to doWhy it matters
1. Confirm the pass in writingGet the firm's own confirmation that both phases are completeA screenshot is not a record; the firm's statement is
2. Read the funded agreementDownload it, read it once properly, and save a dated copyThe differences from the evaluation rulebook surface at the payout otherwise
3. Confirm the payout cycleEstablish the first eligible date and the minimum days required before itThe cycle length determines how much variance the account must absorb between withdrawals
4. Find the payout conditionsLocate the consistency or best-day condition and write down the thresholdIt can block a withdrawal from a compliant month, and it is measured from the first day
5. Recalculate the risk unitExpress it as a share of the funded floor, not the evaluation balanceThe floor and the balance may both have changed
6. Confirm access and ownershipVerify the login, the email on the account and the payout destination are all yoursOwnership problems are cheap to fix today and expensive at the payout
7. Set the withdrawal policyDecide the buffer target and the payout rhythm before tradingThe decision is far easier now than in the middle of a losing week

The ordering is deliberate. Steps two to four are reading tasks that cost an hour and prevent the majority of first-month failures. Step five is arithmetic that takes ten minutes and is the difference between a plan sized for the account you now have and one sized for the account you no longer have.

Do not place a funded trade on the first day. There is no target to reach and no deadline. A day spent reading the agreement and setting the risk unit is a day the account cannot be lost on a misunderstanding.

Reading the funded agreement

The funded document will look familiar, and that is the hazard. Most of it repeats the evaluation terms, which makes the changes easy to skim past — and the changes are the entire reason to read it.

ElementWhat often changesWhat to record
Profit targetRemoved entirelyNothing — but stop trading as though a target exists
Loss floorMay be reset to the funded balance, or change from trailing to staticThe exact definition and the balance it is measured from
Daily loss limitOften retained with a different reset timeThe reset time and whether the basis is balance or equity
Consistency conditionFrequently introduced, and usually evaluated at payoutThe threshold as a percentage, and when it is applied
Minimum days or cycleReplaced by a minimum payout cycleThe days required and the minimum time between payouts
News and holdingFrequently stricter than the evaluationWhich events are restricted and whether weekend holding is allowed on your account type
Data or platform feeMay resume or be deducted from the payoutThe amount and whether it is netted off or invoiced
Profit splitApplies to every payoutThe percentage on your account and whether it increases with performance

Two changes account for most of the surprises. A consistency condition introduced at funding is the classic example: the evaluation permitted any distribution of profit, and the funded account does not. A trader who reached the target with one outsized day can find the first withdrawal blocked despite breaking no trading rule. The remedy is more ordinary days, which is a strategy change rather than a disciplinary one.

The second is a stricter holding policy. A trader who held positions through news events during the evaluation may find the funded account prohibits it. Since the rule is enforced against the position rather than the profit, the only defence is knowing it before the trade.

Resetting the risk baseline

Carrying the evaluation's position size into the funded account is the most common post-pass error, and it is entirely avoidable with ten minutes of arithmetic.

  1. Find the funded floor. Note whether it is measured from the initial funded balance or updated to the current one.
  2. Calculate the cash value of the floor. A 5% floor on a $50,000 funded account is $2,500.
  3. Choose a risk per trade as a share of that floor. Ten per cent of the floor per trade means twenty consecutive losses to breach, and a normal losing run of five or six is comfortably absorbable.
  4. Convert it to a dollar figure and check it feels small. On the account above, a 0.5% position risk is $250, which is 10% of the floor. If the number feels too small to be worth trading, the account is probably smaller than the target you have in mind — which is information, not a problem.
  5. Set the session stop too. Divide the daily limit by the risk unit to get the maximum number of losses in a day, then stop at half of it.
Funded balanceFloor at 5%Risk per trade at 0.5%Losses to breachOrdinary bad week survivable?
$25,000$1,250$12510Yes
$50,000$2,500$25010Yes
$100,000$5,000$50010Yes
$200,000$10,000$1,00010Yes

The table is deliberately uniform, because sizing as a percentage makes it so. The mistake it guards against is sizing in dollars: a trader who risked $500 per trade on a $100,000 evaluation and then risks $500 per trade on a $25,000 funded account has quietly increased their risk eightfold relative to the floor.

Planning the first payout

The first payout is a process with a qualifying period attached, not a payday. Planning it before the first funded trade converts it from an event you hope arrives into one you have scheduled.

QuestionWhat to establishWhy it changes the plan
When is the first payout eligible?The date or the cycle length, plus any minimum trading daysSets the horizon the account has to survive before it produces anything
What is the minimum withdrawal?The floor amount the firm will processA small minimum is useful early; a high one forces a bigger withdrawal than the buffer wants
What must remain afterwards?The minimum balance the account must retainDetermines whether the payout can be taken in full or needs to be partial
What are the processing costs?Any flat or percentage fee, and the currency conversionLargest as a share on a small first withdrawal
What is the payout destination?Whether it must match the account holder's nameA mismatch is the most common reason a payout is held

Two decisions belong here rather than later. The first is the size of the first withdrawal: taking slightly less than the maximum preserves the buffer for the next cycle, and the difference matters far more in month one than the extra cash does. The second is the destination: setting up a payout method in your own name, with the correct documents attached, removes the most common administrative cause of a delayed payment.

Building a payout rhythm

Once the first payout has completed, the account has a rhythm and the strategy should be built around it rather than around a monthly target. The rhythm is the thing that makes a funded account an income stream instead of a balance.

RhythmEffect on the bufferEffect on total collectedSuited to
Every eligible cycle, fixed buffer retainedStable, predictableHighest over a year in most casesMost traders — the default
Every second cycleGrows between withdrawalsSlightly lower count, slightly larger paymentsTraders who want larger individual transfers
Minimum withdrawal each cycle, compounding the restDrifts upward with the balanceVariable; the risk profile drifts with itTraders targeting a larger account later
No withdrawals until a target balanceMaximum retained exposureLowest — a breach loses everything retainedRarely justified

The fourth row is worth a specific warning. A funded account that has accumulated several months of profit and withdrawn none of it holds that profit inside the reach of a loss floor that can still close the account. Withdrawing is not impatience; it is the mechanism that converts a balance into money.

Scaling, and when not to

Scaling is the natural ambition after a few clean payouts, and the conditions attached to it matter more than the ambition. There are two ways to scale, and they are not equivalent.

Sizing up inside the same account means risking more per trade against the same floor. It increases payout per cycle and shortens expected account life. It should only be considered once the buffer is comfortably above twice your worst recent month, and it should be done in small steps rather than one jump.

Adding a second account increases total capital across two floors. It does not increase the risk taken per account, which is why it is generally the better form of scaling — provided the two accounts are not running the same trades.

PrerequisiteThresholdWhy
Clean payouts completedTwo or threeProves the payout process works for your distribution, not just the trading
Buffer levelAbove twice the worst recent monthOtherwise the larger size removes the margin that keeps the account alive
Distribution qualityBest day well under the consistency thresholdA larger size concentrates profit and moves you toward the payout limit
Process stabilityNo plan deviations in the last cycleScaling a method that is already drifting scales the drift
Size tested elsewhereDemonstrated on a demo or small account firstThe edge does not automatically scale with the position

If a firm offers a formal scaling schedule — where the account balance increases automatically after a number of profitable cycles — read the conditions and the review timing carefully. Automatic scaling is genuinely useful, and it usually has qualifying criteria that are easier to satisfy deliberately than by accident.

Records and the paper trail

The administrative side of a funded account is small and disproportionately valuable when something goes wrong. Six documents cover almost every dispute.

  • The firm's pass confirmation, showing both phases complete.
  • A dated copy of the funded agreement as it stood when the account was issued.
  • The payout conditions you recorded, with the threshold values written down.
  • Every payout statement, including fees and the net amount received.
  • Support replies on any question where the answer mattered, kept with the date.
  • Your own trade records, including the daily loss figures, because they are the only independent evidence of your compliance if a payout is queried.

Two of those are habitually neglected: the dated agreement and the support replies. A rule that is reinterpreted six months later is much easier to resolve when you can produce the version you relied on and a written answer from the firm's own support. The records cost nothing to keep and are the difference between a conversation and an argument.

A second account, and when it helps

Adding another funded account is the most common way traders attempt to scale, and it works only under specific conditions.

SituationDoes a second account help?Reasoning
Same strategy, two accounts, same tradesNo — it doubles the risk of the same mistakeBoth floors breach together on the same losing sequence
Same strategy at a different risk unitPartly — it slows the combined drawdownThe two accounts fail at different points, so one survives longer
Two different methods, low correlationYes — genuine diversificationThe accounts are unlikely to breach on the same week
Two different firms with different rulesYes, and it spreads rule riskA rule change at one firm does not end both accounts
A second account while the first has no bufferNo — it is leverage dressed as diversificationCapital is split before either account is stable

The determining question is correlation, not count. Two accounts running the same trades at the same size are one account with twice the capital and the same single point of failure. Two accounts running genuinely different methods, or the same method at materially different risk units, have a real chance of surviving a bad week on one while recovering on the other.

When to stop

Every funded account ends. The only part within your control is whether it ends deliberately or through a breach.

SignalActionReversible?
Two monthly reviews with a falling bufferHalve the risk unitYes
Floor within one risk unitFlatten and stop trading the accountYes, if the firm permits a reset
Repeated plan deviations in one cycleStop for the rest of the cycleYes
Rule ambiguity you cannot resolve in writingFlatten until the answer arrivesYes
Three payouts collected and the method no longer fits the rulebookStop, bank the outcome, change firm or methodYes, deliberately

The last row is the one traders find hardest, and the one that most often converts a good outcome into a bad one. A trader who has collected three payouts and can see that the rulebook no longer suits their method has already won; continuing to trade it until the floor arrives turns a good result into a lost account and a lesson they did not need to buy. Stopping deliberately is the final decision on this page, and it is as much a part of a professional process as the first.

The first 30 days, week by week

The first month determines whether the account becomes an income stream. A week-by-week structure removes the improvisation that otherwise fills it.

WeekObjectiveWhat to avoidReview point
Pre-tradeComplete the 72-hour checklistTrading before the funded agreement is readThe risk unit is recalculated from the funded floor
Week 1Trade at the evaluation's size and paceSize increases justified by "the account is real now"Distance to the floor in cash and percentage
Week 2Bank ordinary results; begin tracking the best-day shareChasing a repeat of the best evaluation sessionBest day as a share of net profit so far
Week 3Confirm progress toward the minimum days and the cycleAdding positions to accelerate the first payoutWhether the first payout request will be eligible on time
Week 4Request the first payout in the eligible window; assess the bufferRetaining the payout to compound the balanceBuffer against your worst recent month

Three of the four review points are about the account's risk rather than its profit, which is the inversion the funded phase requires. During the evaluation the scoreboard was distance to the target; on a funded account it is distance to the floor, because a funded account produces income by continuing to exist.

The seven mistakes that follow a pass

Post-pass failures cluster tightly. Almost all of them happen in the first month, and almost all of them are decisions rather than trades.

MistakeWhat it looks likeThe correction
Increasing size because the account is fundedA trade risk larger than anything taken on the evaluationHold the evaluation size until the buffer supports more
Not reading the funded agreementAn inability to restate the payout condition without looking it upRead it on issue day and keep the dated copy
Trading toward a target that no longer existsAggressive positioning to "finish" as though a deadline appliedRecognise that continuing is the objective, not completing
Withdrawing to the firm's minimumA balance at the floor limit after the payoutKeep a buffer of two to three times the worst month
Letting profit accumulate unwithdrawnSeveral cycles of profit retained with no requestWithdraw every eligible cycle
Ignoring the consistency conditionOne day above a quarter of net profit at the payout requestTrack the share weekly and stretch results across more days
Adding a second account immediatelyTwo accounts running identical tradesStabilise the first account, then diversify rather than duplicate

Two of those mistakes are reverses of each other, which is a useful reminder that there is no single correct posture. Withdrawing too little leaves profit inside the floor's reach; withdrawing too much removes the buffer that keeps the account alive. The answer is a fixed policy applied consistently rather than a judgement made each cycle.

Payouts, records and tax

Funded-account income is real income, and it is generally treated as trading profit or self-employment income depending on where you live and how you are structured. The specifics vary enough by jurisdiction that no general guide should be treated as advice, but the record-keeping requirements do not vary much at all.

  • Keep every payout statement. Gross amount, fees deducted, net received, and the date. Reconstructing a year of payouts from memory is not possible.
  • Keep the fee receipts too. Evaluation fees, reset fees and data subscriptions may be deductible business expenses in some jurisdictions, and only receipts establish that.
  • Separate the accounts. Paying funded income into the same account as personal spending makes the records much harder to produce later.
  • Record the currency and the conversion. If the firm pays in a currency other than your own, the conversion rate at receipt is part of the record.
  • Note the firm's own reporting. Some firms issue statements or tax documents; keep them alongside your own records rather than instead of them.

The practical point is not to prepare a tax return on the day you pass. It is that the paperwork for a year of funded trading takes minutes a month and hours to reconstruct — and the decision about whether to continue trading an account is easier when you can see the net, after fees and splits, rather than the gross figure on the dashboard.

A note on expectations

The most useful adjustment after a pass is to average, not extrapolate. The evaluation was completed over a small number of sessions and a favourable sequence of conditions; the funded account will be traded over many more, including stretches where nothing works.

Two habits make that manageable. The first is measuring performance in payout cycles rather than in weeks, because a cycle is the unit the firm pays in and the unit your income actually arrives in. The second is comparing a month against your own worst month rather than against your best week, since the buffer exists precisely for the difference between the two.

A funded account traded at a conservative size with regular withdrawals will usually collect more over a year than one traded aggressively with a single large payout and a shorter life. That outcome is not a matter of temperament; it is arithmetic. The account's value is the number of cycles it survives multiplied by the average net payout, and only one of those two terms responds to patience.

Getting answers from the firm

The funded phase is the point at which written answers start to matter, because it is the point at which interpretations have money attached. Most disputes are not caused by a firm acting in bad faith; they are caused by a question that was never asked and a rule that was read one way by the trader and another way by the firm.

SituationWhat to sendWhat to keep
A rule you cannot interpretOne specific question, quoting the rule text and describing the positionThe reply, with its date, alongside a copy of the rule as it stood
A payout delayed or queriedThe payout reference, the cycle dates and your own compliance recordsEvery message in the thread, unedited
A rule appears to have changedA request for the current version, with the version you hold attachedBoth versions, dated
A trade is questionedThe order ticket, the timestamps and the reasoning if the plan documents itThe ticket and the reply together
A payout destination issueThe account name, the documentation required, and a request for the exact requirementThe requirement in writing before resubmitting

Three habits make the process work. Ask one question at a time, because a message containing four questions tends to receive an answer to the easiest one. Quote the rule, so the reply is about the rule rather than about your situation. And keep everything in the firm's own channel — a clarification obtained in a chat group or from a third party has no standing, and the version that matters at the payout review is the one the firm gave you directly.

Be reasonable about turnaround. Support teams are often handling many accounts, and a payout that takes a few days longer than expected is usually admin rather than a problem. The distinction worth acting on is a payout that has been queried rather than merely delayed: at that point the useful response is documentation, not volume.

Common questions

What should I do immediately after passing?

Confirm the pass with the firm, read and save the funded agreement, establish the payout cycle and its conditions, and recalculate the risk unit from the funded floor. Do all of that before placing the first funded trade — there is no target to reach and no deadline to beat.

Do the evaluation rules still apply?

Mostly, but not entirely, and the differences matter. The profit target is removed; a consistency condition is often introduced at payout; holding and news rules may be stricter; and a data or platform fee may resume. Read the funded agreement rather than assuming continuity.

How much should I withdraw first?

Slightly less than the maximum, leaving a buffer of two to three times your worst recent month above the firm's minimum balance. A smaller first payout preserves the account for the next cycle, and the cash difference matters far less than the buffer does in month one.

How often should I take payouts?

Every eligible cycle, retaining a fixed buffer, is the default that produces the highest total over a year in most cases. Longer rhythms produce larger individual payments and leave more profit inside the floor's reach.

When should I scale up?

After two or three clean payouts, with a buffer above twice your worst recent month, a profit distribution comfortably inside the consistency threshold, and no plan deviations in the last cycle. Test the larger size on a demo or small account before applying it to the funded account.

Should I increase my position size once funded?

Not immediately. The funded account is governed by the same loss limits, and a size increase shortens expected account life while raising payout per cycle. Increase size only when accumulated profit can absorb the larger loss — buffer first, size second.

Is a second funded account a good idea?

Only if the accounts are not running the same trades. Two accounts on the same strategy at the same size breach together on the same losing sequence, which doubles the exposure without diversifying it. Different methods, different risk units or different firms create real diversification.

What records should I keep?

The firm's pass confirmation, a dated copy of the funded agreement, the payout conditions you recorded, every payout statement with fees, support replies on any question that mattered, and your own daily loss records. The dated agreement and the support replies are the two most often needed and most often missing.
