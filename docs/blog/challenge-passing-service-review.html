Challenge Passing Service Review

How the service works, what it costs, how to check the firm’s third-party trading clause, and when trading the evaluation yourself is the better buy. The review is written as a buyer’s checklist rather than a testimonial, because the only claims worth trusting here are the ones that can be verified before payment.

Key takeaways

  • The firm’s terms, not the service’s marketing, decide whether the arrangement is allowed.
  • A flat fee with retries covered is simpler to audit than a profit split.
  • The funded account should be issued in your name, with no share of profit taken.
  • A written terms check per firm is the minimum standard before payment.
  • The service removes execution risk, not rule risk, counterparty risk or payout risk.

What the service actually does

A passing service trades an evaluation on your behalf for a flat fee and returns a funded account in your name. The arrangement is only legitimate where the firm’s terms permit third-party trading, and the fee is only fair where the retry policy is written down. The consequence is contractual rather than technical: the firm’s terms decide whether the arrangement is permitted, and the fee structure decides whether it is worth doing at all.

The process is deliberately narrow. You choose the firm and the account size; the service reads that firm’s terms for the exact plan; if third-party trading is permitted, it trades the evaluation to the target inside the rulebook; if the account breaches, it retries under the same flat fee; when the target is reached, the funded account is issued in your name. The service does not hold the funded account and takes no share of its profit.

  1. Confirm the firm, plan and account size, and check the terms on third-party trading.
  2. Pay the flat fee only after the terms are confirmed.
  3. The evaluation is traded to the target under the firm’s loss floor and daily stop.
  4. On a pass, identity verification and the funded contract are completed in your name.
  5. The funded login is handed back; payouts are yours, with no profit split.

The parts that are not included matter as much as the parts that are. The firm’s own evaluation fee and any reset charge are separate. The service cannot change the firm’s rules, speed up a minimum-day requirement, or guarantee a payout that the funded terms make conditional. It removes execution risk, not counterparty or rule risk.

That distinction is the whole review in one sentence. A trader who buys the service expecting a guaranteed funded account has misunderstood the product. A trader who buys it because their method works but their execution under evaluation conditions does not has understood it exactly.

What the fee buys and what it does not

A flat fee is easy to describe and easy to audit. It buys a defined piece of work: reading the plan’s terms, trading the evaluation within them, absorbing the retries that the rulebook causes, and handing back a funded login. It does not buy the evaluation fee itself, which is paid to the firm, and it does not buy the outcome of a payout review that the firm conducts under its own rules.

The “included” column is where a provider should be specific. “Retries covered” is a promise until it is written down as a number: unlimited within a period, one free retry, or retries at a reduced fee. A policy that is not written is not a policy, and a policy that is written but excludes a category of breach is a policy with a hole in it.

The “separate” column is where clients are surprised. The firm’s evaluation fee is the obvious one; a reset charge is the less obvious one. Some firms sell a discounted reset rather than a full new attempt, and the service may or may not absorb that charge depending on its policy. Ask which it is before the first attempt fails, not after.

The terms check, step by step

The clause that decides whether a service can operate is usually called “third-party trading”, “account sharing” or “account management”. Some firms prohibit it on the evaluation, some on the funded account, and some on both. A provider that does not ask which plan you bought cannot know which clause applies, because the same firm can publish different terms for different products.

The first step is to identify the exact plan and the terms version that applies. Firms publish separate terms pages per programme, and the version date matters. A check against a generic “terms” page is not a check; it is a reading of the marketing site.

The second step is to quote the clause. A credible provider can point to the paragraph that permits the arrangement, or state plainly that the firm prohibits it and decline the job. A provider that says “everyone does it” has not read the clause, and a provider that says “the firm won’t find out” has read it and decided to ignore it.

The third step is to check the funded stage separately. Even where an evaluation permits third-party trading, the funded contract can prohibit it, and a breach discovered after a pass can lead to a withheld payout. The check must cover both stages, in writing, with the plan name and the date.

The fourth step is to check the adjacent rules that the arrangement touches: identity verification, IP address and device rules, hedging and copy-trading restrictions, and any prohibition on sharing login credentials. A service that trades the account from a different country than the client’s verified identity may comply with the third-party clause and still fail the platform’s access rules.

Where the terms are silent, the safe reading is that the arrangement is not authorised. Silence is not permission, and a firm that later decides the arrangement breached its terms can withhold a payout even after a pass. The only protection is a written confirmation before payment, kept with the dated terms page.

That is why the order of operations matters: terms first, payment second. A provider that asks for the fee before the firm’s terms are checked has inverted the only step that protects the client.

Retries, refunds and the fine print

A retry policy is a pricing policy in disguise. “Retries covered” without a limit is a promise to work until the pass, which is expensive to honour and therefore rare. “One free retry” is a fixed cap that changes the expected cost of the arrangement: if the method needs three attempts, the client pays for the third.

A refund policy is the other half. Some providers refund the service fee if the evaluation cannot be passed, some refund only if the firm prohibits the arrangement, and some offer no refund at all. The refund condition should name the trigger, the amount and the timeline. A refund that depends on the provider’s discretion is not a refund; it is a hope.

The fine print worth reading includes the definition of a breach. If the service treats a firm-side rule change as the client’s risk, a change in the middle of an attempt can end the arrangement without a refund. If the service treats its own execution error as covered, the client is protected against the main risk they are paying to remove.

Ask what happens if the evaluation passes but the funded contract is declined. That is a real outcome when identity verification fails or a rule is found to have been breached after the target is reached. The service cannot override the firm, but the policy should say whether the fee is refunded, whether a retry is offered, and who bears the firm’s fee.

The economics on both sides

The client’s arithmetic is straightforward. Compare the flat fee plus the firm’s evaluation fee with the expected cost of self-trading the same plan. If the trader’s pass probability is 40%, the expected number of self-traded attempts is 2.5, so the self-traded cost is 2.5 times the firm’s fee, plus the running costs of the trades. The service replaces that expected cost with a fixed one, provided the retry policy is what it claims.

The provider’s arithmetic is less obvious and more revealing. A service that charges a flat fee and covers unlimited retries is taking on the variance of its own execution. To stay solvent it must either pass at a high rate, charge enough to cover the tail, or limit retries. A provider that does none of those is either very good or very temporary, and the client should find out which before paying.

That is why a fee that is far below the market is a warning rather than a bargain. Passing an evaluation takes screen time, rule reading and risk management, and a price that suggests none of that is being done is a price for something else: a signal group, a copy-trade scheme, or a fee collected before any work begins.

The fee should also be compared with the value of the funded account, not with the evaluation fee. A $220 service fee on a $100,000 account is 0.22% of the balance; on a $10,000 account it is 2.2%. The same fee is cheap or expensive depending on the size of the prize, which is why the smallest viable account is the right place to start.

Service versus trading it yourself

Trading the evaluation yourself is cheaper in cash if you pass first time. It is also the route that produces the skill, the journal and the evidence base for a second account. A service is not a substitute for those things; it is a way to buy the outcome when execution, not strategy, is the bottleneck.

Run the comparison with numbers rather than adjectives. Suppose the firm’s fee is $500, the trader’s pass probability is 45%, and the service fee is $220 with retries covered. Self-trading has an expected cost of $500 divided by 0.45, or $1,111, plus running costs. The service costs $220 plus the firm’s $500, or $720, with the retries inside the service fee. On those numbers the service is cheaper in expectation, even though it is more expensive on a first-attempt pass.

The comparison flips if the trader’s pass probability is high. At an 80% pass rate, self-trading costs $625 in expectation, below the service’s $720. That is the honest boundary of the product: it is worth buying when the trader’s own pass probability is low relative to the provider’s, and not worth buying when the trader is already likely to pass.

The second variable is time. A trader who needs a funded account by a specific date is buying the calendar as much as the outcome. A self-traded attempt that takes twelve weeks has a cost that does not appear in the fee comparison, and a service that finishes in two weeks is buying twelve weeks back. Whether that is worth the fee depends on what the trader would otherwise do with the time.

How to review a provider in one hour

Start with the terms check. Can the provider name the firms whose rules permit third-party trading, and can it quote the clause? A provider that cannot answer this is not ready to be trusted with an evaluation. Next, ask for the fee, the retry policy, the timeline and the ownership of the funded account. Those four answers separate a process from a promise.

Then ask for evidence. A track record is more useful than a testimonial: which firms, which account sizes, which dates, and what happened after the pass. A provider that shows only screenshots of profits is showing the top of the distribution, not a process. A provider that shows a failed attempt and explains the cause is more credible, because it demonstrates that it understands the rulebook.

Ask how the provider handles a firm-side rule change mid-attempt. The answer reveals whether the provider reads terms as a living document or as a one-time checkbox. A provider that has a process for re-reading the terms after a change is more likely to protect the client from a breach that neither party intended.

Finally, ask for the refund and dispute process in writing, including the timeline for a response. A provider that answers support questions in hours during the sales conversation and days after payment has told you something about its priorities. The written process is the only version that survives a disagreement.

Score the answers on four dimensions: terms compliance, fee transparency, process evidence and post-pass ownership. A provider that scores well on all four is worth considering. A provider that scores well on price and poorly on terms compliance is not, because the evaluation fee is small compared with a funded account that cannot be withdrawn from.

Red flags that end the conversation

The first is a guarantee of a pass. No provider controls the firm’s rules, the platform’s execution or the market, so a guarantee is either a marketing claim or a promise the provider cannot keep. A credible provider describes the rulebook, the risk plan and the retry policy instead.

The second is a request to trade the client’s personal account with the client’s own capital. That is not a passing service; it is discretionary management of real money, with a different risk profile and usually a different regulatory status. The product reviewed here trades an evaluation, not a live account.

The third is a profit split on the funded account. A service that takes a share of funded profit has a claim on the account after the pass, which changes the ownership and the incentive. The flat-fee model exists to avoid exactly that claim.

The fourth is a request to misrepresent who is trading. A legitimate arrangement is disclosed, not disguised. A provider that asks the client to hide the arrangement from the firm has decided that the terms are an obstacle rather than a contract, and the client carries the consequence.

The fifth is a refusal to put the retry or refund policy in writing. Every other answer can be verified later; that one cannot. If it is not written before payment, it does not exist.

Identity, tax and who owns the account

The funded account should be issued in the client’s name, with the client’s identity verification, and the payout should be paid to the client’s own account. That is the definition of the product. An arrangement where the provider holds the funded login, controls the payout method or takes a share of withdrawals is a different product with different risks.

Identity verification is the step where that definition is tested. Firms usually require a government identity document, proof of address and sometimes a video or liveness check. A provider cannot complete that step on the client’s behalf, and a provider that offers to do so is offering to commit identity fraud. The client must complete verification personally.

Tax treatment follows the client’s residence, not the provider’s. Payouts from a funded account are income in most jurisdictions, and the client is responsible for declaring them. A provider that offers tax advice should be treated with the same caution as a provider that offers guarantees.

Login control is the operational half of ownership. During the evaluation, the provider needs access to trade the account; after the pass, the client should change the password and take sole control. A provider that wants to keep access to the funded account has not handed over the product.

How these arrangements usually fail

The most common failure is a terms mismatch discovered late. The provider traded a plan whose funded stage prohibits third-party trading, the account passed, and the payout was withheld. The client paid the firm’s fee and the service fee and received nothing. The only defence is the written terms check, made against the funded stage as well as the evaluation.

The second is a retry policy that does not cover the breach that occurred. The provider covers breaches caused by its own trading but excludes breaches caused by a firm rule change, or covers one retry and not the second. The client discovers the boundary at the worst moment.

The third is a payout condition the service cannot influence: a consistency clause, a minimum-day requirement or a verification delay. The service delivered a funded account, which is what it sold, but the account does not pay when the client expected. That is not fraud; it is a mismatch between what the client thought they were buying and what the terms allow.

The fourth is outright non-delivery: a provider that collects the fee and never trades, or trades an account that is not the client’s. This is rarer than the first three but it is the reason to start with a small account and a written agreement rather than a large fee and a promise.

The client's side of the arrangement

The provider does the trading, but the client still makes four decisions that determine whether the arrangement works. The first is the firm. A firm with clean, published terms and a track record of paying is a better counterparty than a firm with a deeper discount and a vague payout clause, because the service cannot fix a firm that does not pay.

The second is the account size. The service fee is usually flat, so the fee as a share of the funded balance falls as the account grows. That argues for buying the largest account the trader can justify, except that the firm's evaluation fee rises with size and the cost of a failed attempt rises with it. The smallest viable account is the right first purchase; size after the process is proven.

The third is the risk preference. A provider trading to a target inside a loss floor is making decisions on the client's behalf, and the client should say in advance whether a slow, low-risk path or a faster, higher-variance path is acceptable. A provider that cannot accommodate the preference is the wrong provider for that client, even if its fee is competitive.

The fourth is the disclosure decision. Where the firm's terms require the account holder to disclose third-party trading, the client should disclose. Where the terms prohibit it, the client should not proceed. The service can read the clause, but only the account holder can make the representation to the firm, and a misrepresentation is the client's risk.

There is also a practical preparation step: confirm the firm is still accepting the plan and the region. Firms change plan availability and restrict countries without notice, and a service that checked the terms last month may be working from a stale version. The check should be repeated on the day of purchase.

The records to keep

Keep six documents: the dated terms page for the exact plan, the provider's written terms check, the service agreement with the retry policy, the receipt for the firm's evaluation fee, the receipt for the service fee, and the payout statements from the funded account. Together they describe the arrangement well enough to resolve most disputes without a lawyer.

The dated terms page is the most important. Firms revise terms, and the version that applied on the purchase date is the only one that can settle a question about what was permitted or what a payout condition meant. A screenshot with a visible date and URL is sufficient; a bookmark is not.

The written terms check is the second. It should name the firm, the plan, the clause that permits the arrangement and the date of the check. If the provider will not put that in writing, there is nothing to keep and nothing to rely on.

The payout statements are the third. They show the split, the deductions and the processing time, which are the numbers that determine the real return on the arrangement. They also support the tax treatment of the income, which is the client's responsibility regardless of who traded the account.

Store the records outside the provider's dashboard. A provider that closes an account or changes a portal can take its own copy with it; the client's copy is the one that survives.

The verdict framework

Score the service on terms compliance, fee transparency, process evidence and post-pass ownership. A service that scores well on all four is worth considering. A service that scores well on price but poorly on terms compliance is not. The evaluation fee is small compared with a funded account that cannot be withdrawn from.

If the service passes the review, start with the smallest viable account. A small first engagement tests the process without risking a large fee. Scale after the first payout, not after the first promise.

The final question is not whether the service is good in general but whether it is good for this trader, this firm and this plan. A service that is excellent on a firm with clean terms is worthless on a firm that prohibits the arrangement, and a service that is cheap on a small account is expensive on a large one. Judge it on the specific purchase.

Common questions

What should I check before hiring a passing service?

The firm terms for the exact plan, the fee, the retry policy, the timeline and who owns the funded account. A service that cannot answer all five is not ready to be hired.

Are guarantees a red flag?

Yes. A guaranteed pass is not a process; it is a marketing claim. A credible service describes the rulebook, the risk plan and the retry policy instead.

Should I start with a large account?

No. Start with the smallest viable account to test the process, then scale after the first successful payout.

Is a passing service allowed by prop firms?

It depends on the firm and the plan. Some permit third-party trading on the evaluation but not on the funded account, some prohibit it entirely, and some are silent. Silence is not permission, so the clause has to be checked for both stages before payment.

Do I still pay the firm's evaluation fee?

Yes. The service fee is separate from the firm's evaluation fee and any reset charge. The total cost of the arrangement is the sum of the two, plus running costs.

What happens if the evaluation fails?

It depends on the retry policy. Some providers cover unlimited retries within a period, some cover one, and some charge again. The policy should be in writing before payment.
