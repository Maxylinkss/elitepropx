Apex Trader Funding First Payout Timeline

The first payout on a futures evaluation is gated by more than a profit target. There is the trailing threshold, the funded-account confirmation, a minimum number of trading days, a payout threshold and a review window, and each one adds days to the timeline. This guide maps the sequence in order, shows where the time actually goes, and marks the figures that must be confirmed against the current plan because futures firms revise their schedules frequently.

Key takeaways

  • Four gates sit between passing and the first payment: funded confirmation, minimum trading days, payout threshold and review.
  • The trailing threshold is the rule that decides whether a funded account survives long enough to pay at all.
  • Futures payouts are usually requested against a threshold, not on demand for any amount.
  • The first payout is the slowest because account details, tax information and payout method are verified once.
  • Every figure here describes the published structure at the time of writing; confirm it on the plan page before relying on a date.

Most first-payout questions are really one question: when does the money arrive. The answer is a chain of conditions rather than a single date, and each condition has its own clock. Some are measured in trading days, some in calendar days, and one is measured in profit after the account is funded.

Futures adds a variable that forex evaluations do not have: the trailing threshold. On a futures account the drawdown floor typically follows peak equity up to a limit, which means that a funded account can be profitable and still fail to reach a payout because the buffer between equity and the floor has narrowed. Understanding that mechanism is the difference between a date that holds and a date that slips.

The four gates in order

GateMeasured inWhat it requiresTypical cost in time
Funded confirmationBusiness daysEvaluation verified and the funded account issued1–3 days
Minimum trading daysTrading daysA stated number of days with qualifying activity5–10 days from funding
Payout thresholdDollarsProfit above a stated level, above the trailing floorDepends on size and pace
Review and paymentBusiness daysDetails verified, request approved, transfer settled2–7 days

The order matters because the gates are not parallel. The trading-day count usually starts at funding, not at purchase, so a fast pass does not shorten it. The payout threshold is measured on the funded account, not on the evaluation, so the profit made during the evaluation does not count toward it. A trader who plans the timeline from the evaluation start will be wrong by the length of the funded-account phase.

That is the single most common planning error on futures evaluations. The evaluation is the qualifying test; the payout threshold is a separate test on a separate account with a floor that moves. Treating them as one continuous run produces an expected date that is weeks early.

The trailing threshold, in dollars

A trailing threshold follows peak equity upward and stops at a ceiling. The mechanism has three numbers: the starting threshold, the amount it trails, and the level at which it stops trailing.

PointPeak equityThresholdBuffer to the floor
Start$50,000$47,500$2,500
After +$2,000$52,000$49,500$2,500
After +$4,000$54,000$51,500$2,500
At the stop level$55,000$52,500 (locked)$2,500, then widening
After +$8,000$58,000$52,500 (locked)$5,500

The fourth row is the important one. Until the threshold stops trailing, the buffer between equity and the floor stays constant at the trailing amount, which means profit does not create room. A trader who is $4,000 up on a $50,000 account still has exactly the same distance to failure as at the start — the floor has followed them up.

After the threshold locks, profit finally becomes room. That is the point at which the account becomes structurally safer, and it is usually the point at which a payout becomes realistic, because the payout threshold sits above the locked floor. A trader who withdraws before the lock gives up the buffer they just built.

Withdrawal timingEffect on the bufferEffect on the next payout
Before the threshold locksBuffer returns to the trailing amountNext payout starts from a smaller cushion
At the threshold lockBuffer is preservedLater payouts are easier to reach
Well above the lockBuffer grows with retained profitCompounding effect on later cycles

The first row explains a pattern traders report on futures accounts: the first payout is received, and the account then breaches within a month. The withdrawal reset the cushion without resetting the floor, so the next drawdown had less room than the one before it.

From pass to funded confirmation

The evaluation ends when the profit target is reached and the account is verified. On futures plans the verification is usually fast because the evaluation is simulated and the record is already in the firm's system.

Two things can slow it. The first is an open position at the moment of verification: some plans require the account to be flat, and a position held overnight can delay confirmation to the next session. The second is an account detail mismatch — a name, address or payment method that does not match the verification record. Both are avoidable by being flat and complete before the target is reached.

The funded account that arrives is not a continuation of the evaluation. It is a new account with a new floor, a new trailing mechanism and its own rules. The trader's first decision on it is the size, and the correct size is smaller than the size used to pass, because the payout threshold has to be reached without touching a floor that follows equity.

The minimum trading day count

The count applies to the funded account on most futures plans, and its definition is usually a day on which at least one trade is executed and closed. Because futures sessions are short and structured, a "day" is unambiguous — but the count still has to be planned.

For a trader taking two or three trades a session, the count is satisfied incidentally. For a trader who trades one setup a week, it is the binding constraint and the first payout date is set by the calendar rather than by the profit. That is worth knowing before choosing a plan: a method that produces two setups a month will take months to satisfy a ten-day count however profitable it is.

Filler days are cheaper on futures than on forex because micro contracts exist. A single micro contract held briefly satisfies the definition at a cost of a few dollars, which makes the count a rounding error rather than a risk. The caution is the same as elsewhere: the filler must be a real trade with a server-side stop, not a token entry designed to game the definition.

The payout threshold

The payout threshold is the profit level at which a withdrawal can be requested. It is measured on the funded account and usually expressed as a dollar amount or a percentage of the account size.

Account sizeThreshold at 6%Buffer at the lockNet first payout after fees
$25,000$1,500$1,250~$1,400
$50,000$3,000$2,500~$2,850
$100,000$6,000$5,000~$5,750
$150,000$9,000$7,500~$8,650

The fee column is not small in relative terms. Futures payouts often carry a per-request fee, and on a $25,000 account a fixed fee consumes a larger share of a $1,500 threshold than of a $9,000 one. That is part of why small futures accounts are less efficient: the threshold scales with size, the fee does not.

The threshold also interacts with the split. A payout at the threshold is a gross figure; the trader receives their share of it, and any fee is deducted. Planning the date from the gross threshold and the cash flow from the net figure produces two different numbers, and the second is the one that matters.

The review window

A payout request triggers a review of the account: the trading record, the rule compliance, the identity and the payment method. On a first request this is the longest administrative step because nothing is on file yet.

  • Trading record. Whether trades were within session hours, within the position cap and consistent with the stated style.
  • Rule compliance. Whether the trailing threshold was ever breached, and whether any prohibited practice appears in the record.
  • Identity and tax details. The forms required before a first payment, which differ by jurisdiction.
  • Payment method. The destination account must match the verified name.

A complete submission clears in days. An incomplete one is held, and the hold does not announce itself — the request simply does not progress. The practical step is to complete identity and payment details at funding rather than at the request, which removes the most common reason for a first-payout delay.

A modelled 30-day timeline

An illustrative sequence for a $50,000 account, with a fast evaluation and a moderate funded pace. The durations are a model, not a published schedule.

The evaluation took a week and the payout took three more. The gap is entirely funded-account time: seven trading days of count, six days of profit to the threshold, and four days of administration. A trader who planned from the evaluation start would have expected cash around day 12 and would have been wrong by eighteen days.

The model assumes the threshold is reached without touching the trailing floor. If the account has a drawdown after the count is satisfied, the clock resets on the profit requirement while the count stays satisfied — which is the most common shape of a slipped first payout.

The third row is the one that surprises traders who passed comfortably. Because the trailing floor follows peak equity until it locks, a drawdown after a good run can leave the account profitable but below the payout threshold relative to the floor. The count is unaffected, so the trader waits only for the profit to recover — but the wait is real and it is not visible in the evaluation plan.

Building the date backwards

  1. Start from the date you need the cash, and subtract the settlement window in business days.
  2. Subtract the review window; add two days for a first request.
  3. Subtract the time needed to reach the payout threshold at your funded-account pace.
  4. Subtract the minimum trading day count, measured from funding.
  5. Subtract the funded confirmation window, then work back to the evaluation finish date.

Written as a chain, the date is usually further away than expected, and every step is a published condition rather than a delay. That is the useful output: a date the trader can plan around, and a clear view of which gate is the binding one. For most futures traders it is the threshold, not the count — and the threshold is the one variable that size and pace can actually influence.

Confirm every figure on the current plan page before relying on a date. Futures firms revise thresholds, counts and fees more often than forex firms revise their core numbers, and a timeline built on last quarter's terms is a forecast of the wrong product.

It is also worth re-running the chain whenever a plan changes. A moved threshold or a new minimum-day requirement shifts the whole schedule, and because the gates are sequential, a three-day change at the front can move the payout date by more than three days at the back. The chain is cheap to rebuild and expensive to get wrong, and a saved copy of the old chain makes the difference visible when a firm announces a revision, which is the moment a trader most needs to know whether the date moved, and by how much it moved.

The one number the trader controls is the pace to the threshold, and it is controlled through size rather than through speed. A smaller risk on the funded account lengthens the approach to the threshold but keeps the account alive to make it. Given that the threshold is the binding gate on most futures accounts, that trade is usually worth making.

Sizing the funded account

The size used to pass an evaluation is not the size to use on the funded account, and the reason is structural rather than psychological. The evaluation floor is fixed at the start; the funded floor follows peak equity.

Account stageFloor behaviourAppropriate risk
EvaluationFixed distance from the startLargest risk that survives a normal streak
Funded, before the lockFollows peak equitySmaller, because profit does not create room
Funded, after the lockFixed after the ceilingCan rise as buffer accumulates

The middle row is the one that catches traders. Before the threshold locks, a winning streak does not improve the buffer; the floor rises with equity, so the distance to failure stays at the trailing amount. Risking the same percentage as in the evaluation means the funded account has the same room despite the extra profit, and a normal retracement can end it.

A practical rule is to halve the risk at funding and restore it only after the threshold locks. That halves the pace toward the payout threshold, which lengthens the first cycle, but it converts the account from a fragile one that needs a perfect sequence into one that can absorb a bad week.

The alternative — keep the evaluation size and push for the threshold — produces the pattern most often reported on futures accounts: a fast first payout followed by a breach within weeks. The withdrawal reduced the buffer and the size never adjusted.

What the threshold lock changes

The lock is the point at which the trailing floor stops following equity. Before it, profit is a claim that can be taken back; after it, profit becomes room.

StateEffect of +$1,000 profitEffect of a $1,000 drawdown
Before the lockFloor rises; buffer unchangedBuffer unchanged until the floor stops
At the lockFloor fixed; buffer growsBuffer shrinks by the loss
Well above the lockBuffer grows one-for-oneBuffer shrinks, with room to spare

The first row explains why an account can be profitable and still fragile. A trader who is $3,000 up before the lock has not gained $3,000 of safety; they have gained $3,000 of paper profit and the same buffer as at the start.

The second row is why the lock is the real milestone of a funded account, more than the payout threshold. Once the floor is fixed, every further dollar of retained profit improves the risk profile, and the account becomes progressively harder to lose. Before the lock, every dollar of profit is reversible.

That suggests a sequencing decision: where the lock sits above the payout threshold, leaving profit in the account until the lock is reached makes later cycles easier. Where the lock sits below the threshold, the first withdrawal is safe and the second is the one to plan for.

Compared with a forex payout

The timeline shape differs from a forex evaluation in three places, and each shortens or lengthens the wait.

StageFutures timelineForex timeline
TargetDollar amount per account sizePercentage of the account
Floor after fundingTrailing until the lockStatic from the start
Payout gateThreshold plus a day countCycle date plus profit share
First payout waitCount, threshold, reviewSeasoning, cycle, review

The second row is the substantive difference. A forex funded account with a static floor can be traded at a constant risk because the distance to failure never changes. A futures account cannot, because the floor moves until it locks. That makes the futures risk plan a two-stage plan and the forex plan a single-stage one.

The fourth row is closer than it looks. Both waits contain a fixed eligibility period and an administrative tail, and in both cases the eligible period dominates. The practical difference is that a futures threshold depends on the account's own performance after funding, while a forex cycle depends on the calendar. One is earned, the other is waited for.

The payout review's evidence

Futures payouts are reviewed against a trade record, and the record is generated by the platform. A first request goes more smoothly when the trader can produce the same data the firm is looking at.

  • The full trade history export. Entry, exit, size, contract and session, from the first funded trade.
  • The daily equity curve. Shows the floor was never breached and where peak equity sat.
  • The count evidence. Which sessions qualified as trading days and why.
  • Identity and tax details. Completed at funding rather than at the request.
  • The payout method record. Matching the verified name and account.

The second item is the one specific to trailing-floor accounts. Because the floor follows peak equity, the relevant question is not the closing balance but the path: whether equity ever fell below the trailed level on a day when it was not obvious. A trader who exports the equity curve has the answer; one who exports only closed trades does not.

The third item is a common source of avoidable delay. A count that looks satisfied on a dashboard may not be, because the dashboard can count platform activity while the plan requires an executed and closed trade. Producing the session list and the count side by side resolves the question before it becomes a hold.

Export the history monthly rather than at the request. Platform migrations and retention limits occasionally make older records harder to retrieve, and a local export is the only copy the trader controls.

There is a second reason to keep the equity curve. On a trailing threshold, the difference between a good month and a breach can be a single session that recovered by the close. A curve shows the intraday low and the recovery; a closed-trade statement shows neither. If a review asks whether the floor was touched, the curve is the document that answers it.

The count evidence deserves the same treatment. A one-line ledger per session — date, trades taken, whether the session qualified — takes seconds to maintain and removes the ambiguity entirely. Traders who maintain it rarely have count disputes, because the record and the platform agree by construction.

A last point on evidence: the review is usually about consistency between three documents — the platform record, the firm's account data and the trader's account of what happened. A trader who can reconcile the three in a page of notes makes the review short. A trader who cannot forces the reviewer to reconstruct the sequence, which takes time and almost never resolves in the trader's favour when a rule is ambiguous.

Common questions

How long after passing does the first Apex payout arrive?

It depends on the funded-account gates: confirmation, a minimum number of trading days, the payout threshold and the review. A realistic model is three to five weeks from the pass, with the funded-account phase rather than administration accounting for most of the time.

Does evaluation profit count toward the payout threshold?

No. The threshold is measured on the funded account, which is a separate account with its own floor. Profit made during the evaluation qualifies you for funding; it does not count toward the first withdrawal.

What is the trailing threshold?

A loss floor that follows peak equity upward until it reaches a set level, after which it stops moving. Until it locks, profit does not create room to the floor; the buffer stays at the trailing amount. After the lock, additional profit becomes genuine room.

Why did my account breach after a payout?

Because a withdrawal can reduce the buffer between equity and the floor without moving the floor. If the threshold had not yet locked, the next drawdown had less room than the previous one. Withdrawing at or after the lock preserves the cushion.

Can I request a payout before the minimum trading days?

No. The count is a condition of eligibility, and a request submitted early is rejected rather than queued. Check the count on the dashboard before submitting.

Does an open position block a payout request?

It can. Many plans require a flat account at the request, and some require no trades on the request day. Close the position and let the record settle before submitting.

Why is the first payout slower than later ones?

Identity, tax details and the payment method are verified once and reused. Later requests skip most of that, so the review window shrinks. Completing the details at funding removes most of the first-request delay.

Is the payout threshold a percentage or a fixed amount?

Either, depending on the plan. A percentage scales with account size; a fixed amount makes small accounts proportionally more expensive because the fee does not scale with it. Check which applies before choosing an account size.
