Best Prop Firms for Gold Trading in 2026

Gold is the instrument that most often turns a reasonable rulebook into an impossible one. XAUUSD moves in wide dollar ranges, its stops are larger than a forex major's, and a normal day can consume a daily loss limit that looked generous on paper. The right firm for a gold trader is not the one with the lowest fee; it is the one whose drawdown, leverage and news rules fit the instrument's volatility. This guide explains the mechanics and gives you a scorecard to apply to any firm.

Key takeaways

  • Gold's average range is wider than a forex major's, so the same percentage risk produces a larger stop and fewer positions.
  • A static drawdown and a generous daily limit are more useful for gold than a high leverage figure.
  • News and weekend rules matter more on gold because a large share of its moves happen around releases and session opens.
  • Check that XAUUSD is permitted on the specific plan and that the leverage is adequate for the stop distance.

Why gold is different

Gold is quoted in dollars per ounce, and its daily range is large relative to its price. A $30–$50 daily range on a $2,000–$2,400 price is 1.5–2%, which does not sound extreme until it is expressed in dollars per lot. A standard gold lot is 100 ounces, so a $1 move is $100. A $30 range is $3,000 per standard lot. A trader risking $500 per trade needs a stop of $5, which is a small fraction of a normal day's range and is likely to be taken out by noise.

The same arithmetic applies to a micro gold contract, which is 10 ounces. A $1 move is $10 per micro, and a $30 range is $300. The micro makes the risk manageable, but the stop distance in dollars is still wider than on a forex major. The trader has to accept a wider stop or a smaller position, and both reduce the pace toward the target.

The second difference is the behaviour around events. Gold reacts strongly to real yields, the dollar, inflation data and geopolitical risk. A single release can produce a $40–$60 move in minutes, which is a large percentage of a daily loss limit. A firm that restricts news trading can make a gold strategy unworkable; a firm that permits it but measures the daily loss on equity can still end the day in a single candle.

The third difference is the spread and the session. Gold spreads widen outside the London and New York sessions, and the overnight session can have thin liquidity and sharp moves. A gold scalper needs a firm with tight execution and a session that matches the trader's availability. A firm with a wide spread and a thin book can turn a small gold trade into a losing one before the direction is even tested.

Sizing a gold position

The sizing formula is the same as for any instrument: divide the dollar risk by the dollar value of the stop distance. The difference is the size of the numbers.

The formula

For a standard gold lot, the dollar value per $1 move is $100. For a micro gold contract, it is $10. If the per-trade risk is $500 and the stop is $8 away, the standard lot size is $500 divided by ($100 × $8), or 0.625 lots. The micro size is $500 divided by ($10 × $8), or 6.25 micros, which rounds down to 6. The micro gives more granularity and is usually the better choice for a small account.

A $100,000 example

A $100,000 account with a 5% daily limit has a $5,000 daily budget. One third of that is about $1,667 per trade. If the stop is $10 away, the standard lot size is $1,667 divided by $1,000, or 1.67 lots. Rounded down, 1.6 lots risks $1,600. The micro size is $1,667 divided by $100, or 16.6 micros, which rounds to 16. The trader can choose either, but the micro allows a more precise fit.

A $25,000 example

A $25,000 account with a 5% daily limit has a $1,250 daily budget. One third is about $417. With a $10 stop, the standard lot size is $417 divided by $1,000, or 0.42 lots. Many brokers allow fractional lots, but the risk is still $420. The micro size is $417 divided by $100, or 4.17 micros, which rounds to 4. The micro is the more practical choice for this account size.

The rules that break gold strategies

Four rules are more likely to break a gold strategy than a forex one. Each should be checked before buying.

The daily loss measurement

On an equity-measured daily limit, an open gold position can consume the daily budget in minutes during a release. A 1.6-lot position with a $10 adverse move is $1,600 of floating loss, which is a third of a $5,000 daily budget. Two such moves end the day. A firm that measures closed balance is more forgiving, but it is rarer. The trader should assume equity measurement and size accordingly.

The news policy

Gold moves on US data, central bank decisions and geopolitical events. A firm that prohibits opening trades around releases, or holding through them, can make a gold strategy unworkable if the strategy relies on those moves. A firm that permits news trading but measures on equity requires smaller size around the event. Either way, the policy has to be read before the first trade, not discovered at a payout review.

The weekend and overnight policy

Gold can gap at the weekly open, and a position held over the weekend can breach the maximum loss before the trader has a chance to react. A firm that prohibits weekend holding forces the trader to close on Friday; a firm that permits it requires the trader to size for the gap. The policy is usually in the conduct section, not the challenge page.

The instrument list and leverage

Not every plan supports XAUUSD, and not every plan offers the leverage needed for a gold stop. A plan with low leverage may require a margin that is a large share of the account, which limits the position size. The trader should check both the instrument list and the leverage before buying. A plan that supports gold but caps the position size so tightly that the target is unreachable is not a gold plan in practice.

Leverage and margin

Gold leverage is usually expressed as a ratio, such as 1:100 or 1:50. The ratio determines the margin required to open a position, not the risk per trade. A 1:100 leverage on a standard lot at $2,300 an ounce requires about $2,300 of margin per lot, because the notional value is $230,000 and the margin is 1% of that. A 1:50 leverage requires about $4,600 per lot. The higher the leverage, the smaller the margin and the larger the position a given account can control.

Leverage is not risk

A common mistake is to equate high leverage with high risk. The risk is determined by the position size and the stop distance, not by the leverage. A trader with 1:100 leverage can still risk 1% per trade by choosing a small position. The leverage determines how large a position the account can hold, not how much the trader should hold. The risk plan should be based on the stop and the budget, not on the maximum position the margin allows.

Margin calls

If the account equity falls below the maintenance margin, the broker may liquidate the position. On a prop firm evaluation, the firm's drawdown usually ends the account before the broker's margin call, but the two can interact. A trader with a tight margin and a wide gold stop can be liquidated before the stop is hit, which converts a planned loss into an unplanned one. The margin should be large enough to hold the position through the stop distance, not just to open it.

The prop firm leverage

Prop firms sometimes offer lower leverage than retail brokers, especially on gold. A firm with 1:30 leverage requires a larger margin and limits the position size. A gold trader should check the leverage for XAUUSD specifically, because the firm may offer high leverage on forex majors and lower leverage on metals. A plan that offers 1:100 on EURUSD and 1:20 on gold may not support the gold strategy the trader planned.

Sessions and event risk

Gold trades nearly around the clock but has distinct sessions with different liquidity and volatility. The session choice affects the stop distance, the spread and the event risk.

The London and New York overlap

The overlap is the most liquid window for gold, with the tightest spreads and the largest moves. It is also when US data is released, which can produce sharp moves. A trader who trades this window needs a news plan and a size that can absorb a release. The reward is better execution and more opportunities.

The Asian session

The Asian session is quieter, with smaller ranges and wider spreads. A gold trader who trades this window may find the stop distances smaller in dollars but the spread is a larger share of the move. Scalping in this session is difficult; swing entries can work if the trader accepts the wider spread and the lower liquidity.

Event risk

US inflation, employment, Federal Reserve decisions and geopolitical events can move gold by tens of dollars in minutes. A trader who holds a position through an event is taking a risk that is not captured by the normal stop distance. The options are to be flat, to reduce size to a fraction, or to widen the stop and accept a smaller position. The worst option is to hold full size and hope the stop is not gapped through.

Gold versus index futures

A gold trader comparing prop firms often also considers index futures. The choice is not just about the market; it is about the rule fit. Index futures have a smaller average range in dollar terms relative to the daily limit, which means a normal stop consumes a smaller share of the budget. Gold has a larger range and a larger event risk, but it also has a clearer relationship with macro events, which some traders find easier to analyse. The rule fit depends on the instrument's range relative to the firm's daily limit: the larger the range, the more generous the limit needs to be.

A trader who moves from gold to index futures should recalculate the position size from scratch. The contract values, the tick values and the stop distances are all different, and a size that was conservative on gold may be aggressive on an index. The reverse is also true: an index size may be too small to reach a gold target in a reasonable number of trades. The instrument and the rulebook are a pair, and changing one requires recalculating the other.

Gold-specific red flags

  1. XAUUSD not on the instrument list. A firm that supports forex majors may not support gold on every plan. Check the specific plan.
  2. Leverage too low for a gold stop. If the margin for a normal position is a large share of the account, the strategy is not viable.
  3. A broad news ban. Gold's largest moves are event-driven. A ban can eliminate the strategy's edge.
  4. Equity-measured daily loss with a tight limit. A single gold candle can consume a tight daily budget. The limit should be generous relative to the instrument's range.
  5. Wide spreads outside the main sessions. A spread that is a large share of the move turns a winning strategy into a losing one.
  6. Unclear weekend policy. Gold gaps at the weekly open. The policy should be explicit, not implied.
  7. No micro gold contract. A firm that only offers standard lots may force a position that is too large for a small account.
  8. A trailing floor on a news strategy. A news spike can raise the floor and then retrace, leaving the trader with less room than before the event.

A worked $100,000 gold plan

The plan below is illustrative. It shows how the rulebook and the instrument interact on a realistic account.

The setup

A $100,000 account, a 5% daily limit ($5,000), a 10% maximum loss ($10,000) and a static floor. The trader's gold strategy uses a $12 stop, which is a fraction of the normal daily range. The per-trade risk is one third of the daily budget, about $1,667. At $100 per dollar move per standard lot, the position is $1,667 divided by $1,200, or about 1.4 lots. The trader rounds down to 1.3 lots, risking $1,560.

A normal day

The trader takes one trade that wins $20 and one that loses $12. The net is +$8 per lot, or about $1,040 on 1.3 lots. The daily budget used is the $1,560 risk on the losing trade, which is less than a third of the budget. The floor has not moved because the floor is static. The trader is about 1% closer to the target.

An event day

A US inflation release is scheduled. The trader reduces size to half and widens the stop to $20 to account for the volatility. The position is now about 0.6 lots, risking $1,200, which is still inside the daily budget. The release produces a $30 move in the trader's direction, and the trade closes at +$30, or about $1,800. The trader has captured the event without risking the daily limit.

The lesson

The plan works because the size is calculated from the daily budget, not from the maximum position the leverage allows. The event day is handled by reducing size and widening the stop, not by avoiding the event or doubling the position. The static floor means the profit does not reduce the room, so the trader can keep the same risk after a good day. A trailing floor would require a reduction, which is why the drawdown type is the first criterion on the scorecard.

Cost per gold trade

Gold's cost structure is different from forex. The spread is usually wider, the commission may be charged per lot, and the swap or financing on a multi-day position can be significant. On a prop evaluation, the commission and spread come out of the account's profit, and they are a larger share of a small gold win than of a forex major.

The spread

A gold spread of $0.20–$0.40 per ounce is common in liquid sessions. On a standard lot of 100 ounces, that is $20–$40 per round turn, before commission. On a micro of 10 ounces, it is $2–$4. The spread is a fixed cost per trade, and it is larger as a percentage of the move when the strategy targets small moves. A scalper on gold needs a tight spread and a broker that does not widen it during the session.

The commission

Some brokers charge a commission per standard lot, either per side or per round turn. The commission is separate from the spread and can be a meaningful part of the cost. A trader should calculate the all-in cost per round turn: the spread plus the commission. On a $30 move, a $30 all-in cost is 10% of the gross profit, which is a large drag for a strategy with a small edge.

The swap

Holding a gold position overnight incurs a swap, which can be positive or negative depending on the direction and the interest rates. On a prop evaluation, the swap is usually small relative to the daily budget, but a multi-day swing position can accumulate a meaningful cost. The trader should check the swap rates before holding a position over multiple sessions, especially if the strategy relies on a small edge.

The cost against the target

The total cost per trade should be compared with the average win. If the average win is $200 and the all-in cost is $40, the cost is 20% of the win, which is too large for most strategies. If the average win is $1,000 and the cost is $40, the cost is 4%, which is manageable. The cost per trade is a function of the position size and the strategy's frequency; a high-frequency gold strategy needs a much tighter cost structure than a swing strategy.

How to choose

Four questions narrow the choice for a gold trader.

  1. Does the specific plan support XAUUSD, and at what leverage? Check the instrument list and the leverage for metals, not just forex.
  2. What is the drawdown type and the daily limit? A static floor and a generous daily limit are the most useful for gold's range.
  3. What is the news and weekend policy? Gold's moves are event-driven, so the policy decides whether the strategy is viable.
  4. What is the execution quality? A tight spread and a deep book matter more on gold than on a forex major, because the dollar moves are larger.

Use the scorecard to compare the answers. A firm that scores well on the first four questions and has a clear weekend policy is a better gold firm than one with a lower fee and a tighter drawdown. The fee is a one-time cost; the drawdown and the news policy affect every trade.
