FTMO vs FundedNext · reviewed October 2026

FTMO vs FundedNext: the 2026 rulebook comparison

FTMO and FundedNext are compared most often on their headline targets, which is the least informative row in either schedule. The real difference is architectural: FTMO sells one conventional two-phase rulebook with a static floor, while FundedNext sells a menu of one-step and two-phase plans with different drawdown styles. This comparison puts the rulebooks side by side, models the same account under each, and explains which trader each firm actually fits.

Key takeaways

  • Compare the plan, not the brand. FundedNext's one-step and two-phase models do not share one rulebook, and some variants use a trailing floor.
  • FTMO's static maximum loss is the more forgiving structure for a trader who wants a fixed failure point; FundedNext's menu offers a choice between speed and a softer drawdown.
  • The daily loss, consistency and payout conditions decide more outcomes than the headline target. Read them before comparing fees.
  • Both firms publish their terms; the comparison should be built from those documents, not from a review or a video.

The comparison trap: brand versus plan

The most common mistake in an FTMO versus FundedNext comparison is to treat each firm as a single product. FTMO's evaluation has a stable architecture: a two-phase process followed by a verification stage, a static maximum loss measured from the starting balance, and a daily loss measured on equity. FundedNext's architecture is a menu. It sells a one-step model and a two-phase model, and the drawdown style can differ between them. A trader who compares "FTMO vs FundedNext" without naming the FundedNext plan may be comparing a static two-phase challenge with a trailing one-step challenge, which are not the same product.

The second trap is to compare the target percentages. A 10% target with a 6% static floor is a 1.67:1 challenge. A 10% target with a 10% static floor is 1:1. A 10% target with a trailing floor is harder than the headline suggests, because the floor moves. The target alone says nothing about difficulty. The ratio of target to usable drawdown, and the type of drawdown, are the numbers that matter.

The third trap is to treat the fee as the deciding difference. Fees matter, but the expected cost of a failed attempt, the reset policy and the payout conditions usually matter more. A firm with a slightly higher fee and a cleaner rulebook can be the cheaper route over a year. The comparison below is therefore organised around mechanics first, cost second.

Drawdown mechanics

The drawdown is the rule that decides whether a challenge is forgiving or brutal, and it is where the two firms diverge most.

FTMO's static floor

A static maximum loss is measured from the starting balance and never moves. On a $100,000 account with a 10% floor, the failure level is $90,000 for the life of the evaluation, regardless of how much profit the trader makes. That predictability is valuable: the trader can calculate the distance to the floor every morning and size from that distance. A profitable run does not reduce the room, so the middle and final phases of the challenge are no more dangerous than the start.

FundedNext's two-phase floor

The two-phase model is described as static, which means it behaves like FTMO's floor for the life of the plan. The difference is not the mechanic but the plan's other terms: the target, the daily loss, the calendar and the payout conditions. A trader comparing the two should compare those rows, not the word "static" in isolation.

FundedNext's one-step variant

Some one-step variants use a trailing floor. A trailing floor follows profit, and an intraday trailing version follows unrealised profit. The practical effect is that a winning trade raises the failure point, and a retracement can end the account even though the balance is higher than it was at the start. The one-step model is therefore not a faster version of the two-phase model; it is a different challenge with a different risk profile.

The lesson is to read the drawdown type for the exact plan, not the brand. A trader who wants a fixed failure point should choose a static plan; one who wants a shorter path should accept that the one-step variant may compress the room. The two goals are in tension, and the plan menu is the firm's way of letting the trader choose which tension to accept.

Daily loss and measurement

Both firms measure the daily loss on equity, which means the worst moment of the day counts, not just the closed result. That is the common ground, and it is the rule that most often ends a session.

What equity-based means in practice

An open position that is $2,000 in the red has consumed $2,000 of the day's budget, even if it later recovers. If the trader adds a second position at the low point, the aggregate floating loss can breach the limit before either position is closed. The daily limit is therefore a portfolio rule, not a per-trade rule, and the trader has to track the total floating loss rather than the risk on each ticket.

The reset time

The daily budget resets at a defined time, usually midnight server time or the broker's daily close. The reset convention matters for late-session trades: a position opened twenty minutes before the reset belongs to tomorrow's budget in effect, even if the trader is thinking about today's. Both firms publish the reset time in their terms, and it should be written on the daily card.

The consequence of a breach

The consequence varies. At some firms a daily breach ends the day and the account continues; at others it ends the evaluation. The terms state which, and the difference changes the sizing. If a daily breach is fatal, the effective risk per day is the maximum loss divided by the number of days the trader can afford to lose, which is often two. If it is survivable, the daily limit is a session stop rather than an account stop.

The practical sizing rule is the same at both firms: risk a fraction of the daily budget per trade, usually one third, and stop at a fixed percentage of the budget rather than at the limit. The fraction is what keeps the daily rule a bound rather than a threat.

Targets and calendar

The target and the calendar decide the pace, and the pace decides the size. A target that requires ten R at normal risk is a different product from one that requires five, even if both are described as a percentage.

FTMO's process

FTMO's evaluation has a two-phase structure followed by a verification stage. The verification stage is not a formality; it resets the clock and requires the trader to demonstrate that the result was not a single lucky run. The calendar has no time limit on the evaluation, which allows a patient approach, but the verification stage adds a step after the profit is made. A trader who plans to finish in phase two and stop has not finished the process.

FundedNext's plan menu

FundedNext's calendar depends on the plan. Some plans have no minimum trading days; others require a minimum. Some have a time limit; others do not. The one-step model compresses the path but may impose a tighter calendar, because the firm is taking on the same risk over a shorter observation window. The two-phase model adds a phase but may give more room in phase one.

The R calculation

Convert the target and the drawdown into R at your normal risk. If the target is 10% and the maximum loss is 10%, the challenge is 1:1 at the headline level. At 1% risk per trade, the target is 10R and the floor is 10R. At 0.5% risk, the target is 20R and the floor is 20R. The ratio stays the same, but the number of trades and the time required change. The calendar determines whether the slower plan is feasible.

The comparison should therefore be made on the R distance and the calendar, not on the target percentage. A 10% target with a 6% floor is 1.67:1, which is harder than a 10% target with a 10% floor. A 10% target with a trailing floor is harder still, because the usable room shrinks as profit accumulates.

Consistency and payout

The payout conditions are where a clean pass becomes money, and they are the part of the comparison most often skipped.

Consistency

FTMO's 2-Step has no consistency rule at either stage; the 50% best-day rule applies only to the 1-Step and Instant products. On the 2-Step the trader can therefore pass and withdraw without managing the distribution of profit, subject to the 14-day first-reward window. FundedNext's models vary: some apply a consistency condition at payout, and some may apply it during the evaluation. The distinction matters because an evaluation-time rule has to be planned from the first trade, while a payout-time rule can be managed by adding winning days before the withdrawal.

First payout timing

FTMO's first payout is subject to a seasoning period, then payouts are available on demand. FundedNext releases the profit share on a fixed cycle after the first payout. The practical difference is the time between passing and receiving money. A trader who needs cash quickly should compare the first-payout conditions, not just the split.

The split

Both firms advertise competitive profit splits, and both may offer higher tiers after scaling or a number of payouts. The headline split is usually the starting rate, not the long-run rate. The comparison should be made on the net split after any payout fees, on the typical payout size, not on the headline percentage.

Payout timing and the cost of waiting

A payout that arrives in six weeks is not the same asset as one that arrives in six days, even if the amounts are identical. The delay has a cost: the trader may need the money, the account may be exposed to the market for longer, and the firm may change its terms in the interval. The first-payout conditions are therefore part of the comparison, not an afterthought. Check the seasoning period, the minimum profit threshold, the processing time and the payment method before choosing a firm.

The delay also affects the expected value of the challenge. If the first payout is three months after passing, the trader has carried the risk of the funded account for a quarter before receiving anything. A firm with a faster first payout can be worth a slightly lower split, because the time value of the money is real. The comparison should discount the payout for the waiting period, not treat all payouts as equal.

Why the first payout matters more than the split

The first payout is the point at which the evaluation becomes a business. Until then, the trader has paid fees and taken risk without receiving anything. A firm that pays reliably but at a lower split can be a better partner than one with a higher split and a difficult first withdrawal, because the first payout validates the process and funds the next attempt. The split matters over a long relationship; the first payout matters immediately.

Payout denial risk

A payout can be delayed or denied if the firm finds a conduct breach, a consistency issue or a KYC problem. The risk is not unique to either firm, but the conduct clauses differ. A trader using a third-party service should confirm the firm's position on third-party trading before starting, because a payout denial after funding is the most expensive outcome in the comparison.

Cost and billing

Both firms bill per attempt, and both sell multiple account sizes. The fee is the visible cost; the expected cost of a failed attempt is the number that matters.

The fee

FTMO's fee varies by account size, and the larger accounts cost more. FundedNext's fee also varies by plan and size, and the one-step and two-phase models may be priced differently. The fee should be compared on the cost per dollar of drawdown room, not on the headline. A larger account often costs less per dollar of room, which is why the cheapest entry point is not always the cheapest route.

Resets

Both firms offer reset or retry options, sometimes at a discount and sometimes at full price. The reset policy changes the expected cost more than the fee does. A firm with a free or discounted reset has a lower expected cost than one that charges full price, even if the initial fee is higher. Check the conditions: same plan, same size, time limit and eligibility.

Platform and data costs

Forex evaluations usually have no separate data fee, but platform costs and subscriptions can apply. Futures products have separate data costs, but this comparison is forex-focused. The cost comparison should include any monthly charge that continues between attempts.

Platforms and instruments

FTMO offers MT4, MT5, cTrader and DXtrade. FundedNext offers MT4, MT5 and cTrader. The platform coverage is broad at both firms, and the choice is more about the trader's workflow than about the firm.

A trader who uses cTrader will find it at both firms. A trader who uses DXtrade will find it at FTMO but not at FundedNext, which may be a deciding factor if the platform is central to the strategy. A trader who uses TradingView for analysis can do so at either firm and execute on the firm's platform, provided the handoff is clear.

The instrument list is also similar: forex, metals and indices at both, with the exact list varying by plan. A trader who trades a specific exotic pair or index should check the instrument list before buying, because a missing instrument can make the plan unusable even if the rules are otherwise a good fit.

A worked $100,000 example

The arithmetic is easier with a concrete example. The figures are illustrative and round; the structure is the point.

The account and the risk unit

Assume a $100,000 account, a 10% target and a 10% maximum loss. At 1% risk per trade, the target is 10R and the floor is 10R. The daily loss is 5%, or $5,000, which is 5R at 1% risk. The trader decides to risk 1.6% per trade, or $1,600, which is about a third of the daily budget and just over 6R to the floor. The target is about 6.25R.

The static plan

On a static floor, the distance to failure stays $10,000 for the life of the evaluation. After $5,000 of profit, the trader still has $10,000 of room and is halfway to the target. The risk plan does not need to change, except for the daily budget, which is fixed. The challenge is a straightforward grind: 6.25R of profit at the chosen risk, with a fixed floor.

The trailing plan

On an intraday trailing floor, the $5,000 of profit raises the floor by $5,000, leaving only $5,000 of room above the starting balance. The trader is halfway to the target but has half the room. The correct response is to cut the per-trade risk, perhaps to 0.8%, which extends the time to target but keeps the distance to the floor at a safe multiple. A trader who keeps the original size is taking more risk at the moment the floor is closest.

The comparison

The static plan is easier to execute because the risk plan is stable. The trailing plan requires active risk reduction as profit accumulates, which is a skill that many traders have not practised. The headline target and drawdown are the same, but the experience is different. That is the practical meaning of the FTMO versus FundedNext comparison: not which firm is better, but which rule shape fits the trader's process.

Which trader should pick which

The choice depends on the trader's process, not on a universal ranking. Four profiles cover most cases. Before deciding, it helps to look at recently passed evaluations to see which plans actually complete, and at what buyers report about the firms' payout behaviour.

The trader who wants a fixed failure point

Choose the static two-phase plan. FTMO is the reference example, and FundedNext's two-phase model is close. The fixed floor allows a stable risk plan and a predictable distance to failure. The trade-off is a longer process, including a verification stage at FTMO, and a fee that may be higher than a one-step plan.

The trader who wants speed

Choose the one-step plan, if the drawdown style is compatible with the strategy. The one-step path is shorter, but it may compress the room and require smaller size. A trader with a high win rate and a small average loss may find the one-step plan efficient; a trader with a lower win rate may find the tighter room unforgiving.

The trader who wants a softer phase one

Choose the two-phase FundedNext model or FTMO. The two-phase structure usually gives more room in phase one, which is where most failures occur. The cost is a second target and a longer calendar.

The trader using a passing service

Choose the plan whose rule map the service can execute with the most margin around the binding rule. A static plan is easier to map and to size; a trailing plan requires active recalculation. The service should be able to explain the choice in terms of the rule map, not the fee. How to verify the current terms

Firm terms change, and a comparison written from memory is a comparison of last year's products. Verify these items on the plan document before buying.

  1. The exact plan name and whether it is one-step or two-phase.
  2. Whether the maximum loss is static, trails at the daily close, or trails intraday.
  3. The maximum loss percentage and the reference point.
  4. The daily loss percentage, measurement point and reset time.
  5. The profit target for each phase and whether it is measured on balance or equity.
  6. The time limit, minimum trading days and the definition of a trading day.
  7. The consistency rule: when it applies and how it is calculated.
  8. The profit split, first-payout conditions and payout cycle.
  9. The conduct section: news windows, weekend holding, hedging, automation and third-party execution.
  10. The reset policy and any platform or data fees.

Get the answers from the plan document, not from a review. If a term is ambiguous, ask support in writing and keep the reply. The effort is small compared with the cost of discovering a rule after a failure. If a service is involved, send the completed list to the provider and ask them to confirm it matches the plan being traded.

Frequently asked questions

Does FundedNext use a trailing drawdown?

The two-phase model is described as static; some one-step variants use a trailing floor. Check the plan name and the drawdown section before buying.

Is FTMO's daily loss measured on equity?

Yes, the daily loss is equity-based, so an open losing position counts before it is closed. The reset time is stated in the terms and should be written on the daily card.

Which firm has the easier payout?

The payout conditions differ by plan and can change. Compare the first-payout seasoning, consistency rule, minimum days and processing time, not just the profit split.

Can I use the same strategy on both firms?

Which firm is better for a beginner?

A beginner usually benefits from a static floor and a two-phase structure, because the fixed failure point is easier to plan around. The longer calendar is a trade-off, not a disadvantage.
