Prop Firm Scaling Plan Explained

A scaling plan is the promise that a funded account will grow if the trader performs. It is one of the most attractive features in prop trading and one of the least understood, because the requirements are usually stricter than the headline and the math of each stage is rarely written down. This guide explains the three scaling models, models what a typical path actually pays, and shows when opening a second account is a better use of capital than chasing a scale-up.

Key takeaways

  • Scaling is a reward for a pattern, not for a single good month. Most plans require consistency across several payout cycles.
  • The headline scale-up percentage is not the same as the increase in your income, because risk and drawdown scale with the account.
  • A second account can produce more income than a scale-up, with less waiting and more diversification.
  • Read the scaling clause for the requirements, the reset conditions and the maximum account size before counting on it.

What a scaling plan actually is

A scaling plan is a contractual path from a smaller funded account to a larger one. It usually specifies a series of stages, the requirements to move from one stage to the next, and the maximum account size the plan will reach. The stages are often expressed as a percentage increase — 25%, 50% or 100% — and the requirements are usually a combination of profitable months, minimum payouts, consistency and adherence to the rules.

The first thing to understand is that a scaling plan is not automatic. It is an option the firm grants if you satisfy its conditions, and the conditions are designed to identify traders who are likely to keep performing. A trader who has one excellent month and five flat ones has not demonstrated the pattern the firm is looking for. A trader who has six modest profitable months has, even if the total profit is lower. The plan rewards reliability, not brilliance.

The second thing is that the scale-up is usually applied to the account size, not to the trader's risk budget. If the account grows from $100,000 to $150,000, the maximum loss also grows, and the dollar risk per trade grows with it if the trader keeps the same percentage. The income can increase, but so can the size of a bad day. A scaling plan is a larger version of the same challenge, not a shortcut to a different one.

The third thing is that the plan may have a ceiling. Some firms scale to a maximum of $200,000, $400,000 or $1,000,000, and the ceiling may apply per trader or per strategy. A trader who plans to scale indefinitely should check the ceiling before building a business around it. The ceiling is usually stated in the terms, but it may be in a footnote or an FAQ rather than the scaling page.

The three scaling models

Most scaling plans fall into one of three models. The model determines what the firm is measuring and how long the path takes.

Profit-based scaling

The firm increases the account after a defined amount of profit, either in dollars or as a percentage of the account. A common structure is a 25% increase after a 10% profit, or a 50% increase after three profitable months. The advantage is that the path is linked to performance; the disadvantage is that a single large month can satisfy the profit requirement without demonstrating consistency. Some plans therefore combine profit with a consistency condition.

Time-based scaling

The firm increases the account after a defined number of profitable months or payout cycles, regardless of the total profit. A typical structure is a 25% increase every three profitable months, up to a ceiling. The advantage is predictability: a trader knows roughly when the next stage arrives. The disadvantage is that the increase may be small relative to the profit generated, and a trader who has a bad month resets the clock.

Consistency-based scaling

The firm increases the account when the trader demonstrates a consistent distribution of profit — for example, no single month contributing more than 40% of total profit over six months. This is the strictest model and the closest to how a fund would evaluate a trader. It rewards the behaviour that survives in funded accounts, but it can be slow, and a trader with a lumpy but profitable style may never qualify.

The model matters because it tells you which behaviour to optimise. Under a profit-based plan, the optimal behaviour is to reach the profit target as reliably as possible, which may mean taking slightly more risk. Under a consistency-based plan, the optimal behaviour is to smooth the distribution, which usually means smaller size and more sessions. Under a time-based plan, the optimal behaviour is to avoid a losing month, which may mean reducing risk near the end of the qualifying period. A trader who applies the wrong behaviour to the wrong model will keep resetting the clock.

The math of a scaling path

Scaling plans are usually described in percentages, which makes them sound larger than they are. The arithmetic below is illustrative and uses round numbers; the structure is what matters.

Suppose a trader starts with a $100,000 account, makes 5% a month, and takes an 80% profit split. The monthly payout is $4,000 before fees. The firm offers a 25% scale-up every three profitable months.

Start$100,000$5,000$4,0000

Scale 4$244,141$12,207$9,76612

After a year, the account has grown by about 144% and the monthly payout has grown from $4,000 to about $9,766 — a 2.4x increase, not a 2.4x compounding of the original because the scale-ups are applied to the base account, not to the accumulated profit. The trader has also received twelve monthly payouts along the way, which is the real income of the path. The scale-up is a multiplier on future payouts, not a lump sum.

The math changes if the trader withdraws the profit rather than leaving it in the account. Most prop firms keep the account size at the funded level and pay out the profit, so the 5% monthly return is calculated on the same base unless a scale-up occurs. The scale-up is the only mechanism that increases the base. A trader who assumes compounding without a scale-up is overestimating the path.

There is a second arithmetic point that is easy to miss: the risk per trade grows with the account. At 1% risk, the $100,000 account risks $1,000 per trade; at $244,141, it risks $2,441. The dollar swings are larger, and the psychological difficulty increases even if the percentage is unchanged. A trader who scales without adjusting their tolerance may find that the larger account is harder to trade, not easier.

The requirements that block traders

The scale-up percentage is the marketing; the requirements are the product. Most traders who fail to scale do so because they miss a requirement that was not emphasized.

Profitable months

The most common requirement is a number of profitable months, often three to six. A month is usually defined as a calendar month or a payout cycle, and a small loss can reset the count. The definition matters: a month with one $50 loss is not profitable, even if the trader was up for most of it. A trader who is close to a scale-up may reduce risk near the end of the month to protect the streak, which is a rational response to the rule but can distort the strategy.

Minimum payouts

Some plans require a minimum number of payouts before scaling, or a minimum total payout. The requirement ensures that the trader has actually withdrawn money, not just generated paper profit. A trader who leaves profit in the account may still qualify, but the terms often specify a payout count. Check whether the payouts must be consecutive and whether a skipped month resets the count.

Consistency

A consistency rule at scaling can be stricter than the payout rule. It may require that no single month contributes more than a set percentage of total profit, or that the best month is within a multiple of the average. A trader with a lumpy distribution may be profitable and still ineligible. The rule is designed to identify traders whose income is repeatable, not traders who had one exceptional run.

Rule compliance

Any breach during the qualifying period usually resets the count. That includes a daily loss breach, a consistency breach, or a conduct issue. A trader who is close to a scale-up has an incentive to be more conservative, which is the intended effect of the rule. The risk is that the trader becomes so cautious that the account stops producing, which delays the scale-up anyway.

Why scaling is not compounding

Scaling and compounding are often described as if they were the same thing. They are not, and the difference changes the expected value of the path.

Compounding means that profit is added to the capital base, so the next return is calculated on a larger amount. In a funded prop account, the profit is usually withdrawn, and the account size stays at the funded level unless a scale-up occurs. The trader's income does not grow automatically with performance; it grows only when the firm increases the account. A trader who makes 5% every month on a $100,000 account earns $5,000 a month, not a compounding series, unless the profit is left in the account and the firm allows it to increase the base.

Scaling is a step function. The account jumps by a percentage at defined intervals, and the income jumps with it. Between steps, the income is flat. That has two consequences. First, the path is lumpy: a trader may wait months for a step, then receive a large increase. Second, the step is conditional on performance during the waiting period, which creates a incentive to protect the streak rather than maximise the return.

The practical implication is that a trader should value the path on the expected number of steps, not on the headline percentage. A 25% step every three months is a 25% increase per quarter, which is roughly 100% annualised if the trader qualifies every time. A 25% step every six months is roughly 50% annualised. The difference is large, and it depends on the requirements, not on the headline.

Scaling and drawdown

A larger account is not just a larger income; it is a larger drawdown. The same percentage loss produces a larger dollar loss, and the same daily limit produces a larger dollar stop. The risk of a scale-up is that the trader's process does not scale with the account.

The dollar-risk problem

A trader who risks 1% per trade on a $100,000 account risks $1,000. On a $250,000 account, the same percentage risks $2,500. If the trader's emotional tolerance was calibrated to $1,000, the larger account may produce worse decisions even though the percentage is unchanged. The correct response is to scale slowly and to keep the dollar risk within a range the trader has already demonstrated they can handle.

The drawdown problem

The maximum loss also increases with the account, which means a bad streak costs more in dollars. A 10% drawdown on a $100,000 account is $10,000; on a $250,000 account it is $25,000. The trader may have the same percentage tolerance but a different absolute tolerance. A scale-up that doubles the account also doubles the cost of the worst case, which is easy to overlook when the focus is on the income.

The consistency problem

Some scaling plans tighten the consistency requirement as the account grows. A trader who qualified at $100,000 with a 50% best-month rule may face a 40% rule at $250,000. The larger account is not just a larger version of the same challenge; it can be a stricter one. Read the scaling clause for any change in the rules at each stage, not just the account size.

The practical rule is to treat a scale-up as a new evaluation, not a reward. Recalculate the risk unit, the daily budget and the consistency distribution before trading the larger account. A trader who applies the old plan to the new size is taking more risk than they have tested.

Scale-up versus a second account

A scale-up is one way to increase income. A second funded account is another, and it is often more efficient. The comparison depends on the capital required, the waiting time and the diversification benefit.

The capital comparison

A scale-up is usually free in the sense that it does not require a new evaluation fee. A second account requires a new evaluation, which costs the firm's fee and the trader's time. If the scale-up is available in three months and the second account can be passed in one month, the second account may produce income sooner, even after paying the fee. The comparison is the expected income from the second account over the waiting period minus the fee, versus the expected income from the scale-up after the waiting period.

The diversification benefit

Two accounts at different firms diversify the rule risk. If one firm changes its terms or delays a payout, the other account continues to produce. A single large account concentrates the risk in one firm's rulebook. For a trader building a business, diversification can be worth more than the scale-up percentage.

The operational cost

Two accounts mean two sets of rules, two dashboards and two payout schedules. The operational overhead is real, and it can reduce the trader's effective focus. A trader who can manage one account well may not manage two equally well. The scale-up has the advantage of simplicity: one account, one rulebook, one payout schedule.

The decision is not either-or. A trader can pursue a scale-up on the original account while building a second account at another firm. The scale-up is passive if the performance is there; the second account is active work. The combination gives both the step increase and the diversification, at the cost of more operational load.

Planning a realistic scaling path

A scaling plan should be written down like any other trading plan. Five elements are enough.

  1. The target account size. The size at which the trader's income goal is met, and the number of stages required to reach it.
  2. The qualifying behaviour. The monthly return, consistency and risk that satisfy the plan's requirements without distorting the strategy.
  3. The waiting time. The number of months per stage, and the expected calendar to the target.
  4. The risk at each stage. The dollar risk per trade and the maximum drawdown, recalculated for each account size.
  5. The fallback. What the trader does if a stage is missed, a rule changes, or the firm delays a payout.

The plan should be conservative. A path that requires every month to be profitable is fragile; a path that assumes one losing month per quarter is more realistic. The same is true of the return assumption: a 10% monthly return is not a plan, it is a hope. A 3–5% monthly return with a consistent process is a more credible basis for a multi-stage path.

There is also a cap to consider. If the plan reaches the firm's maximum account size, further scaling is impossible, and the trader needs a second firm or a different structure. Knowing the ceiling in advance prevents building a plan that runs out of stages.

The plan should also include a review date. Every quarter, compare the actual account size, monthly payouts and rule compliance with the path you wrote. If the pace is behind, the correct response is not to increase risk; it is to extend the timeline or add a second account. A scaling plan that is reviewed is a plan that can be corrected before a missed stage becomes a lost evaluation.

Common scaling mistakes

These are the errors that most often turn a scaling opportunity into a setback.

  1. Assuming the scale-up is automatic. It is conditional on the requirements, and the requirements may reset. Read the clause before counting on the increase.
  2. Optimising for the scale-up instead of the process. Reducing risk near the end of a qualifying month can protect the streak but distort the strategy. The plan should be sustainable, not a sprint to a step.
  3. Keeping the same dollar risk after a scale-up. The account is larger; the dollar risk should be recalculated. Keeping the old dollar risk may be too small relative to the new account, or too large if the trader was already at the limit.
  4. Ignoring the tighter rules at the next stage. A scale-up can come with a stricter consistency or drawdown rule. Read the clause for each stage, not just the first.
  5. Concentrating everything in one firm. A single large account is exposed to a single rulebook. Diversification is a real benefit, even if it costs a second evaluation fee.
  6. Counting profits before they are paid. The scale-up increases the account, not the bank balance. The income is the payout, and the payout depends on the firm's schedule.
  7. Scaling faster than the process. A trader who doubles the account before doubling the consistency of the process is taking on more risk than they have demonstrated they can manage.

The common thread is that scaling is a test of whether the process survives a larger size. A trader who treats it as a reward may find that the larger account exposes weaknesses that the smaller one hid. A trader who treats it as a new evaluation will prepare for it the same way they prepared for the first one.

Questions to ask the firm

Before building a plan around a scaling clause, get specific answers to these questions in writing.

  1. What are the exact requirements for each stage, including the definition of a profitable month and the consistency calculation?
  2. Does a losing month reset the count, or is it cumulative?
  3. Do the payouts have to be consecutive, and is there a minimum payout amount?
  4. Does the drawdown or consistency rule change at each stage?
  5. Is the scale-up applied to the account size or to the profit split?
  6. What is the maximum account size under the plan, and does it apply per trader or per strategy?
  7. Does a rule breach during the qualifying period reset the count?
  8. Can the firm change the scaling terms after the account is funded?

The answers should be in the terms, not only in a support reply. If a support reply adds a condition that is not in the terms, ask for it to be confirmed. A scaling plan is a contractual promise, and the written version is the one that will be enforced.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to scale a prop firm account?

Most plans require three to six profitable months per stage, so a meaningful scale-up can take a year or more. The exact pace depends on the model and the requirements, not on the headline percentage.

Is a scaling plan guaranteed?

No. It is conditional on the requirements, which usually include profitable months, payouts, consistency and rule compliance. A missed requirement can reset the count.

Does the profit split change when the account scales?

Sometimes. Some plans keep the same split, others improve it at higher stages. Check the scaling clause for the split at each stage, not just the account size.

Can I scale faster by making more profit?

Only if the plan is profit-based and the requirements are met. Many plans use time or consistency requirements that cannot be accelerated by a single large month.

Is a second account better than a scale-up?

It depends on the waiting time and the capital available. A second account can produce income sooner and diversifies firm risk, but it costs a new evaluation fee and adds operational load.

What happens if I breach a rule during the qualifying period?

Most plans reset the count, and some may disqualify the account from scaling for a period. Check the clause for the specific consequence before relying on the path.

Do I need to withdraw profits to qualify for a scale-up?

Many plans require a minimum number of payouts, so leaving all profit in the account may not satisfy the requirement. Check whether the plan counts payouts or profit.
