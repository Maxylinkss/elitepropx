Apex Trader Funding vs Earn2Trade: which rulebook fits your trading

Both firms evaluate US futures, so the market, the platforms and the session are the same. The split is in the trail. Apex measures its floor against peak equity including open profit, which means an unrealised winner moves the failure point while the trade is running. Earn2Trade trails at end of day on most programmes, so the floor updates after the close rather than during the session. That single difference decides whether a position can breathe. Figures are the rulebooks' published shape, not quotes — confirm the plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Apex trails intraday against peak equity including floating profit; Earn2Trade trails at end of day on most programmes.
  • An end-of-day trail leaves room for an open winner to retrace without lifting the floor mid-trade.
  • Apex has no separate daily stop on the standard evaluation; Earn2Trade applies one.

What the two rulebooks measure

Two futures evaluations measure the same five things: progress toward a target, survival against a floor, behaviour inside a session, pace through the calendar, and the conditions for converting a pass into money. Because the market is identical, the differences are all in how those five are defined, and the largest is the floor.

Apex defines survival against peak equity including open profit. That is a live number: a position that is $800 in profit has already raised the failure point by $800, and the trail continues to update as the position moves. Earn2Trade trails at end of day on most programmes, which means the floor is recalculated after the close from the day's settled high rather than continuously from an unrealised one.

The daily rule is the second difference. Apex's standard evaluation has no separate daily stop, so a session is bounded only by the trail. Earn2Trade applies a daily stop, which caps the session independently of the total floor. That changes how a bad day is absorbed: one product stops the bleeding at a figure, the other relies on the trader to do it.

The third difference is commercial. Apex bills monthly per account, so duration is a cost. Earn2Trade bills once per programme, so duration is free. Two products that evaluate the same market therefore reward opposite pacing strategies, and the difference compounds across retries.

Intraday trail against end-of-day trail

An intraday trail updates continuously from the highest equity the account has reached, including unrealised profit. An end-of-day trail updates once, after the session settles, from the highest value recorded at a defined point. The distinction sounds technical and it changes what a trader can do with an open position.

On an intraday trail, a position that runs $1,500 into profit has already lifted the failure point by $1,500. If it retraces to entry, the account has not merely given back a gain; it has consumed $1,500 of buffer that the trader never banked. The floor remembers the high. On an end-of-day trail, the same position can retrace to entry and the floor is unchanged, because the settled high never included the unrealised gain.

The behavioural consequence is that intraday trails discourage holding for a large target. A trader who lets a winner run is repeatedly raising the floor and then risking a give-back, which is a poor risk-adjusted choice even when the target is reached. End-of-day trails allow a position to breathe within the session, which suits a method with a wide target and a patient hold.

The second consequence is at the close. On an end-of-day trail, a good day raises the floor once, so the position size for the next session has to be recalculated against the new buffer. That is a single adjustment per day rather than a continuous one, which is easier to execute and easier to journal. Neither rule is better in the abstract; they suit different holding periods.

A $100,000 attempt with a $1,000 open winner

Model a $100,000 account with a $3,000 threshold. On Apex, the failure point starts at $97,000. A position opens and runs to $1,000 unrealised profit, which lifts the failure point to $98,000. The position then retraces to entry and is closed flat. The balance is still $100,000, the failure point is still $98,000, and the trader has lost $1,000 of room without losing a dollar.

On Earn2Trade's end-of-day version, the same sequence leaves the failure point at $97,000 until the close. If the position is closed flat, nothing changes. If it is closed at $1,000 profit, the floor moves to $98,000 at settlement, and the trader starts the next session with the gain banked and the buffer recomputed.

The difference in room is $1,000 on a $3,000 threshold — a third of the buffer — from a trade that made nothing. That is the strongest argument for matching the trail type to the holding period. A scalper who closes within minutes is barely affected. A swing trader who holds a runner for hours is affected every time.

The sizing response follows directly. On an intraday trail, the risk unit should be based on the buffer as it stands after the last high, and the plan should take partial profit at a defined level rather than holding for a target. On an end-of-day trail, the unit can be based on the settled buffer and the plan can allow a wider target, because a retracement during the session does not move the floor.

The daily stop difference

Apex's standard evaluation has no separate daily stop. The trailing floor applies continuously, so a bad session is bounded by the floor rather than by a session rule. Earn2Trade applies a daily stop, which ends the day at a known loss regardless of how much of the total floor remains.

A daily rule changes the failure mode in a useful way. It interrupts a losing run rather than letting it consume the buffer, and it forces a review at a point where the trader can still think clearly. On a product with a live trail, that interruption is especially valuable, because a bad session can otherwise take the account from comfortable to critical in an afternoon.

Without a daily rule, the trader has to supply one. A sensible personal cap on Apex is a figure that keeps a worst day at a third of the buffer — with $3,000 of room, roughly $1,000, which at a $250 unit is four losses. That number belongs on the card beside the buffer and the unit, and it should be reviewed before the session rather than after a loss.

On Earn2Trade the published limit is likely to be more generous than a sensible personal cap. The firm's rule protects the account; the personal rule protects the attempt. Running both is not redundant, because the smaller one is the one that actually shapes behaviour.

The educational layer on one side

Earn2Trade's published model includes an educational path before the funded step. That is a genuine product difference, and it matters for two kinds of trader: one who is new to futures and needs the structure, and one who wants a documented process before risking a larger account.

The cost is time. An educational path adds a stage that does not pay, and a trader with an existing method may find it redundant. The benefit is a sample: a structured programme produces a record of trades that can be reviewed, which is exactly what a payout review asks for and what a self-directed trader usually fails to keep.

Apex's path is shorter and assumes the trader already has a method. That suits an experienced futures trader and it removes a gate that would otherwise delay the funded account. It also means the review has less to look at, so the journal becomes the trader's responsibility rather than the programme's output.

Neither approach is better in general. A trader who has never traded futures should value the structure; a trader with ten years of NinjaTrader history should not pay for it. The mistake is to treat the educational layer as a cost to be minimised rather than as a stage that either fits the trader's experience or does not.

Contract sizing on each

Both products size in contracts against tick value, so the arithmetic is identical and only the buffer differs. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 per contract. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100. One Micro E-mini Dow contract pays $0.50 per tick, so a 20-tick stop risks $10.

With a $250 unit, the Nasdaq micro allows eight contracts, the S&P mini allows two, and the Dow micro allows twenty-five. Those counts vary by a factor of twelve for the same dollar risk, which is why sizing from contracts rather than from dollars is the most common error on a futures evaluation.

The buffer differs because the trails differ. On Apex, a live trail means the buffer can shrink mid-session, so the unit should be recalculated at the last known high rather than at the open. On Earn2Trade, the buffer is settled at the close, so the unit can be set once per day and held through the session. That is a small operational difference with a large effect on how often the trader has to make a sizing decision.

Both need a slippage allowance. A stop order becomes a market order on trigger, and in a fast tape the fill can be several ticks beyond the level. On a 15-tick stop, three ticks of slip is a 20% overrun. Adding that to the planned risk changes the contract count by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the unit.

Minimum days and the calendar

Both products apply a minimum-day or minimum-session condition, so neither can be finished in a single good week. The condition exists to ensure the result is a process rather than one oversized trade, and it turns the calendar into a constraint that has to be planned rather than discovered.

On Apex, a monthly fee makes the minimum-day rule expensive: every required session is a day of subscription, and a calendar extended by caution is a calendar billed. On Earn2Trade, a one-off fee makes the same rule free: the sessions are required, and they cost nothing beyond the time. That is the clearest example of how billing and rulebook interact.

The planning response is the same on both: count the required sessions, map them against your availability, and build the target across that number rather than in the first week. A trader who reaches the target in week one and then waits out the minimum still has to keep the account alive during the wait, which is an underrated risk.

The wait is not passive. It is the best opportunity to build the distribution a consistency condition wants and to prepare the payout documentation. Treating the required sessions as administration rather than as trading is what keeps a completed target from becoming a breached account.

Platforms and data costs

The lists are Rithmic, Tradovate and NinjaTrader on both sides, which removes the workflow question entirely. A trader who already uses one of them can move between the products without changing anything, and a trader who does not will face the same learning curve on either.

The choice within the list is about style. Rithmic is a data and routing layer valued for depth of market and speed; Tradovate is the lightest front end and the fastest to learn; NinjaTrader is the programmable one with the largest ecosystem. The evaluation result does not depend on the choice, and the trader's familiarity does.

Data costs are usually separate from the evaluation and are billed by the platform or the provider. Depth-of-market data is more expensive than chart data, and the feed a method needs determines the real monthly cost. On Apex, that cost sits on top of a monthly subscription; on Earn2Trade, it sits on top of a one-off fee, which makes the first month more expensive and later months cheaper.

Automation is the fourth consideration. NinjaTrader supports coded strategies, and both firms' activity clauses apply regardless of platform. A compliant strategy run on a restricted order type is still a breach, so the clauses should be read before a strategy is deployed rather than after it produces a review question.

Monthly against one-off

At an illustrative $150 per month plus a $50 data fee, an Apex attempt costs $200 for one month, $600 for three and $1,200 for six. At an illustrative $500 one-off, an Earn2Trade attempt costs $500 whatever the calendar. The break-even is around two and a half months, which is close to the typical duration of a properly sized attempt.

That makes the two closer than the headline suggests, and it makes the decision depend on expected duration rather than on price. A trader who passes in six weeks is better off on the subscription; a trader who takes five months is not. Since a correctly sized futures attempt usually finishes in six to ten weeks, the two are roughly equivalent in expected cost.

Retries change the arithmetic sharply. Three Apex attempts at three months each is nine months of subscription plus fees, which can be four times the headline price. Three Earn2Trade attempts cost three fees with no calendar charge. A trader who expects to need retries should prefer the one-off structure.

The hidden line is behavioural. A monthly fee creates pressure to finish, which is useful for a disciplined trader and dangerous for one who presses. A one-off fee removes the pressure, which is comfortable and can produce an attempt that drifts. Neither is wrong; both require the trader to supply the pace the billing does not.

Payout paths

Apex pays from the funded account after qualifying days, with the request made from the dashboard. Earn2Trade pays after qualification under its own programme terms. The difference that matters is the time from a profitable month to cleared funds, and the conditions that can interrupt it.

Both review conduct before releasing profit: the statements, the size relative to the rules, the distribution of profit and any pattern resembling prohibited practice. On a product with a live trail, the statements show a floor that moved during the session, which can look odd to a reviewer unfamiliar with the mechanic. A journal that records the buffer at the start and end of each session answers that question before it is asked.

Identity and payment verification sit on the critical path on both. Withdrawals usually return to the method used for the fee, so an expired card or a changed wallet triggers a manual review. Complete verification before the target is reached, and keep the receipt for the fee.

Confirm what the split is measured on and whether the funded account inherits the evaluation's rules. Those two answers decide the real value of a pass, and neither is usually on the pricing page.

Retries and expected cost

Estimate the pass probability from your own record and divide the cost by it. If an Apex attempt costs $600 and has a 30% pass probability, the expected cost per funded account is $2,000. If an Earn2Trade attempt costs $500 and has a 30% probability, the expected cost is $1,667. The difference is smaller than the fee difference suggests and it is driven by duration.

The lever that improves both numbers is a smaller unit. Reducing the unit by a third typically raises the pass probability by converting a normal losing streak from a breach into a drawdown. The fee stays the same, so the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

The second lever is the trail type. A trader whose method holds winners for hours is structurally better matched to an end-of-day trail, and running the same method on a live trail will produce a lower pass probability regardless of skill. Choosing the trail that matches the holding period is worth more than a smaller unit.

The third lever is the calendar. On Earn2Trade a longer attempt is free, so patience is affordable. On Apex it is billed, so the optimal duration is shorter and the sizing has to be right the first time. The structures genuinely reward different behaviour, and the trader should choose the one whose incentives match their temperament.

Releases and the overnight session

Futures react to a short list of scheduled events: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment reports and, for some instruments, inventory data. The reaction is fast, the spread widens before the release, and the stop that looked safe at 09:25 is not the stop that fills at 09:30.

Restrictions vary by plan. A window of two minutes before and after is a different rule from a window of thirty, and "no new positions" differs from "no holding". The clause is short, rarely on the marketing page and enforced at review, which makes it a delayed failure with a complete trail.

The conservative plan is to be flat into the release and re-enter after the first half hour. That costs some of the largest moves and removes a category of dispute. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the buffer can survive if it gaps — a calculation that is stricter on a live trail, because the gap can lift the floor and consume the buffer in the same move.

The overnight session is the other scheduled risk. It is thinner, spreads are wider, and a position held into the settlement may be marked at a price that changes the buffer before the next session opens. On an end-of-day trail, that settlement is the moment the floor updates, so the position carried into it is the position that sets tomorrow's risk unit.

Who should choose which

Apex fits a futures trader who closes positions within a session, who takes profit at levels rather than holding for a large target, and who can watch the US regular session. The live trail suits precision and punishes give-backs, and the monthly fee rewards a trader who expects to finish inside two months.

Earn2Trade fits a trader who holds a winner for longer, who wants a daily stop to cap the session, and who values an educational structure or a one-off fee. The end-of-day trail lets a position breathe, and the one-off billing makes a longer or more cautious attempt affordable.

The tie-breaker is the holding period. A trader whose average trade lasts minutes is barely affected by the trail type and should choose on billing. A trader whose average trade lasts hours is strongly affected and should choose on the trail, because the difference can consume a third of the buffer in a single trade that makes nothing.

If the answer is still unclear, simulate both on the same hundred trades with the same dollar risk: apply the live trail to one and the settled trail to the other. Whichever survives with the smaller drawdown is the structure the method already fits.

What to check before paying

The first check is the trail definition: whether the high is measured from equity including open profit, from settled balance, or from a scheduled point in the day. The difference between those three is larger than any fee difference and it is usually stated in a single clause.

The second is the daily stop and its basis. Equity or realised, and what time it resets. The third is the minimum-day condition and whether it applies to the evaluation, the funded account or the payout. The fourth is whether the funded phase inherits the evaluation's rules or replaces them.

The fifth is billing. Confirm whether the fee is monthly, one-off, or promotional, and whether a retry is charged again. The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, automation and third-party trading. Each is short, each is enforced, and none is reliably on the comparison page.

Save a dated copy of every answer. It takes ten minutes and it is the only document that settles a dispute six weeks later, when the terms page has been revised and the support thread has scrolled away.

A four-week plan for either

Week one is calibration: half the unit, a record of the buffer before every entry, and a note of how the floor behaves after a winner. On the live trail the goal is to see it move during the position; on the settled trail it is to see it move once at the close.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recalculate the buffer each morning and halve the unit when it falls below one twelfth of the starting threshold. On the live trail this is where the compression is felt; on the settled trail it is where the target usually appears.

Week four is consolidation: reduce size, protect the result, and prepare the payout documents. A trader who reaches the target early and then increases size to make the pass worthwhile is the trader who loses the account in the final week, and the pattern is identical on both products.

Across all four weeks, three numbers belong on a card: buffer, unit and daily cap. The rulebooks differ in how they move the first one; neither can do anything about a trader who does not look.

Common questions

What is the difference between an intraday and an end-of-day trail?

An intraday trail follows the highest equity reached, including open profit, so a give-back consumes buffer. An end-of-day trail updates once after the close, so a position can retrace within the session without moving the floor.

Does Apex have a daily stop?

The standard evaluation does not list a separate daily stop; the trailing floor is the only published limit on a session. A personal daily cap is worth adding, since the trail alone allows a very bad day.

Which firm is cheaper?

It depends on duration. A one-month Apex subscription is cheaper than a one-off fee; a six-month attempt is not. Since a correctly sized futures attempt usually finishes inside three months, the two are often close.

Are the platforms the same?

The lists are Rithmic, Tradovate and NinjaTrader on both sides, so the workflow does not change. Data fees may still differ, and they are billed separately from the evaluation on both.

Does earn2trade require an educational stage?

The published model includes an educational path before the funded step. It suits a trader new to futures and adds time for an experienced one, so check whether the programme allows a direct route.

Can either be passed in a week?

No. Both apply a minimum-day or minimum-session condition, so the calendar is a constraint regardless of how quickly the target is reached. Plan the target across the required sessions rather than in the first week.
