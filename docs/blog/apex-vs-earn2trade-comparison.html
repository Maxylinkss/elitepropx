Apex Trader Funding vs Earn2Trade: which rulebook fits your trading

Both firms evaluate US futures, but the commercial models run the opposite way to the way most comparison pages describe them. Apex charges once for 30 days of access and has no monthly renewal; Earn2Trade's Gauntlet Mini and Trader Career Path evaluations are monthly subscriptions that stop charging once the account is funded. The rules differ as well: Apex uses a 50% best-day rule at payout, Earn2Trade a 30% consistency rule during the evaluation that lifts when funding begins. The figures below come from each firm's own pages, checked on 21 October 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Apex is a one-time fee for 30 days. Earn2Trade charges monthly during the evaluation and stops charging when the account is funded.
  • Apex offers an EOD or an intraday drawdown. Earn2Trade's evaluations use an EOD drawdown, with a trailing floor in the live account.
  • Both have a daily loss limit: fixed in dollars at Apex's EOD model, and dollar-based at Earn2Trade.
  • Consistency is applied at different stages: 50% at payout at Apex, 30% during the evaluation at Earn2Trade, lifting once funded.
  • Neither firm requires minimum trading days, so a pass can happen in the first week on either.
  • Earn2Trade publishes a 50% split below a profit threshold and 80% above it; Apex publishes its split and activation terms in the help centre.

What the two rulebooks measure

Two futures evaluations with the same market and similar platforms are separated by three questions. Does the floor follow the high-water mark, and does it include open profit? Is there a session limit on top of the floor? And how is the fee charged, because that determines what a slow attempt costs.

Apex answers the first question twice. The EOD evaluation recalculates the threshold at the close from the ending balance and holds it fixed through the next session. The intraday evaluation moves the threshold in real time with peak balance, including unrealised profit, and never moves it down. On Rithmic and WealthCharts the intraday threshold stops rising once it reaches the profit-target balance; on Tradovate it trails for the life of the evaluation.

Earn2Trade answers it once. The Gauntlet Mini and Trader Career Path evaluations use an end-of-day drawdown, with a daily loss limit on top. Once funded, the LiveSim account keeps the EOD model while the live account uses a trailing drawdown. That transition is the detail most comparisons miss: a trader who passes on a settled floor wakes up to a moving one.

The third question is where the two firms genuinely diverge. Apex charges once for 30 days and requires no minimum trading days. Earn2Trade's evaluation is a monthly subscription — $170 a month for GAU50, $150 a month for TCP25 — and the subscription stops once the account is funded. A trader who takes three months to pass pays three times at Earn2Trade and once at Apex, which reverses the usual "subscription is cheaper" assumption.

Two different trails

A trail that includes open profit moves while a position is running. A trade that reaches $900 unrealised has lifted the failure point by $900, and a retracement to entry consumes that room without a dollar being lost. A trail that settles at the close does not move until the session ends, so a position can breathe within the day.

Apex's intraday model is the strictest version of the first kind, with a platform exception. On a $50,000 account with a $2,000 drawdown, the threshold starts at $48,000. If unrealised profit takes the balance to $50,900, the threshold becomes $48,900 immediately. On Rithmic and WealthCharts it stops climbing once it reaches the target balance of $53,000; on Tradovate it keeps climbing with the peak.

Earn2Trade's evaluation is the settled version. The EOD drawdown is calculated from the closing balance, so a trade that runs into profit and comes back does not move the floor until the close. That is a meaningful advantage for a trader who holds through intraday noise, and it disappears in the live account, where the drawdown trails.

The planning consequence is a taper on the live trail. Full size until half the target, half size until three quarters, and a third afterwards. On the EOD model the taper can be gentler, because the floor only moves once a day and the buffer is known before the session begins.

A $50,000 attempt, line by line

Take Apex's EOD model on $50,000: a $3,000 target, a $2,000 drawdown and a $1,000 daily loss limit. The threshold starts at $48,000 and recalculates at each close. A day that closes at $51,000 moves it to $49,000; a day that closes at $49,500 leaves it at $49,500, because the threshold never moves down. Losing $1,000 in a session pauses trading for the day without closing the account.

Take Earn2Trade's GAU50: a $3,000 target, a $2,000 EOD drawdown, a $1,100 daily loss limit and a 30% consistency rule. The target is the same, the drawdown is the same, and the daily allowance is $100 wider. The consistency rule is the additional constraint: the best day cannot be more than 30% of total profit, which means a $3,000 target cannot be built from one $3,000 day. The firm's own guidance is that the rule effectively requires at least four profitable days.

That is the clearest practical difference between the two evaluations. Apex lets a trader pass in one session and applies its consistency test at the payout stage; Earn2Trade requires the pass itself to be distributed. A trader with a concentrated method will notice the Earn2Trade rule during the evaluation and the Apex rule at the first withdrawal.

Daily stops on both sides

Both firms use a daily loss limit, which is not true of every futures evaluation. Apex's EOD model publishes $500 on $25k, $1,000 on $50k, $1,500 on $100k and $2,000 on $150k. Earn2Trade publishes $550 on $25k and $1,100 on $50k. Both pause or end trading for the session rather than closing the account.

The Apex intraday model has no daily loss limit at all; risk control is enforced entirely through the trailing threshold. That is an unusual combination: the model with the more forgiving floor has no session stop, while the model with the stricter floor has one.

Read whether the limit is measured on equity or on closed balance. A floating loss of $600 has already consumed part of an equity-based allowance, while a balance-based limit ignores it until the trade closes. That difference is worth a full risk unit on a single position, so it should be confirmed before the first trade.

Monthly against thirty days

Earn2Trade's Gauntlet Mini is priced from $170 a month and the Trader Career Path from $150 a month. The subscription is an evaluation cost, not a permanent one: the firm lists "no monthly subscription" and "no subscription fee" on its funded LiveSim and live accounts. A trader who passes in six weeks pays for two months; one who takes five months pays for five.

Apex is the opposite shape. The evaluation is a one-time payment for 30 days of access with no automatic renewal, and the Performance Account activation fee is also one-time. A trader who passes in six weeks pays once; a trader who runs out of days pays again for a new evaluation.

That makes the cost comparison depend on expected duration and on the probability of needing a retry. At $170 a month, three months at Earn2Trade costs $510; at Apex, the equivalent is one evaluation fee plus one activation fee if the pass happens inside 30 days. The two are close in the median case and diverge at the tails, which is why the decision should be made on the rulebook rather than on the headline price.

Data costs sit on top of both. Futures data is usually billed by the platform or data provider separately from the evaluation, and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Add it to the monthly budget on either side.

Contract sizing on either

Both accounts trade the same CME contracts, so the tick maths carries over unchanged. A Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract is $2 per tick, which makes a 15-tick stop a $30 risk per contract. The E-mini S&P is $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop is $100. The Micro E-mini Dow is $0.50 per tick, so a 20-tick stop is $10.

With a $200 unit, the Nasdaq micro allows six contracts, the S&P mini allows two, and the Dow micro allows twenty. A factor of ten between the smallest and largest count for the same dollar risk is why sizing from contracts rather than from dollars is the most common error on a futures account.

Check the position cap before the risk budget. Apex's evaluation caps are fixed at 4 contracts on $25k, 6 on $50k, 8 on $100k and 12 on $150k. Earn2Trade's Gauntlet Mini allows up to 6 contracts on its $50,000 account and follows a progression ladder as the balance grows. A size that fits the dollar budget and breaches the cap is a rule violation, not a size.

Leave room for slip. A stop becomes a market order when triggered, and three ticks of slippage on a 15-tick stop is a 20% overrun on the planned risk. On Apex's intraday model that overrun lands against a floor that has already recorded the high, so the buffer absorbs it twice.

Minimum days and the calendar

Neither firm requires a minimum number of trading days. Apex says so on both evaluation pages and allows a pass in a single session. Earn2Trade states the same, with the practical caveat that its 30% consistency rule usually requires at least four profitable days to satisfy. The calendar is therefore a deadline or a billing period, not a quota.

That distinction matters for pacing. On Apex, the 30-day window is a hard deadline: an unfinished evaluation expires and a new purchase is needed. On Earn2Trade, the monthly billing means a slow attempt costs money rather than expiring, so the trader can choose patience and pay for it.

The correct plan counts the required sessions, maps them against availability, and spreads the target across them. On Earn2Trade the spread is mandatory; on Apex it is a choice that reduces variance. Either way, the plan should assume at least one losing week and should not require a perfect sequence to finish.

The shared platform stack

Both firms list Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader among their supported platforms, so the workflow question largely disappears. A trader can move between the products without changing order entry, and a trader who has not used any of them faces the same learning curve on either side.

The choice within the list is about style. Tradovate is the lightest and fastest to learn, with a clean DOM. Rithmic is a data and routing layer valued for depth of market and execution speed. NinjaTrader is the programmable option with the largest ecosystem and the most complete order log.

Data fees are the remaining variable. Futures data is usually billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Confirm which feed the plan includes and which one the method needs, and add it to the budget rather than discovering it at the first statement.

Payout conditions

Apex applies a 50% best-day consistency rule at payout on current accounts, with 30% retained on legacy Performance Accounts. A payout request that fails the rule is refused rather than breaching the account, so the fix is more ordinary winning days before requesting again. The 50% figure is the one to plan for.

Earn2Trade pays weekly. Its published split is 50% on profit below $1,500 and 80% above $1,500 on the Gauntlet Mini's $50,000 account, with the thresholds scaled by account size. The 30% consistency rule applies during the evaluation and is lifted once the account is funded, so a funded trader does not face the same constraint at withdrawal.

Neither firm releases profit without reviewing the record. A journal that shows the buffer at the start and end of every session answers most of the questions in advance, and the gross-versus-net split question is worth asking before the first payout rather than after it.

Retry maths over twelve months

Estimate the pass probability from your own record and divide the annual cost by it. At a 30% pass probability, three attempts per funded account is a reasonable planning assumption. On Earn2Trade, a retry is another run of monthly fees; on Apex, it is another one-time fee.

The lever that improves both numbers is a smaller unit. Cutting the unit by a third usually turns a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown, and the fee is unchanged, so the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

The second lever is the plan variant. On the Apex side the question is EOD or intraday; on the Earn2Trade side it is Gauntlet Mini or Trader Career Path, which have different targets and progression ladders. Choosing the variant that matches the method's holding period is worth more than optimising the fee.

Releases and the overnight session

Both products trade US futures, so both face the same event calendar: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment reports and, for some instruments, inventory data. Both firms permit news trading according to their published rules, but the conduct clauses are enforced at review and are worth reading before the first release.

Being flat into the release and re-entering after the first half hour avoids the whole category. A trader who chooses to hold should cut the position and size the stop for a gap, not for normal volatility; on an intraday trail the gap can raise the floor at the same moment it takes the loss.

Overnight is the other scheduled risk: thinner liquidity, wider spreads, and a settlement that can change margin without a trade. On Earn2Trade's EOD evaluation the settlement is also when the threshold recalculates, so whatever is held through it decides tomorrow's buffer and tomorrow's size.

Which futures trader belongs on which

Apex fits a trader who wants a one-time 30-day attempt, a choice between a fixed daily limit and a pure trail, and account sizes up to $300,000. The EOD model suits a trader who holds through intraday retracements; the intraday model suits a trader who takes profit at levels and closes within the session.

Earn2Trade fits a trader who wants a settled EOD floor during the evaluation, a defined progression ladder afterwards, and a monthly cost that stops at funding. The 30% consistency rule makes it a better fit for a trader who already produces several profitable days a week than for one who relies on a single large session.

The tie-breaker is the concentration of the method. A trader whose profit comes in one or two big days will find the Earn2Trade evaluation rule binding and the Apex payout rule binding later. Neither is unworkable; both require the plan to include ordinary winning days.

What to check before paying

The first is the drawdown model and whether it includes open profit. The second is the daily limit and its basis. The third is the contract cap at the account size being bought.

The fourth is the billing structure: a one-time 30-day purchase at Apex, a monthly subscription at Earn2Trade. The fifth is the consistency stage and threshold. The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, automation and third-party trading. Each is short, each is enforced, and none is reliably on the comparison page.

Keep a dated copy of each answer. Ten minutes of screenshots is the only evidence that survives a terms revision, and it is what a dispute six weeks later will turn on.

A thirty-day plan for either

Use the first week to calibrate at half size. Log the buffer before each entry, watch whether the threshold moves on open or closed profit, and note the commission and slip actually paid on each round turn. By Friday the method should have a cost sheet as well as a P&L.

Weeks two and three are the working block: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losses. Recalculate the buffer each morning and cut the unit once it falls below a twelfth of the starting threshold. This is where an intraday trail starts to compress and where the EOD model feels more forgiving.

Week four is consolidation: a third of the unit, the result protected, and the payout file assembled. Pressing after the target is met is the classic way to hand back a finished attempt.

Three numbers stay on the desk for the whole attempt: buffer, unit and session cap. Apex and Earn2Trade move the first one on different schedules, and neither protects a trader who stops checking it once the account is green.

Common questions

Is Apex a monthly fee?

No. A current Apex evaluation is a one-time payment for 30 days of access with no automatic renewal, and the Performance Account activation fee is also one-time. Legacy subscription accounts are a different product.

Is Earn2Trade a subscription?

Yes during the evaluation: GAU50 starts at $170 a month and TCP25 at $150 a month. The subscription stops once the account is funded, and the firm lists no monthly fee on its LiveSim and live accounts.

Which consistency rule is stricter?

They apply at different stages. Earn2Trade's 30% rule constrains how the evaluation target is built and lifts when funded. Apex's 50% rule applies to every payout request. A concentrated method will feel the first during the pass and the second afterwards.

Are the platforms the same?

Both list Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader among their supported platforms, so the workflow does not change. Data fees may still differ, and they are billed separately from the evaluation on both.

Can either be passed in a week?

Apex can be passed in a single session, because it has no minimum-day rule. Earn2Trade has no minimum-day rule either, but its 30% consistency rule usually requires at least four profitable days to satisfy.
