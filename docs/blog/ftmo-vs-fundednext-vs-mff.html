FTMO vs FundedNext vs My Funded Futures: three rulebooks compared

These three names appear in the same comparison tables, which hides the fact that they are not substitutes. FTMO and FundedNext both sell forex and CFD evaluations, but one measures a static percentage floor and the other sells a menu of one-step and two-phase plans. My Funded Futures sells a futures evaluation with an end-of-day trailing threshold, priced as a one-time fee on its Builder plan and as a subscription on other plans. The right way to use this page is to eliminate two options, not to rank three. The figures below describe published rulebook shapes; confirm the current plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Two of the three evaluate the same market. That makes the first comparison a like-for-like one and the third option a different product category.
  • FTMO's floor is fixed at the starting balance; FundedNext's depends on the plan you buy; My Funded Futures trails peak equity.
  • The billing rhythm splits the group: one-off on the forex side, monthly on the futures side.
  • Platform stacks barely overlap, so the choice of front end is a real constraint rather than a preference.
  • Eliminate by market and session first, floor mechanic second, billing and payout third.

Read the market row first. Two of the three products are forex and CFD evaluations, which means they are genuine alternatives for the same trader and the same method. The third is a futures programme, which is a different instrument, a different session and a different sizing unit. A table that puts all three in one row invites a comparison that cannot be made.

The first comparison is between the two forex plans

FTMO and FundedNext both sell access to the same underlying market, often through the same platforms, with the same weekday session. For a forex trader, the choice between them is a choice between two floor mechanics and two fee structures, not between two markets. That is the cleanest comparison on this page, and it should be settled first.

The essential difference is that FTMO's failure point is fixed at the start and FundedNext's depends on the plan. The two-phase FundedNext model is commonly static, which makes it close to the FTMO structure. The one-step variants are tighter and sometimes trailing, which makes them a different product with the same brand on the checkout page.

The second difference is the path. FTMO runs two phases and a verification stage, so the trader has to reproduce the result three times. FundedNext's one-step model asks for one target, and its two-phase model asks for two. Fewer stages means fewer attempts at the same process, which is either a saving or a loss depending on whether the process is repeatable.

A menu against a single structure

A menu is a feature when the trader knows which structure fits. A one-step plan is faster and demands a tighter floor; a two-phase plan is slower and generally gives more room. A trader with a high win rate and a shallow drawdown can take the fast route; a trader with a lower win rate and deeper retracements needs the slower one. FundedNext lets that choice be made at checkout.

The cost of the menu is administrative. Two plans sold under one brand do not share one rulebook, and support answers can be written for the other plan. Every rule that matters, from the floor to the consistency threshold, has to be confirmed for the specific plan name. A trader who reads "FundedNext rules" on a review site is reading an average of several products.

FTMO's single structure removes that ambiguity at the cost of flexibility. There is one path to learn and one rule set to verify. For a trader who intends to repeat the attempt several times, that consistency is worth more than the option to choose a faster plan, because the process is the thing being practised.

Three floors, one equity curve

Imagine the same equity curve applied to all three products: a $100,000 account that gains $4,000, gives back $3,000, gains $6,000, then loses $2,500. On the static floor the failure point never moves, so the account ends with a cushion and no breach. On a trailing floor the peak after the $10,000 gain sets a new high-water mark, and the subsequent $2,500 loss reduces the room from that peak rather than from the start.

That is the whole argument in one example. The static floor measures survival from the starting line; the trailing floor measures survival from the best moment. A trader whose equity curve is jagged with a rising trend will do better on the static version, because the give-backs do not tighten the floor. A trader whose curve rises smoothly and rarely retraces will do better on the trailing version, because the floor follows them up and the room stays constant.

The practical test is to take your last fifty trades and compute the maximum drawdown from a running peak, not from the start. If that number is close to your total loss floor, a trailing product will end the account before the static one does. If it is small relative to the floor, the two products behave similarly and the choice should be made on cost and platform.

A worked $100,000 on each

On FTMO, a 10% floor puts the failure point at $90,000. A $500 risk unit buys twenty losses from the start and twenty losses after a $5,000 gain. The account can be traded with a fixed size and reviewed weekly. The target takes as long as it takes, and the floor does not punish a good month.

On the FundedNext two-phase model, the floor is also commonly static, so the arithmetic is similar. On a one-step variant with a tighter floor, the number of losses falls to twelve, and a trailing variant reduces it further after each new high. The trader has to recalculate at the start of each session, which is a small habit with a large effect.

On My Funded Futures, a $3,000 trailing threshold gives six losses at $500. After a $1,500 gain the floor moves up and the remaining room from the original balance is $1,500, or three losses at the old size. Cutting the unit to $250 restores six. That recalculation is not optional on this product; it is the difference between an account that survives a normal week and one that does not.

The monthly fee adds a variable the forex products do not have. A three-month attempt costs three times the monthly rate, so the effective cost per attempt rises with the time taken. A trader who needs six months pays for six, which can make the one-off products cheaper even at a higher sticker price.

Daily stops and their measurement basis

Each product applies a daily loss limit, and each states it slightly differently. The basis matters more than the number: a limit measured on equity counts open losses immediately, while a limit measured on balance counts only closed results. The first punishes an open position that is under water; the second lets it sit until it is closed, which is either a grace period or a trap depending on the position.

The daily limit also interacts with the floor. On a static account, a bad day consumes cushion but does not move the failure point. On a trailing account, a bad day can consume both the daily allowance and the room above the floor. Two bad days in a row on a trailing product can leave an account alive but unable to trade its normal size, which is the point at which most traders make the mistake of doubling up.

The rule to adopt is the same on all three: cap any single trade at one third of the daily allowance, and stop after three stops in a session. That rule prevents the daily limit from ever being the cause of a breach, and it keeps the next session tradeable.

One-off against monthly, priced honestly

FTMO and FundedNext charge once per attempt on the plans compared here. My Funded Futures now sells its Builder plan as a one-time payment, with other plans priced as a subscription, so the billing model has to be confirmed for the exact plan. A sticker-price comparison between a one-off fee and a monthly rate is meaningless until the monthly rate is multiplied by the expected number of months.

Assume a trader expects the attempt to take two months and a second attempt to take another two. The one-off product costs two fees; the monthly product costs four monthly payments. If the monthly rate is a third of the one-off fee, the monthly product is still more expensive over that path. If the attempt takes three weeks, the monthly product can be cheaper.

The second hidden cost is the platform. Futures front ends can carry data or software charges, and forex platforms sometimes charge for premium tools. Add those to the monthly side, because they recur with the subscription rather than with the attempt. The comparison should be made on total cost to first payout, not on the number in the plan name.

Platform stacks and switching cost

FTMO and FundedNext share the MetaTrader and cTrader ecosystem, with FTMO adding DXtrade. My Funded Futures uses Tradovate, Rithmic, NinjaTrader and TradingView. The overlap is close to zero, so the choice of product is partly a choice of front end, and the switching cost is real.

For a trader with an MT5 workflow, moving to a futures platform means relearning hotkeys, order handling and the DOM, and possibly rewriting an automation. That cost is usually larger than the fee difference between the products. For a trader already on NinjaTrader, the reverse is true, and the futures product is the natural fit regardless of its monthly fee.

Automation deserves a separate check. MetaTrader expert advisors and cTrader cBots do not run on NinjaTrader, and NinjaTrader strategies do not run on MetaTrader. If the method is automated, the platform decision is effectively made before the comparison starts. If the method is discretionary, the platform is a comfort issue rather than a hard constraint.

Session fit decides more than fee

The forex products trade from Sunday evening to Friday evening, with liquidity concentrated in London and the New York overlap. The futures product trades on US session hours with a daily settlement. A trader who can only watch the Asian session can trade forex but not US futures. A trader who works during the US day can trade futures but may miss the forex session that suits their method.

This is the constraint that eliminates products fastest, and it is the one traders most often ignore because it does not appear in the fee table. Mark the hours you can actually be at the screen for the next month. If the liquid session of a product falls outside those hours, the product is not a candidate, however good its floor.

Holding period matters too. Futures positions face a daily settlement and a margin recalculation; forex positions face rollover and weekend gaps. A method that holds for days fits forex more naturally, and a method that opens and closes within a session fits either, subject to the session you can attend.

Payout cycles and the review

All three firms release profit after a review, and all three define the review in terms broader than the arithmetic. The split percentage is the number traders quote; the cycle and the conditions are the numbers that determine when money arrives.

FTMO's first payout follows a fixed seasoning period, with subsequent withdrawals on a shorter schedule. FundedNext releases profit share on a cycle after the first payout. My Funded Futures pays on a fixed cycle after qualification. In each case, the time from a profitable month to cleared funds is longer than the cycle alone, because verification and conduct review sit in between.

Complete identity and payment verification before the first payout request. Keep a journal that records the setup, the risk, the stop and the result for every trade, and keep the support correspondence. A review is an evidence exercise, and a trader with evidence answers it in an hour.

Where My Funded Futures changes the ranking

My Funded Futures matters in this comparison for one reason: it is the only futures product among the three. If the trader can work US hours and wants a futures evaluation, it is not competing with the other two at all. If the trader cannot work US hours, it is eliminated before its floor is considered.

Its trailing threshold and monthly fee make it a specific fit rather than a general one. The trailing mechanic suits a trader who closes positions at the end of the session and does not want open profit to tighten the floor mid-trade. The monthly fee suits a trader who works intensively and finishes quickly. Neither feature is better or worse than the forex alternatives; they are answers to different questions.

The third product also changes the decision by making the platform question concrete. A trader who wants TradingView execution and NinjaTrader-style depth is choosing a workflow that the forex products cannot offer. Once that preference is established, the comparison collapses to a single candidate.

Which trader should choose which

Choose FTMO if you want a static percentage floor, the widest forex platform list and a conventional multi-stage path. It fits a trader whose method produces deep but recoverable retracements, because the failure point does not move while the account is under water.

Choose FundedNext if you want to choose your structure. The one-step plan suits a trader with a high win rate and a tight drawdown; the two-phase plan suits a trader who needs room. Confirm which floor attaches to the plan before paying, because the brand name does not settle it.

Choose My Funded Futures if you trade futures on US hours and want a trailing evaluation with broad platform support. It fits a trader who closes at the session and can finish an attempt inside a few months, since the monthly fee rewards speed.

If none of those descriptions fits, the honest answer is to wait. A trader who cannot describe their own drawdown profile should not be buying a floor they have not measured against it.

Reset policy and the retry path

A reset is a second chance on the same account, usually at a discount and sometimes within a time limit. The policy matters because it changes the cost of a failed attempt, and the three products structure it differently. A generous reset makes a trailing floor less expensive in practice, because the first breach is cheap to recover from.

Ask three questions before buying. Does a reset restart the clock and the floor, or only the balance? Is the reset price fixed or a percentage of the original fee? Is there a limit on the number of resets, and does a reset preserve the trading history that a payout review might need?

The answers change the retry calculation. If a reset costs half the fee and restarts the floor completely, the effective cost of a second attempt is lower than the sticker suggests. If it only tops up the balance while leaving the peak equity in place, a trailing product may not give back the room the trader needs.

Treat the reset policy as part of the product, not as a discount to be discovered later. A trader choosing between a static floor with no reset and a trailing floor with a cheap reset is choosing between two different cost curves, and the cheaper-looking product is not always the one with the lower expected cost.

What the dashboard tells you

The dashboard is where a rulebook becomes operational. It should show the current balance, the equity, the remaining room to the floor, the day's loss against the daily limit, and the progress against the target. A dashboard that shows only profit is a marketing page; one that shows the remaining room is a risk tool.

On a trailing account, the dashboard must show the peak equity that the floor is measured against. Without it, the trader is guessing where the failure point sits, and guessing is how a normal week becomes a breach. Ask for a screenshot of the dashboard before buying, and check that the peak figure is visible.

On a multi-stage product, the dashboard should separate the stages and show which rules apply to the current one. A trader who carries a rule from phase one into verification because the dashboard did not distinguish them is making an expensive mistake with no warning.

The payout statement is the second operational document. It should show the gross profit, the split, the deductions and the net amount, with the cycle dates. A statement that shows only a net number cannot be reconciled, and reconciliation is the trader's only defence if a deduction is disputed.

The elimination order that works

Step one: eliminate by market and session. If you cannot trade US hours, the futures product is out. If you cannot trade forex hours, both forex products are out. This step usually leaves one product, and the rest of the comparison becomes documentation.

Step two: eliminate by floor mechanic. Take your fifty-trade history, compute the maximum drawdown from a running peak, and compare it with each floor. A product whose floor would have been breached is not a candidate, however attractive its fee.

Step three: price the path. Multiply the fee by the expected number of attempts, multiply the monthly rate by the expected number of months, and add the platform cost. Compare the total with a realistic first payout.

Step four: verify the operational details. Support response, dashboard clarity, reset policy, payout statement. These are the parts of the product you use after the pass, and they are the parts no comparison table scores.

Three myths about three-way comparisons

"The cheapest of the three is the best value." Value is the fee divided by the probability of a payout. The cheapest product with a floor that rejects your method has the worst value of the three.

"They all evaluate the same thing." Two evaluate forex and one evaluates futures. Even the two forex products measure different floors on different plans. The shared vocabulary hides the difference.

"A three-way comparison is more objective." It is longer, not more objective. The third column adds a product that most traders cannot use, and it makes the table look balanced while the real decision is between two plans and one market.

Common questions

Are FTMO and FundedNext the same product?

No. Both evaluate forex and CFD accounts, but FTMO runs a fixed multi-stage path on a static floor, while FundedNext sells a menu of one-step and two-phase plans with different floors.

Does My Funded Futures evaluate forex?

No. It evaluates futures accounts on US session hours, which is why its platform list and its billing rhythm differ from the two forex products.

Which firm has the easiest loss floor?

A static percentage floor is usually easier for a method with deep retracements, because the failure point does not move. Trailing floors are easier for a method with a smooth rising curve. There is no universal answer.

Is a monthly fee always more expensive?

No. A monthly product can be cheaper if the attempt finishes quickly. It becomes more expensive when the attempt drags, because the fee keeps accruing while the account is open.

What should I check before buying any of the three?

The floor type, the daily stop basis, the consistency threshold, the maximum position size, the session hours and the payout cycle. All six appear in the terms, and all six change the plan.
