Cheapest prop firm challenge 2026: the cost after the discount

Every firm on this page advertises a sale. That is the least useful number in the decision. What decides whether a challenge was cheap is the fee divided by the probability of passing, plus everything the fee does not cover: commissions, resets, currency conversion and the months of subscription that a slow attempt consumes. This guide prices an attempt properly, then ranks nine plans by what a funded account actually costs.

Key takeaways

  • The entry fee is a small part of the cost. The expected number of attempts is the multiplier that matters.
  • A one-off fee rewards patience; a monthly subscription punishes it. The same trader can pay double on the wrong billing model.
  • Discount depth is usually a function of how long the firm has been selling that plan, not how good it is.
  • Commission and spread are charged per trade, so a high-frequency method pays the fee again in running costs.
  • A cheap challenge attached to a rulebook you cannot trade is the most expensive purchase on the page.

What "cheapest" should mean

A challenge fee buys one attempt at a rulebook. It does not buy a funded account, and it does not buy the right to keep the fee if the attempt fails. The honest unit of comparison is therefore cost per funded account: the fee multiplied by the number of attempts a trader of a given skill level expects to need, plus running costs, plus the value of the time the attempt consumes.

That definition changes the ranking immediately. A $99 challenge with a trailing floor and a consistency clause can cost more per funded account than a $540 challenge with a static floor, because the cheaper product rejects a larger share of the traders who buy it. The fee is visible at checkout; the rejection rate is not, which is exactly why the fee dominates the marketing.

There is a second reason to price the attempt rather than the entry. Most firms run permanent or near-permanent promotions, so the list price is rarely the paid price. A trader who compares list prices is comparing numbers that nobody pays. A trader who compares the cost of a realistic path — first attempt, one reset, one retry — is comparing something that will actually happen.

The practical version is a single sentence to write down before buying: "This plan costs me X dollars per attempt, I expect to need Y attempts, and my running costs are Z dollars per hundred trades." If those three numbers cannot be filled in, the comparison is not ready to be made.

Two columns carry most of the weight. A static floor means the failure point stays where it started, so profit becomes additional room and a position can be held through a retracement. A trailing floor follows the equity high, so open profit raises the failure point and a give-back can end an account that is still in profit overall. That is not a defect in the product; it is a different product, and it costs a different amount in failed attempts for any method that holds through pullbacks.

The billing column is the second multiplier. A one-off fee is paid once per attempt regardless of how long the attempt takes. A monthly subscription is paid for every month the account remains open, which means the cost of a slow pass rises with the calendar rather than with the number of attempts. A trader who expects to take ten weeks should price ten weeks of subscription, not one month.

The arithmetic of an attempt

Start with a representative case: a $100,000 account, a fee of $540 at the advertised discount, and a trader who estimates a 55% chance of passing this rulebook on a given attempt. The probability of needing more than one attempt is 45%, of needing more than two is 20%, and the expected number of attempts is 1 divided by 0.55, or 1.82. Expected fee cost is $540 × 1.82, which is $983.

Now change one variable. The same trader on a rulebook with a trailing floor and a five-day minimum estimates 40%. Expected attempts rise to 2.5 and expected fee cost to $1,350. The two plans may sit $20 apart on the pricing page and $367 apart in expectation. That difference, not the discount, is what a buyer is choosing between.

Running costs belong in the same sum. A scalper taking forty trades a week for ten weeks takes four hundred trades. At $4 commission per round turn on a mini contract, that is $1,600 of running cost, three times the fee. At a spread cost of half a pip on a standard lot, a day trader taking six trades a day for fifty days pays another $1,500 in spread. For high-frequency methods the fee is not the cost of the attempt; it is the entry ticket to the costs.

Then there is the cost of the account size itself. A $10,000 account with a $60 fee is cheaper in dollars than a $100,000 account with a $540 fee, but the small account earns a tenth as much per successful trade. If the objective is to withdraw a specific amount, the small account requires ten times the percentage gain or ten times the time. Cost per dollar of payout is the comparison that survives contact with a real income target.

Finally, put a value on the time. An attempt that takes three months at one hour a day is ninety hours. If the expected profit from the funded account is modest, the hourly return can be below the wage the trader could earn elsewhere. That is not an argument against trading; it is an argument for buying the plan that shortens the path when the fee difference is small.

How discount cycles actually work

Prop firm pricing is a promotional market. Most firms run a standing discount that rotates between 30% and 80%, punctuated by countdown offers tied to events, holidays or the launch of a new plan. The countdown is a conversion device: it converts a considered purchase into an urgent one, and urgency is the enemy of reading a rulebook.

The mechanical point is that a discount applies to the price, not to the rulebook. A plan bought at 80% off carries the same loss floor, the same daily stop, the same consistency clause and the same payout conditions as the full-price version. If the rulebook does not fit, the discount has reduced the cost of finding that out, which is worth something, but it has not made the plan suitable.

The second mechanical point is plan identity. Firms often run several plans under one brand with different floors, targets, minimum days and payout cycles, and the discount may apply to only one of them. A trader who compares "FTMO at 50% off" with "Funding Pips at 70% off" may be comparing a two-phase standard plan with a promotional one-step variant that carries a tighter trailing floor. The plan name, not the brand, is the unit of comparison.

The third point is the base price. A discount from a list price that is never charged is not a discount. The way to test it is to record the price of the same account size at three points across a month; if the list price resets after every promotion, the sale is the normal price with a deadline attached. That is not dishonest, but it is not information either.

What a buyer should do with a discount is narrow. If the plan already fits, buy it during a promotion and save the difference. If the plan does not fit, no percentage makes it fit. The discount is a reason to choose the timing of a purchase, never a reason to choose the purchase.

Costs the fee does not cover

Commission is the first. Futures evaluations charge per contract per side, and the schedule varies by firm and by platform. Four dollars per round turn on a $100,000 futures account sounds trivial until a scalper takes four hundred trades, at which point it is $1,600 and larger than the challenge fee. Forex accounts usually fold commission into the spread instead, which hides it in the fill rather than the statement.

Spread is the second. It is charged on entry and exit whether or not the trade works, and it widens around the London open, the New York open and scheduled releases. A method with a four-pip target and a two-pip spread is paying half its gross edge to the venue. On an evaluation with a fixed target, that difference is the difference between finishing in six weeks and finishing in twelve.

Resets are the third. Some firms sell a discounted reset rather than a full new attempt; others treat a reset as a new purchase with no discount at all. The reset price belongs in the expected-cost calculation as a separate line, because a trader who resets twice has effectively bought a subscription whether or not the plan advertises one.

Currency conversion is the fourth. Fees are usually quoted in dollars, and a card issued in another currency pays a conversion margin plus a possible foreign transaction fee. On a $500 purchase that can add three to five percent, which erases a small promotional discount entirely. Paying in the quoted currency where the card allows it is the cheapest fix.

The fifth is the cost of a missed payout condition. A plan with a consistency clause can produce a funded account that pays less than expected because one day was too large a share of the profit. The cost is not a fee, but it is real, and it belongs in the comparison between a cheap plan with a clause and a more expensive plan without one.

FTMO

FTMO sits at the top of this list for a reason that has little to do with price. Its rulebook is the reference implementation: a static floor measured from the starting balance, an equity-based daily stop, two evaluation phases and a verification stage, one fee per attempt and a first payout after a seasoning period. The mechanics are conservative and widely documented, which reduces the chance that a buyer is surprised by a clause in month two.

On cost, the fee band is mid-market rather than cheap. What makes it competitive is the expected-attempt multiplier: a rulebook that traders already understand produces fewer avoidable failures than an unfamiliar one. A trader who has passed a similar evaluation before is buying a known quantity, and known quantities are cheaper in expectation than their sticker price suggests.

The one-off billing is the other cost advantage. A slow attempt does not generate additional charges, so a trader who needs twelve weeks pays the same as one who needs four. For a patient method with a modest edge, that is worth more than a 20% larger discount elsewhere. The trade-off is that a failed attempt costs the full fee again, with no reset discount assumed in the published structure.

Where FTMO is expensive is in the funded phase for a trader who needs frequent withdrawals. A fixed seasoning period before the first payout delays income, and delay has a cost for anyone trading to a monthly budget. That cost does not appear on the pricing page and should be priced explicitly by anyone whose plan depends on the first payout arriving in a particular month.

Funding Pips

Funding Pips is the low-friction version of the same idea: a static floor, a two-phase path and a one-off fee, priced aggressively for small and mid-size accounts. The appeal is that the arithmetic is legible. A trader can see the target, see the floor, divide the room by the number of losses they expect to absorb, and know the position size before paying.

Its cost advantage is largest at the bottom of the account ladder. Small accounts carry small fees, and a small fee on a static floor is the cheapest way to test whether a method survives a rulebook at all. The same trader who would hesitate to spend $540 on a first attempt can spend a fraction of that, learn whether the process holds, and scale afterwards.

The caution is scale. A small account with a fixed target produces a small dollar payout, and the fixed costs of trading do not scale down with it. Commission per trade is the same on a $10,000 account as on a $100,000 one, so the running cost as a share of profit is ten times higher. Cheap at entry is not the same as cheap per dollar earned.

The second caution is promotional plan drift. When a firm sells several plan generations at once, the cheapest visible price may belong to an older plan with different payout terms. Check which plan the discount applies to and whether its payout cycle matches the current standard. A legacy plan can be a genuine bargain or a quiet downgrade, and only the terms page distinguishes them.

AquaFunded

AquaFunded sells both one-step and two-phase structures under one brand, which makes it useful for pricing the cost of speed. The one-step plan shortens the path to a funded account but carries a tighter effective margin because there is no second phase in which to recover from a slow start. The two-phase plan is slower and more forgiving per phase.

The published loss floor is trailing on the plans that carry the AquaFunded name in this comparison, which changes the cost calculation more than the fee does. A trailing floor raises the failure point as equity rises, so a method that gives back a third of its open profit is charged for that behaviour on every trade. The same method on a static floor would pay nothing for the give-back.

That does not make the product expensive; it makes it expensive for a particular style. A trader who takes profit at a fixed multiple and rarely lets a position run has little exposure to the trailing mechanic. A trader who scales out over hundreds of pips is exposed on every winner, and should price the product as though the fee were higher than advertised.

For a buyer comparing the two structures inside one brand, the useful exercise is to price both against the same method. If the one-step plan requires a larger position to hit its target in the same time, the risk per trade rises and the probability of a breach rises with it. The cheaper-looking path can be the more expensive one once that feedback loop is included.

Glow Node

Glow Node is a smaller forex and CFD evaluator with a static floor and a one-off fee. Its position on this list comes from price rather than from documentation: the fee band is low, the structure is simple, and the rulebook is short enough to read in a sitting.

The trade-off is verifiability. Smaller firms publish less third-party evidence about payout timing, dispute handling and rule enforcement, so a buyer is relying more heavily on the terms page and on the firm's own statements. That is not a reason to avoid the product; it is a reason to read the payout clause twice and to keep a dated copy of the terms.

On cost, the absence of a subscription is the main advantage. A static floor plus one-off billing gives a patient trader a fixed budget for the attempt, which is the property that makes a cheap plan genuinely cheap. If the attempt takes three months, the cost is the same as if it took three weeks.

The main risk to the cost calculation is a rule change between purchase and payout. Smaller firms revise terms more often than large ones, and a revised payout schedule can turn a cheap attempt into a long wait. Check the terms version date at checkout and compare it with the version published when the plan was bought.

OFP Funding

OFP Funding competes on the entry price of a first attempt. A static floor and a one-off fee keep the structure legible, and the low end of the fee band makes it one of the least expensive ways to test a forex method against a published rulebook.

The arithmetic that matters here is the ratio between the fee and the drawdown allowance. A $40 fee on an account with a $5,000 floor is cheap in absolute terms, but if the target requires a position size that puts the daily stop within one normal day's range, the expected number of attempts rises sharply. Price per attempt is only meaningful when paired with the probability that the attempt survives its first week.

Documentation is the caution. A smaller firm with limited public material leaves the buyer to verify payout timing, platform access and rule enforcement directly. That is manageable, but it should be done before payment rather than after a breach, and the answers should be saved alongside the receipt.

For a trader with a small bankroll and a simple method, the combination of a low fee and a static floor is the most defensible cheap purchase on this list. For a trader who needs a large account or a fast payout, the same product is the wrong shape at any price.

Funded Trading Plus

Funded Trading Plus offers a conventional two-phase evaluation with a static floor, priced in the middle of the market. Its cost profile is unremarkable in a good way: no exotic mechanic, no trailing floor on the standard plan, and a fee that is easy to compare with the rest of the list.

The value of an unremarkable rulebook is that it is easy to price. A trader who has traded a static two-phase evaluation before can estimate their pass rate from their own record rather than from a marketing claim. That estimate is the largest single input into expected cost, and anything that makes it more reliable reduces the true cost of the attempt.

The weakness is differentiation. When the mechanics match several competitors, the decision comes down to price, platform access and payout reliability, and those are the three things a buyer can verify least easily from a landing page. Ask for the payout schedule in writing, confirm the platform list, and compare the total fee after any mandatory add-ons.

On billing, the one-off structure keeps a slow attempt affordable. The cost that does rise with time is opportunity: a two-phase path with a minimum-day requirement cannot be compressed, so a trader who needs a funded account by a particular date should check the minimum days before assuming the fee is the whole cost.

FundedPrime

FundedPrime sells several plan structures, which is both its appeal and its cost trap. The cheapest plan in the family may carry a trailing floor while a more expensive sibling carries a static one, so a buyer who sorts by price can end up with the structure they were trying to avoid.

The correct way to compare inside a multi-plan firm is to fix the account size and the loss-floor type first, then compare fees. That removes the plans that are cheap for the wrong reason and leaves a like-for-like set. It also exposes the add-ons: a low headline fee with a paid reset and a paid platform upgrade is not the same product as a slightly higher fee that includes both.

The trailing floor on the cheaper plans raises expected cost for any method that holds through retracements. A trader should price that explicitly by asking how often a typical winner gives back more than a third of its open profit. If the answer is often, the trailing plan is the expensive one regardless of the fee.

Documentation is thinner than at the largest firms, so the payout clause deserves a careful read. Confirm the profit split, the minimum withdrawal, the processing time and whether the first payout is subject to a separate condition. Those four numbers determine what the funded account is worth, and they are not always on the same page as the price.

Karma Prop Firm

Karma Prop Firm appears on cost-led lists because its entry prices are low and its structure is simple. A static floor and a one-off fee are the right ingredients for a cheap first attempt, provided the counterparty checks out.

That proviso is doing real work. Smaller firms carry more counterparty risk: the risk that payout terms change, that support becomes slow, or that the firm's commercial model does not survive long enough to pay a funded trader. None of that is visible in a fee comparison, and all of it belongs in the expected cost as a probability-weighted loss.

The practical checks are simple. Look for a registered company name and jurisdiction, a published terms version with a date, a payout policy that states processing times, and evidence of payouts from traders who are not affiliated with the firm. If two of those are missing, the low fee is buying uncertainty rather than value.

For a trader who has done those checks, the product is a reasonable low-cost way to test a method. For a trader who has not, the cheapest plan on the internet is the one most likely to cost the most in the end.

FunderPro

FunderPro sells one-step and multi-phase options with a trailing floor on the plans that appear in this comparison. The one-step product is the cheaper route to a funded account in time, and the more expensive one in expected attempts, because there is no second phase in which to absorb a bad start.

That trade-off is the whole cost story. A one-step plan compresses the target into a single attempt, so the risk per trade has to be higher to reach the target in a reasonable window, and a higher risk per trade lowers the probability of surviving the floor. The fee may be lower; the expected number of purchases is not.

The trailing floor compounds the effect. On a one-step plan the account has to reach the target without giving back enough open profit to breach the moving floor, which is a narrower path than a static structure allows. A trader whose method depends on letting winners run should price this plan as the most expensive option in its family.

Where the product is competitive is platform familiarity. A trader already working inside a known rulebook family pays less in mistakes, and mistakes are the largest hidden cost in any evaluation. That is a real advantage, but it is an advantage of fit rather than of price.

The pre-purchase checklist

Before paying, confirm the loss-floor type on the exact plan being bought. Static, trailing intraday, or end-of-day trailing are three different products, and the difference is larger than any discount on the page.

Confirm the daily stop and its basis. An equity-based stop counts open positions; a balance-based stop does not. The first ends a session earlier than most traders expect, and the second allows a position to be held through a drawdown that would otherwise breach.

Confirm the minimum trading days and the maximum time limit. A plan with no minimum can be passed in a day; a plan with a ten-day minimum cannot be compressed, and on a subscription that is ten days of billing.

Confirm the payout terms: split, minimum withdrawal, processing time, first-payout condition and whether a consistency clause applies at payout rather than during the evaluation. These determine what the funded account is worth, which is the other half of the cost equation.

Confirm the platform list and the commission schedule. A platform the trader does not use is a cost in adaptation, and a commission schedule that is high relative to the target is a cost in every trade.

Finally, save the terms page as a dated file. Rules change, and the version that applied on the day of purchase is the only one that can settle a dispute later.

Questions buyers ask

Is the cheapest challenge always the worst value?

No. A low fee on a static floor with one-off billing can be excellent value for a trader with a simple method and a small bankroll. The failure case is a low fee attached to a trailing floor, a tight daily stop and a consistency clause, because those three mechanics multiply the probability of needing another attempt.

Do discount codes stack?

Rarely, and the terms usually say so. Most firms apply one promotion per purchase and exclude resets, add-ons and already-discounted plans. Assume no stacking and price the purchase at the single best available rate.

Should I buy the largest account I can afford?

Only if the method is already proven on a smaller one. A large account increases the dollar value of a mistake as well as the dollar value of a win, and the fee rises faster than the probability of passing. Buy the size that matches the risk unit the method actually uses.

Does a reset cost less than a new attempt?

Sometimes, and it is worth asking before the first attempt fails rather than after. A discounted reset changes the expected-cost arithmetic meaningfully; a full-price reset does not, and the plan should be priced as though every attempt is a new purchase.

What about firms that refund the fee on the first payout?

A refundable fee shifts cost from the attempt to the payout, which is favourable for a trader who expects to pass and unfavourable for one who does not. Read the refund condition carefully: some require a minimum number of payouts, some exclude promotional purchases, and some refund only the base fee after the discount.
