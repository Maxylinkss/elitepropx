FTMO vs FTUK: which rulebook fits your trading

FTMO and FTUK are usually compared on target size and fee, which are the two rows that decide the least. FTMO evaluates forex and CFD accounts on a static percentage floor and a two-phase path with a verification stage. FTUK evaluates futures accounts on a trailing threshold measured against peak equity. The market decides the session and the sizing unit; the floor mechanic decides how many attempts your method gets. The figures below describe each published rulebook's shape — confirm the current plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • FTMO measures its floor from the starting balance; FTUK trails it against peak equity, so profit reduces the remaining room.
  • FTMO's path includes a verification stage after the two evaluation phases; FTUK runs a single evaluation with a trailing threshold.
  • Forex sizing is lots and pips, futures sizing is contracts and ticks. A method does not transfer between them unchanged.
  • The platform lists barely overlap: MT4, MT5, cTrader and DXtrade against Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader.
  • Both bill once per attempt on the plans compared here, so retries rather than subscriptions drive the annual cost.

What each rulebook actually measures

Every evaluation is a set of measurements taken at the same time. The profit target measures progress. The total loss floor measures survival. The daily stop measures discipline inside a session. The minimum day count measures pace. The payout conditions measure whether the progress can be converted into money. A comparison that looks only at the target is looking at one of five instruments.

FTMO measures the loss floor as a percentage of the initial balance, so the failure point is fixed in dollars for the life of the account. That is a static measurement: it does not care whether the account is up or down. FTUK measures a threshold against peak equity, so the failure point moves up when the account makes a new high. That is a trailing measurement, and its behavior depends on the path the equity takes, not just the final number.

The distinction sounds technical and is not. On a static account, a good week adds room above the floor and the floor stays where it was. On a trailing account, a good week can leave the same absolute distance to the floor as before, or less, because the floor chased the equity up. Two traders with identical P&L can finish a month with different remaining room purely because of the order of their trades.

A percentage floor against a trailing threshold

Model a $100,000 account with a 10% floor. On the static version the failure point is $90,000 from day one and remains there after a $5,000 gain. The trader has $15,000 of room and a $500 risk unit buys thirty losses. That is a genuinely forgiving structure, and it is why the static model remains the reference point for forex evaluations.

On the trailing version, assume a $3,000 threshold measured against the peak. At the start the failure point is $97,000 and a $500 unit buys six losses. After a $2,000 gain the floor rises to $99,000 if the peak is measured on equity, and the room from the new peak is again $3,000 — but the room from the original balance is now $1,000, or two losses at the old size. A trader who keeps the original size is now taking triple the relative risk they started with.

The remedy is mechanical: recalculate the risk unit at the start of every session as the remaining room divided by a fixed number of losses, capped at your original unit. If the calculation gives less than five losses, halve the unit. If it gives less than three, take the day off and review. This is not conservatism for its own sake; it is the only way a trailing account survives a normal losing streak.

Two phases plus verification against one evaluation

FTMO's path runs two evaluation phases and then a verification stage before the funded account. Each phase has its own target and its own timer, and the rules apply to all of them. The structure rewards a trader who can repeat a process three times rather than a trader who can produce one exceptional month. That is a meaningful filter, and it is why the pass rate on multi-phase evaluations is low.

FTUK runs a single evaluation with a trailing threshold. One target, one floor, one decision. The shorter path means fewer fees and less calendar risk, but it also means the trailing floor is the only protection against a bad streak. On a two-phase path, a bad first phase is a reset; on a single evaluation, a bad week can end the attempt outright.

The practical difference is in planning. A three-stage path should be planned as three separate campaigns with a pause between them, because the mental load of carrying a target across months is what breaks most traders. A single-stage path should be planned as one campaign with a hard stop date, because without intermediate checkpoints there is nothing to force a review.

The daily loss rule and its basis

The daily stop is the rule that ends more attempts than any other. Both firms apply one, and both express it as a limit on the day's loss. The detail that matters is the basis: equity or balance. An equity-based limit counts open losses immediately, so a position that is $700 underwater has already consumed $700 of the day's allowance. A balance-based limit counts only closed results, which allows an open position to sit beyond the limit until it is closed.

FTMO's daily loss is commonly stated as a percentage of balance and measured on equity. FTUK's is a dollar limit on its futures programme. Neither is more generous in the abstract; the percentages and dollars are set so that a normal losing day fits and an abnormal one does not. The trader's job is to know which basis applies and to size so that a single stop cannot consume more than a fraction of the daily allowance.

A useful rule is to cap any single trade at one third of the daily allowance. That way three consecutive stops end the day rather than the account, and a fourth trade is never the one that breaches. The rule is crude, and it is far better than discovering the limit by hitting it.

Consistency and minimum trading days

Consistency rules limit how much of the profit can come from one day. Minimum day rules limit how quickly the target can be reached. Both exist to stop a trader passing on a single oversized position, and both change the optimal pace. A plan with a minimum day count rewards patient distribution; a plan without one rewards speed, at the cost of a thinner sample.

On the plans typically compared, FTMO does not apply a consistency rule to the evaluation and does not impose a hard minimum day count beyond the phase structure. FTUK applies consistency conditions within its programme. That difference matters for a trader whose method produces occasional large days: the same month can pass one rulebook and fail the other without any change in skill.

The way to test this is to take your last sixty trading days and compute the share of total profit produced by the best five. If that share is high, a consistency rule will bind and the plan needs a distribution plan before the first trade. If it is low, the rule is unlikely to affect you and the choice should be made on the floor instead.

Platform lists and what they change

FTMO offers MT4, MT5, cTrader and DXtrade. FTUK offers Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader. The overlap is essentially zero, which means the choice is partly a choice of workflow. A trader with years of MT5 templates and a tested expert advisor faces a real cost in moving to a futures front end, and that cost is usually larger than the difference in evaluation fee.

Order handling differs too. On MT4 and MT5, stops and limits are held by the broker's server, but the behaviour in a gap depends on the execution model. On futures platforms, orders rest at the exchange and are filled by the matching engine, which is a cleaner structure in fast markets. If your method depends on precise stop fills during volatility, that is a genuine advantage on the futures side, and it is not visible in a comparison table.

TradingView is the wildcard. It is a charting and analysis environment first, and its execution layer is newer than the dedicated platforms. A trader who lives in TradingView will value having it on the FTUK list; a trader who needs a mature DOM will not. Neither preference is wrong, but the platform should be chosen before the fee is compared.

Sizing a $100,000 account on each

On the forex account, size in lots. If one standard lot of a major pair is worth roughly $10 per pip and the stop is 25 pips, one lot risks about $250. With a $500 risk budget, two lots fit. The same budget on a 50-pip stop allows one lot. The arithmetic is simple, and it changes with the pair, the account currency and the spread.

On the futures account, size in contracts. If one contract of an index future is worth $5 per tick and the stop is 20 ticks, one contract risks $100. A $500 budget allows five contracts. That is a much larger position count than most traders expect, which is why futures accounts feel different on the first day: the same risk budget produces a visibly bigger position.

Both calculations assume the stop is the maximum loss. In practice slippage adds to it, particularly on forex around news and on futures at the open. Build a buffer of ten to twenty percent into the risk budget, and re-check the position limit in the rulebook. A size that fits the risk budget but exceeds the contract cap is not a size you can take.

Session maps: 24/5 against US hours

Forex trades from Sunday evening to Friday evening, with the deepest liquidity in the London and New York overlap. A trader who can only watch one session is effectively trading a narrower market, and the method should be built for that session rather than for the whole day. Positions held overnight carry rollover and gap risk, and positions held over the weekend carry the risk of a Sunday open beyond the stop.

Futures trade on a defined session with a daily settlement. The regular hours concentrate liquidity, and the overnight session is thinner. A trader who cannot be present during the regular session is trading a different market with different spreads and different behavior. The settlement also marks the account to market, which can change the margin requirement without any trade being placed.

The practical test is a calendar test. Mark the hours you can actually watch for the next four weeks, then check whether the instrument's liquid session falls inside them. If it does not, the evaluation is being traded on hope. That single check prevents more failed attempts than any strategy adjustment.

News, events and restricted windows

Both firms restrict some activity around major scheduled events. The restrictions usually cover holding through a release, opening a position within a window around it, or profiting from a price spike caused by it. The definitions matter: "around" can mean two minutes or thirty, and "profit from" can be judged on the individual trade or on the account.

Forex news risk is concentrated in central bank decisions and employment data, and the spike can be large enough to jump a stop. Futures news risk is concentrated in the same releases, with the addition of inventory reports and index rebalancing. A trader who intends to trade through news should confirm in writing that the practice is allowed, and should size for a stop that may fill well beyond its level.

The safest plan is to flatten before the scheduled release and re-enter after the first hour. It costs some opportunity and removes an entire category of dispute. Traders who insist on holding through news are usually the ones who discover the restriction during a payout review.

The payout path on each side

FTMO's first payout is available after a seasoning period, with subsequent withdrawals on a shorter schedule. FTUK pays after qualification on its own cycle. The difference that matters is not the split percentage but the time from a profitable month to cleared funds, and the conditions that can interrupt it. A cycle that pays in fourteen days is worth more than a cycle that pays in thirty if the trader needs the cash to keep trading.

Both firms review conduct before releasing profit. The review covers the trading record, the account statements and any signs of prohibited practice. A trader with a complete journal answers the review quickly; a trader without one is at the firm's mercy. Keep the record from the first day of the evaluation, not from the first payout request.

Identity and payment verification are the other gate. Complete them before the first withdrawal request, because verification is the step that turns a payout into a delay. The same applies to the payment method: withdrawals usually return to the method used for the fee, and a mismatched method triggers a manual review.

The fee, the split and the seasoning period

The fee is the price of entry; the split is the share of profit the trader keeps; the seasoning period is the delay before the first withdrawal. Three numbers, and traders usually compare only the first. A 90% split with a sixty-day seasoning is worth less to a trader who needs cash flow than an 80% split with a fourteen-day cycle, because the delay is a cost measured in opportunity.

On the forex side, the seasoning period begins when the funded account starts, not when the evaluation is purchased. The clock therefore runs after a process that may already have taken two months. On the futures side, the payout cycle begins after qualification, which is a shorter path. The total time from first payment to first withdrawal is the number to compare, and it is usually longer than either page suggests.

The split also interacts with the tax position. A profit split is income, and the trader is responsible for declaring it. A higher split with a longer delay can push income into a later tax year, which is sometimes an advantage and sometimes not. That is a personal calculation, but it belongs in the comparison because it changes the net value of the same headline percentage.

Retry economics over twelve months

Both firms bill once per attempt on the plans compared here. There is no monthly charge accumulating while the account sits idle, which makes the annual cost a function of the number of attempts. A trader who needs three attempts on a $500 fee spends $1,500 before the first funded payout. That is the number to compare with a realistic monthly profit, not with the account size.

The trailing floor on the FTUK side increases the expected number of attempts relative to a static floor, because it removes room after a good run. The multi-phase path on the FTMO side increases the number of stages but not necessarily the number of fees, since one fee covers the path. The two effects pull in opposite directions, and the net result depends on the trader's drawdown profile rather than on the firms.

A simple model: estimate the probability of passing each stage from your own record, multiply the stage probabilities to get the pass probability, and divide the fee by that number to get the expected cost per funded account. Most traders are surprised by how high that number is, and by how much it falls when the risk unit is reduced by a third.

Which trader should choose which

FTMO fits a forex trader who wants a static floor, a familiar platform stack and a structured multi-stage path. The static floor is the decisive feature for a trader whose method produces occasional deep retracements but recovers, because the floor does not move while the account is under water.

FTUK fits a futures trader who wants a one-off purchase, a TradingView or NinjaTrader workflow, and a single-stage evaluation. The trailing threshold is the decisive feature in the other direction: it punishes a trader who gives back gains, and it rewards a trader who locks profit at the close.

If both markets are accessible, the tie-breaker is the sizing table. Whichever account lets you keep your normal risk unit without breaching on a normal losing streak is the one that fits. The fee is the last variable, not the first.

What the verification stage actually changes

A verification stage is not a formality. It repeats the measurements of the evaluation with the same floor and a new target, which means the trader has to produce a second positive period of a defined length. The stage exists to confirm that the first result was a process rather than a lucky month, and it is the stage where traders who passed on a single oversized trade are removed.

The planning consequence is that the first phase cannot be traded as a sprint. A trader who reaches the target in five days with three oversized positions has no way to repeat the performance in the verification stage, and often breaches the floor trying. A trader who reaches the target over four weeks with a consistent size can repeat it, because the process is the same.

On a single-stage evaluation there is no verification, which removes one hurdle and one source of information. The firm learns less about the trader, and the trader learns less about their own consistency. For a trader who intends to run the funded account for years, the extra stage is cheap evidence. For a trader who intends to withdraw once and stop, it is pure cost.

The stage also affects the retry calculation. A trader who fails verification has already paid the fee and spent the time; the retry cost is the whole path again. That asymmetry is why the verification stage should be modelled as a separate probability in any expected-value calculation, not folded into the evaluation pass rate.

Red flags in either rulebook

The first red flag is a floor type that is not stated plainly on the pricing page. If the difference between static and trailing is buried, assume the stricter reading. The second is a daily loss rule without a stated basis; equity and balance produce different outcomes, and the ambiguity usually resolves against the trader.

The third is a payout condition that depends on a discretionary review with no published criteria. Every firm reserves the right to refuse profit from prohibited activity, which is reasonable. A rulebook that leaves the definition of prohibited activity open is a different matter, and it should be priced as a risk.

The fourth is a plan that changes mid-attempt. Firms update terms, and the version in force when you bought may not be the version in force at payout. Save a dated copy of the terms at checkout, and check whether the firm states which version governs. That single file is the difference between a dispute you can win and one you cannot.

A thirty-day plan for either account

Week one is calibration: trade half the normal risk, record every setup, and confirm that the daily stop and the floor leave comfortable room at that size. The goal is not profit; it is to find out how the account behaves when a stop fills and when a session goes badly.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal size, a fixed number of setups per day, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Track the remaining room to the floor at the start of each session, and cut the risk unit when it falls below the threshold you set in advance. On a trailing account, this is the week where the floor usually starts to matter.

Week four is consolidation: reduce size, protect the target, and prepare the payout documentation. A trader who reaches the target in week two and then doubles size to "make it worthwhile" is the trader who loses the account in week three. The last week of an evaluation is a risk-management exercise, not a trading one.

Common questions

Does FTUK evaluate forex accounts?

No. FTUK evaluates futures accounts on US session hours. FTMO evaluates forex and CFD accounts on weekday forex hours, which is why the platform lists barely overlap.

Which firm uses a static loss floor?

FTMO measures its maximum loss from the starting balance, so the failure point stays fixed. FTUK trails its threshold against peak equity, which reduces the remaining room after a gain.

Which one charges monthly?

Neither on the plans compared here: both bill once per attempt. Confirm the exact plan at checkout, because promotional or newer plans can change the billing rhythm.

Can I use an MT5 expert advisor on FTUK?

No. FTUK's stack is Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader. Automation is possible through those platforms, but an MT5 expert advisor does not run on them.

Is the trailing threshold measured on open profit?

It depends on the firm's definition of peak equity. Confirm whether open positions count, and whether the peak updates continuously or at a scheduled point. The answer changes how a give-back trade affects the floor.
