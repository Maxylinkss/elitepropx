How to pass an Audacity Capital challenge: the rulebook, the math and the plan

Audacity Capital sells a capital allocation programme rather than a conventional evaluation: a fixed loss allowance, a minimum trading period and a profit split, sometimes with a profit-share fee rather than an upfront one. That structure changes the preparation. There is no two-phase target to hit, but there is a period during which the account has to survive and a share of profit that may be committed before the first trade. This guide works through the mechanics with illustrative numbers. Figures are models, not quotations — confirm the programme terms before applying.

Key takeaways

  • A fixed loss allowance behaves like a static floor: it does not move with profit, so a constant unit stays valid.
  • The minimum trading period, not a target, sets the calendar; the account has to survive long enough to qualify.
  • Profit-share billing means the fee is paid from results, which changes the cost of a slow start.
  • The daily stop, where it applies, is programme-specific and has to be confirmed rather than assumed.
  • Lot sizing is the same arithmetic as any forex account: pips × pip value × lots, plus a buffer.
  • This is a survival product, not a sprint: the account's job is to still be open at the end of the period.

What an allocation programme actually is

A conventional evaluation asks a trader to reach a target inside a risk budget. An allocation programme asks a trader to trade a capital allocation for a defined period without breaching a loss allowance, and then shares the profit. The two structures test different things: the first tests target production, the second tests survival and consistency across time.

Audacity Capital's published shape is forex and CFD, a capital allocation programme rather than a conventional challenge, a firm-specific loss allowance that is often a fixed figure, a programme-specific daily stop, a consistency condition expressed as a minimum trading period, an undefined minimum-day count, one-off or profit-share billing depending on the programme, a profit split on a fixed schedule, MT4 and MT5, on weekday forex hours.

The most consequential difference is the absence of a target in the usual sense. There is nothing to hit, which removes the pressure that makes a conventional evaluation a sprint, and it introduces a different failure mode: a trader with no target can overtrade, because activity feels like progress. The allocation programme rewards the opposite — a small number of well-sized trades repeated across the period.

The four questions to answer before applying are the size of the loss allowance, whether a daily stop applies and on what basis, the length of the minimum trading period, and how the fee is charged. Those four answers determine everything that follows, and none of them can be inferred from the brand.

The fixed loss allowance in dollars

Assume a $50,000 allocation with a $5,000 fixed loss allowance. The failure point is $45,000 and it does not move, because the allowance is a fixed figure rather than a trailing threshold. Profit accumulates above it, and the distance to failure grows as the account earns. That is the same behaviour as a static floor and it produces the same planning simplification: the risk unit can be fixed.

The ratio that matters is the allowance against the expected profit. With $5,000 of room and a realistic monthly return of 2% to 4% on $50,000, the account produces $1,000 to $2,000 a month while risking $5,000. The ratio of risk to monthly return is therefore between 2.5 and 5, which is the number to compare with the fee and the split. A programme with a large allowance and a small allocation is not generous; it is expensive relative to what the account can earn.

The second number is the unit. With $5,000 of allowance and a ten-loss tolerance, the unit is $500. With a fifteen-loss tolerance it is $333. The second is more conservative and produces a longer calendar, which on a survival product is usually the better trade, because there is no target to miss and no penalty for patience beyond the period itself.

The third number is the self-imposed stop. A fixed allowance permits a slow bleed, so set a personal limit at one third of the allowance from the equity high. If the allocation reaches $54,000, the stop is $52,500. That rule preserves gains the programme permits giving back, and it is the only discipline the structure does not supply.

The minimum trading period

A minimum trading period is the length of time the account has to be traded before a payout can be requested. It replaces the target as the binding constraint: there is no number to reach, but there is a date before which nothing can be withdrawn. That changes the plan from a sprint into a survival exercise with a calendar attached.

The period interacts with the allowance in a way that is easy to underestimate. A trader who makes 6% in the first month and then gives back 4% in the second has a positive result and a smaller cushion for the remaining weeks. The programme does not care about the path, but the account does: the further the balance falls toward the allowance, the smaller the unit must become to keep the same loss tolerance.

The practical response is to treat the period as a series of equal blocks, each with its own budget. Divide the allowance into the number of months in the period and give each month a share. A three-month period with a $5,000 allowance gives $1,650 per month, and a month that consumes its share ends early rather than borrowing from the next one.

The end of the period is a checkpoint, not a finish line. A payout request needs a verified account, a completed review and a payment method that matches the fee. Starting those in the final week adds days to a timeline that was already long, and on a profit-share programme a delay is a delay in being paid for work already done.

Profit-share billing versus an upfront fee

Profit-share billing means the programme takes a larger share of the profit instead of an upfront fee. The appeal is obvious: nothing is paid until the account earns. The cost is equally obvious once it is modelled: the share is paid on every profitable period, not once, so a successful trader pays more over a year than an upfront fee would have cost.

The break-even is straightforward. If an upfront programme costs $500 and takes 20% of profit, and a profit-share programme costs nothing upfront and takes 40%, the profit-share version is cheaper until cumulative profit reaches $2,500, and more expensive after. A trader who expects to earn $2,000 in total should prefer the profit-share version; a trader who expects to earn $20,000 should prefer the upfront one.

The second difference is the incentive to size. On a profit-share programme the marginal cost of a larger position is a larger share of a larger profit, which is neutral in expectation. On an upfront programme the marginal cost of a larger position is a higher chance of a retry, which is a real cost. Neither structure is more dangerous; they fail differently.

The third difference is the cash-flow requirement. An upfront fee is paid before any evidence that the account will earn; a profit-share arrangement defers that cost. For a trader with limited capital, the deferral is worth a higher share, and the calculation is a personal one rather than a universal rule.

Lot sizing on a fixed allowance

Forex sizing is pips × pip value × lots. On a standard lot of a USD-quoted major, one pip is about $10, so a 25-pip stop risks $250 per lot and a $500 unit buys two lots. A 50-pip stop buys one. The stop distance comes from the method; the position count is the consequence, not the decision.

Pip value varies by pair and account currency. USD/JPY depends on the exchange rate, crosses run through two conversions, and metals and indices do not use pips in the usual sense. A trader who applies $10 per pip universally mis-sizes every non-USD-quoted instrument, and the error is largest when volatility is highest, which is exactly when the allowance is most at risk.

The buffer is the difference between a planned unit and a realised one. Spreads widen around the London open and around releases, and stops slip. Adding ten to twenty percent to the planned risk changes the lot count by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the unit. Without it, a slipped stop consumes one and a half units and the monthly budget is reached sooner than planned.

Leverage is rarely the binding constraint on an allocation programme. A correctly sized two-lot position on a $50,000 account uses a small fraction of available margin, so the only way leverage binds is when the size was wrong to begin with. That is an argument for sizing from the allowance rather than from the margin line.

The daily stop, when there is one

The daily stop is programme-specific here, which makes it a question rather than an assumption. Where it applies, it is usually a percentage of the balance, and its basis — equity or realised — determines whether floating losses count immediately. Where it does not apply, the trader has to supply the limit, because a fixed allowance with no daily rule permits a single session to consume the programme.

A personal daily cap of three units is a reasonable default on any forex account. With a $500 unit that is $1,500, or 3% of a $50,000 allocation. Three losses end the session, and a second session is only allowed if the monthly budget still has room. That rule is stricter than most programmes and it is what keeps a bad week from becoming a bad month.

The reset time matters for a swing method. Forex sessions roll at a fixed hour tied to New York, and a position carried across the roll may be attributed to either day. If the daily rule is unclear, flatten before the roll rather than discovering the attribution during a review. The cost of flattening is a spread; the cost of a misattributed loss is a dispute.

The weekly cap is the second layer. Four sessions at the personal limit is $6,000, which exceeds a $5,000 allowance. A weekly cap of one third of the allowance stops the accumulation before it becomes structural, and it is the only rule in the system that nobody else enforces.

Consistency and the trading-period condition

On an allocation programme the consistency condition is usually expressed as a minimum trading period rather than as a share of profit. The purpose is the same: to ensure the account is being traded as a process rather than as a single bet. A trader who makes the entire return in one week and then sits flat has satisfied neither the spirit nor, often, the letter of the programme.

The design response is a weekly target rather than a daily one. A return of 1% per week on a $50,000 allocation is $500, which at a 2:1 payoff with a $500 unit is one net winner per week. That is a modest requirement and it produces a smooth record across the period, which is exactly what a minimum-period condition is looking for.

The second response is a daily profit cap. Stopping a winning day at three units leaves room for the next day to contribute and flattens the distribution. It is counter-intuitive — leaving money on the table feels wrong — and it is the cheapest way to make a record look like a process rather than a streak.

Confirm whether a consistency condition applies on top of the minimum period, and whether it is measured on gross or net profit. Those two questions change the plan for week one, and neither answer is usually on the summary page. Ask in writing and keep the reply with the programme document.

MT4 and MT5 workflow

MT4 remains the most widely used retail forex platform and the one with the largest library of expert advisors. It handles hedging, it runs on modest hardware, and its ticket-based order model is familiar. For a trader with years of templates and scripts, the migration cost of leaving it is real and usually larger than any difference in spreads.

MT5 adds a multi-threaded backtester, more timeframes and an economic calendar, but its default netting model changes position management: several entries on one symbol combine into a single position with an average price. A trader moving from MT4 without understanding that difference will place stops that do not correspond to the tickets they think they hold.

Automation is the main reason to care about the platform on an allocation programme. A minimum trading period rewards steady activity, and a well-tested expert advisor can produce that activity without the trader watching every session. It also raises the rulebook question: some programmes restrict high-frequency activity or certain order types, and an advisor can breach those clauses without intent.

The practical recommendation is to settle the platform before applying and to test any automation on a demo under the same rules. A strategy that is compliant in principle and non-compliant in its order flow is still a breach, and a complete log is the only way to prove what happened months later.

Forex sessions and the pairs that fit

Forex liquidity is concentrated in London and New York, with the overlap between them the deepest window of the day. A method should be built for one window: a London breakout and a New York reversal have different stop distributions, different trade frequencies and different daily loss profiles, and mixing them produces a distribution neither was designed for.

The pair matters as much as the hour. EUR/USD and GBP/USD are liquid in London; USD/JPY is active in Tokyo and New York; AUD and NZD pairs move in the Asian session. A method that works on EUR/USD at 09:00 does not transfer to AUD/USD at 02:00 without re-measuring the spread and the stop.

On an allocation programme the session choice also affects how the minimum period is filled. A trader who trades one session per day has a natural daily cadence, which suits a period-based condition. A trader who trades around the clock produces an uneven record and a higher chance of a session that consumes the weekly budget.

Holding overnight adds rollover and gap risk, and the weekend adds a second gap. A position held across the weekend at full size carries a risk the stop does not measure. Either reduce the unit for positions held across a session boundary, or flatten before it, and write the choice down before the week starts.

A sixteen-week survival calendar

Weeks one and two are measurement: half the unit, a full record of setups, and a note of spread and slip on every entry. The output is a table of how the method behaves under the allocation's conditions, including which sessions and pairs are expensive. There is no target to chase, so this block is purely diagnostic.

Weeks three to eight are the working block: full unit, a fixed setup list, a daily cap of three losses and a weekly cap of one third of the allowance. The aim is a return of roughly 1% per week, which is a modest number that compounds across the period without requiring a large position.

Weeks nine to fourteen are the maintenance block: the same unit and the same caps, with a review every two weeks comparing the realised drawdown with the self-imposed limit. If the drawdown from the equity high exceeds a third of the allowance, stop for a week and review rather than pressing to recover.

Weeks fifteen and sixteen are the close: two thirds of the unit, the result protected, and the payout documentation prepared. On a profit-share programme the payout is the point of the exercise, and the paperwork is on the critical path to being paid for work already done.

Drawdown control without a trailing rule

A fixed allowance allows profit to be given back without any rule being broken, which is a comfort and a hazard. The account can fall from $56,000 to $47,000 and still be alive, which means the rules will not stop a slow bleed. Only the trader's own limits will.

The first control is a drawdown stop from the equity high, set at one third of the allowance. If the allocation reaches $54,000, the stop is $52,500; if it reaches $56,000, the stop is $54,500. The limit moves up with profit, which is the opposite of the programme's rule and the same as the trader's interest.

The second control is a time stop. If the method has not produced a positive return over two consecutive months, stop and review rather than extend indefinitely. On a profit-share programme there is no billing pressure to force that decision, which makes the time stop more necessary, not less.

The third control is a trade-count review. If trade count is far above the plan, the unit is probably too small and activity is compensating; if far below, the setup list is too narrow or the session is wrong. Both are diagnosable from the journal, and neither is fixed by changing the size.

News, gaps and weekend risk

Currency markets reprice on a short list of scheduled events, and the repricing is violent. A rate decision, a CPI print or a payrolls number can move a major pair by tens of pips before a retail stop is reached, and the spread quoted during that window is not the spread quoted a minute earlier. Once a position is open through such an event, the stop level becomes a wish rather than a measurement.

Programme terms handle this differently. One document bans new entries inside a stated window; another bans holding an existing position through the event; a third bans capturing the spike itself. The third is the hardest to interpret and the easiest to apply retrospectively, since a reviewer can judge it trade by trade or on the account as a whole.

The conservative plan is to flatten before the release and re-enter after the first hour. That costs some of the largest moves and removes a category of dispute. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the allowance can survive if it gaps.

Weekend gaps are the larger risk on a period-based programme, because there are more weekends in the period than there are releases. A position held over the weekend at full size is a position whose stop may not exist on Monday morning. Either reduce the unit for weekend exposure or flatten on Friday, and make the choice a rule rather than a mood.

The split, the cycle and the review

The split is the share the trader keeps; the cycle is how often it can be withdrawn. A 70% split on a monthly cycle and a 90% split on a quarterly cycle are different products, and the right choice depends on whether the profit is income or reinvestment. Compare the total time from the first trade to cleared funds rather than the headline percentage.

The review examines the record: size relative to the allowance, distribution of profit, the number of sessions and any pattern resembling prohibited practice. A complete journal answers the questions in an afternoon; a statement without one invites interpretation, and interpretation by a third party rarely favours the trader.

Verification sits on the critical path. Withdrawals usually return to the method used for the fee, so an expired card or a changed wallet triggers a manual review. On a profit-share programme there may be no fee to match, which makes the identity verification the only gate — and a missing document can still delay a payment by weeks.

Confirm what the split is measured on and whether the allowance is restored after a payout. A programme that reduces the allocation after a withdrawal has a different risk profile from one that resets it, and the difference is worth more than a percentage point of split.

Failure patterns on allocation programmes

The first is overtrading. With no target, activity feels like progress, and a trader who takes every setup consumes the monthly budget in a fortnight. The second is treating the allowance as a spending limit rather than a survival budget, which produces full-size positions from the first day instead of a measured ramp.

The third is ignoring a programme-specific daily rule because it was never confirmed. The fourth is a profit-share calculation made without modelling the annual cost, which turns a cheap-looking programme into an expensive one after six good months. The fifth is a drawdown that is allowed to reach the allowance because no self-imposed stop existed.

The sixth is starting the payout paperwork at the end of the period instead of in the final fortnight. The seventh is holding over a weekend at full size and discovering that the gap was larger than the stop. Each of these is a process error, and each is visible in a journal before it becomes expensive.

The remedy is a one-page plan with five numbers: allowance, unit, monthly budget, drawdown stop and period length. Add a rule that the unit does not change after a good month, and a rule that the journal is updated before the next trade. That page is the whole control system an allocation programme requires.

Who this programme suits

It suits a forex trader who wants a longer horizon than a challenge, who can trade a steady cadence across months, and who prefers a structure without a target to hit. The fixed allowance rewards a constant unit and a measured pace, and a profit-share fee defers the cost until the account earns, which suits a trader with limited capital.

It suits less well a trader who needs a defined finish line, one whose method produces occasional large months and long flat periods, or one who cannot commit to a minimum period. It also suits poorly a trader who needs an upfront fee for budgeting reasons, because a share of every profitable period is more expensive over a successful year.

The test is a simulation on your own record across the length of the period: apply the allowance, the personal daily cap and the drawdown stop, and record the maximum drawdown and the number of months with a positive return. If both fit, the programme is a fit. If either fails, no amount of discipline changes the arithmetic.

If it fits, the process is unglamorous: size from the allowance, cap the day and the week, aim for a small return each week, and treat the period as a survival exercise with a payout at the end. That is what an allocation programme is built to reward.

Common questions

Is this a challenge or an allocation programme?

It is described as a capital allocation programme rather than a conventional challenge. There is usually no profit target to hit; the account has to trade for a minimum period without breaching a loss allowance, and profit is then split.

Is the loss allowance fixed or trailing?

It is usually a fixed figure rather than a trailing threshold, so profit does not move the failure point. A constant risk unit stays valid, but a self-imposed drawdown stop is still needed because a fixed allowance permits a slow bleed.

How is the fee charged?

Depending on the programme, either as a one-off fee or as a share of profit. Model the annual cost before choosing: a profit-share arrangement is cheaper for a short or modest result and more expensive across a successful year.

How long is the minimum trading period?

It is programme-specific. The period, not a target, is the binding constraint, so confirm the length before applying and divide the loss allowance across the months it covers.

Which platforms are supported?

MT4 and MT5 are listed for the programme described here. Confirm the platform list and any automation clauses for the exact programme, because activity restrictions vary.

How much can I risk per trade on a $50,000 allocation?

With an illustrative $5,000 allowance and a ten-loss tolerance, the unit is about $500. A fifteen-loss tolerance gives $333, which is more conservative and better suited to a period-based programme with no target.
