Apex Trader Funding vs Bulenox: which rulebook fits your trading

Both firms sell a 30-day futures evaluation for a one-time fee, and both now offer a choice between an end-of-day drawdown and a live intraday trail. The meaningful differences are which model applies a daily stop, where the trail stops moving, the account sizes and contract caps, and what the payout rules require. Monthly billing is not part of either product in 2026, despite what most comparison pages still say. The figures below come from each firm's own help centre and pricing page, checked on 21 October 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Apex charges once for 30 days of access. There is no monthly renewal on a current evaluation, and no minimum trading days.
  • Apex sells two evaluations: an EOD model with a fixed daily loss limit, and an intraday model with no daily limit but a trail that follows unrealised profit.
  • Bulenox sells two risk models on the same account sizes: a live trailing drawdown with no scaling, or an EOD drawdown with a daily loss limit and a scaling plan.
  • The intraday trail behaves differently by platform at Apex: it stops at the target on Rithmic and WealthCharts but trails indefinitely on Tradovate.
  • Neither firm requires a minimum number of trading days. Both give 30 days of access, so the calendar is a deadline rather than a quota.
  • The platform lists are not identical in 2026: Bulenox runs on Rithmic-based platforms and does not issue Tradovate credentials.

What the two rulebooks measure

Two futures evaluations with the same market and similar account sizes are separated by three questions. Does the floor follow the high-water mark including floating profit, or settle at a defined point? Is there a session limit on top of the floor? And how is the fee charged, because that determines what a slow attempt costs.

Apex answers the first question twice, because it sells two products. The EOD evaluation calculates the threshold once a day from the closing balance and holds it fixed through the next session. The intraday evaluation moves the threshold in real time with peak balance, including unrealised profit, and never moves it down. Bulenox also offers two models: a trailing drawdown tracked live during the session, and an end-of-day drawdown paired with a daily loss limit and a scaling plan.

The third question is simpler than most reviews imply. Both firms charge a one-time fee for a 30-day evaluation. Apex states explicitly that there are no monthly charges and nothing renews automatically; Bulenox describes its Qualification accounts as having no recurring payment. A trader who budgets a monthly subscription for either firm is budgeting a product that no longer exists.

That matters for planning. On a one-time fee, a careful attempt costs the same as a rushed one, so the calendar can be used to reduce risk rather than to save rent. The trade-off moves to the 30-day deadline: an attempt that is not finished by day 30 needs a new purchase.

Two trails, one important difference

A trail that includes open profit moves while a position is running. A trade that reaches $900 unrealised has lifted the failure point by $900, and a retracement to entry consumes that room without a dollar being lost. A trail that settles at a defined point, usually the close, does not move until the session ends, so a position can breathe within the day.

Apex's intraday model is the strictest version of the first kind, with a platform-specific exception. On a $50,000 account with a $2,000 drawdown, the threshold starts at $48,000. If unrealised profit takes the balance to $50,900, the threshold becomes $48,900 immediately. On Rithmic and WealthCharts it stops climbing once it reaches the $53,000 target balance; on Tradovate it keeps climbing with the peak for the life of the evaluation.

Bulenox's Option 1 behaves like the live trail without the platform exception: the loss limit follows the highest point, including open positions, in real time and never falls back. Option 2 is the settled version, calculated at the close on realised profit, with the threshold fixed through the next session.

The planning consequence is a taper on the live trail. Full size until half the target, half size until three quarters, and a third afterwards. On the settled model the taper can be gentler, because the floor only moves once a day and tomorrow's buffer is known before the session begins.

A $50,000 attempt, line by line

Take Apex's EOD model on $50,000: a $3,000 target, a $2,000 EOD drawdown and a $1,000 daily loss limit. The threshold starts at $48,000 and is recalculated at the close from the day's ending balance. A day that closes at $51,000 moves the threshold to $49,000 for the next session; a day that closes at $49,500 leaves it at $49,500, because the threshold never moves down. The daily limit is a separate rule: losing $1,000 in a session pauses trading for the rest of that day without closing the account.

Now take Bulenox Option 2 on the same size: a $3,000 target, a $2,500 drawdown and a daily loss limit, with contract size scaling as cash on hand grows. The drawdown is calculated at the close on realised profit, and the day's limit pauses trading rather than closing the account. The $500 of extra drawdown room is the main structural difference at this size, and the scaling plan is the cost of it.

On Apex's intraday model the arithmetic is the same until the first winner with an open position. A $900 unrealised gain raises the threshold to $48,900 before the trade closes, and a return to entry leaves the buffer $900 smaller with no realised loss. That is the difference that decides holding behaviour: on the live trail, profit is banked against the floor the moment it appears.

Daily stops where they exist

A daily stop caps a session independently of the buffer. Where it exists, a bad day ends at a known figure and the account continues with the floor intact. Where it does not, the trail is the only limit, and the trail does not distinguish between one bad afternoon and five bad days.

Apex's intraday evaluation has no daily loss limit; risk control is enforced entirely through the trailing threshold. Its EOD evaluation does have one, fixed in dollars by account size: $500 on $25k, $1,000 on $50k, $1,500 on $100k and $2,000 on $150k. Hitting it pauses trading for the session and does not fail the account.

Bulenox puts the daily limit on Option 2 only. Option 1 relies on the live trail, which means a single session can consume the whole buffer. A trader choosing Option 1 should supply a personal session cap: with $2,500 of room, roughly $800 a day, which at a $250 unit is three losses.

The basis matters on both sides. A limit measured on equity counts floating losses, so a position that is $600 underwater has consumed the allowance before it is closed. A limit measured on realised results permits a larger floating loss. Apex's EOD limit is a fixed dollar figure checked against the account during the session; Bulenox's Option 2 limit pauses trading for the day. Confirm the basis at checkout, because the difference is a whole risk unit on a single trade.

One-time fees, not subscriptions

Apex's help centre is unusually direct: an evaluation is a one-time payment for 30 days of access, there are no monthly charges, and nothing renews automatically. The Performance Account activation fee is also one-time, not a subscription. Older reviews describing Apex as a monthly product are describing the legacy subscription accounts, which still exist but are not what the current checkout sells.

Bulenox prices its Qualification accounts the same way. On 21 October 2026 the listed prices were $145 for $25k, $175 for $50k, $215 for $100k and $325 for $150k, each described as one-time with 30 days of access. The firm also states that Qualification accounts have no recurring payment and that access simply expires at the end of the cycle.

Both firms have costs beyond the evaluation. Apex charges a one-time activation fee when a passed evaluation becomes a Performance Account, and market data is billed by the platform or data provider. Bulenox includes exchange data for traders who declare themselves non-professional; declaring professional adds $116 a month, and connecting through a third-party API or composite software adds $100 a month. Those are the numbers to put in the budget, not a fictional monthly evaluation fee.

Contract sizing on either

The arithmetic is identical on both products because the instruments are the same. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 per contract. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100. One Micro E-mini Dow contract pays $0.50 per tick, so a 20-tick stop risks $10.

With a $250 unit, the Nasdaq micro allows eight contracts, the S&P mini allows two, and the Dow micro allows twenty-five. A factor of twelve between the smallest and largest count for the same dollar risk is why sizing from contracts rather than from dollars is the most common error on a futures account.

Check the position cap before the risk budget. Apex's evaluation contract caps are fixed during the evaluation and do not scale: 4 contracts on $25k, 6 on $50k, 8 on $100k and 12 on $150k. Bulenox Option 1 gives full size from day one (3, 7, 12 and 15 contracts by size), while Option 2 starts smaller and scales with cash on hand. A size that fits the dollar budget and breaches the cap is a rule violation, not a size.

Add a slippage allowance. A stop order becomes a market order on trigger, and in a fast tape the fill can be several ticks beyond the level. On a 15-tick stop, three ticks of slip is a 20% overrun. On a live trail that overrun is felt immediately, because the floor has already recorded the high.

Minimum days and the calendar

Neither firm requires a minimum number of trading days in 2026. Apex says so on both evaluation pages and allows a pass in a single session; Bulenox lists "min. trading days: none" on its pricing table. The calendar is therefore a deadline, not a quota: 30 days of access on both sides, after which the evaluation expires.

That removes the old argument about billed waiting time. A trader who reaches the target in week one can stop trading and activate the funded account rather than keeping the account alive to satisfy a day count. It also removes the excuse for pressing: a target reached in three weeks is a target reached, and the remaining days cost nothing.

The deadline still shapes the plan. A 30-day window with a $3,000 target and a $2,000 drawdown leaves room for roughly ten $300 days, or a slower pace with smaller size. Mapping the target across the available sessions is the difference between a plan and a hope, and the mapping should assume at least one losing week.

The platform stacks

The platform lists are no longer the same, which is the single most out-of-date claim in most Apex vs Bulenox comparisons. Apex supports Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader, among others. Bulenox lists more than twenty platforms including NinjaTrader, R|Trader Pro, Quantower, Sierra Chart, MultiCharts and ATAS, and does not issue Tradovate credentials.

That matters if the method depends on a platform feature. A trader who has built a workflow around Tradovate cannot move to Bulenox without changing it; a trader on NinjaTrader can move either way. Rithmic is a data and routing layer rather than a front end, so "Rithmic" on both lists does not mean the same order entry experience.

Data fees follow the platform, not the firm. Futures data is usually billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Confirm which feed the plan includes and which one the method needs, and add it to the 30-day budget rather than discovering it at the first statement.

Payout conditions

Apex applies a 50% best-day consistency rule at payout on current accounts, with 30% retained on legacy Performance Accounts. That is stricter than the 30% figure still quoted in older reviews, and it is the rule that most often blocks a first withdrawal from an otherwise clean funded account. The split and the activation process are documented in the help centre; the consistency calculation is the part to read before requesting.

Bulenox pays a 90/10 split in the trader's favour. Payout requests on Fast Track and Momentum accounts are processed daily, while a regular Master Account is processed every Wednesday. The firm also applies a consistency rule at payout, with the exact figure set by account type and published in its help centre — so a passed evaluation is not automatically a payable one.

Both firms review conduct before releasing profit. On a trailing product the statements show a floor that moved during the session, which can look unusual to a reviewer. A journal that records the buffer at the start and the end of each session answers that question before it is asked. Ask whether the split is calculated on gross or net profit, and whether the funded stage keeps the evaluation's rulebook; those two replies are worth more than the advertised percentage.

Retry maths over twelve months

With one-time fees, the annual cost is fee times attempts. Bulenox publishes $175 for a $50,000 Qualification; Apex's checkout price varies with promotions, so use the price actually shown rather than a remembered one. At a 30% pass probability, three attempts per funded account is a reasonable planning assumption, which makes the annual cost roughly three fees plus data.

The lever that improves the number is a smaller unit, not a cheaper fee. Cutting the unit by a third usually turns a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown, and the fee is unchanged, so the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

The second lever is the model. An EOD drawdown is easier to survive than a live trail for a trader who holds through intraday noise, and a daily loss limit is easier to survive than an unbounded session for a trader who revenge-trades. Choosing the model that matches the failure mode is worth more than a few dollars of discount.

Releases and the overnight session

Both products trade US futures, so both face the same event calendar: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment reports and, for some instruments, inventory data. Bulenox states that news trading is allowed. Apex publishes conduct rules rather than a blanket news ban, but the details sit in the help centre and are enforced at review, which makes a violation a delayed failure with a complete audit trail.

The conservative plan is to be flat into the release and re-enter after the first half hour. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the buffer can survive if it gaps — a stricter calculation on a live trail, because the gap can lift the floor and consume the buffer in the same move.

The overnight session is the second scheduled risk. It is thinner, spreads are wider, and the daily settlement can change margin without a trade. On an EOD model the settlement is also the moment the threshold recalculates, so a position held through it sets tomorrow's buffer and therefore tomorrow's unit.

Which futures trader belongs on which

Apex fits a trader who wants a one-time 30-day attempt, a choice between a fixed daily limit and a pure trail, and a path to a large account: sizes run to $300,000. The EOD model suits someone who holds through intraday retracements; the intraday model suits someone who takes profit at levels and closes within the session.

Bulenox fits a trader who wants a cheaper one-time attempt with a 90/10 split and daily payout processing on its faster account types. Option 2 suits a trader who wants a daily stop and accepts smaller size while the account scales; Option 1 suits a trader who wants full size immediately and can supply their own session cap.

The tie-breaker is platform and payout cadence. A Tradovate trader stays at Apex. A trader who values daily processing over the evaluation price starts with Bulenox's Fast Track or Momentum path. Neither difference is about the headline target, which is identical on a $50,000 account at both firms.

What to check before paying

The first is the drawdown model, named exactly: EOD or intraday, and on which platform. The second is whether a daily stop applies to that model and on what basis. The third is the contract cap at the account size being bought.

The fourth is the fee structure at checkout, including the activation fee at Apex and the data declarations at Bulenox. The fifth is the payout consistency figure, which differs by firm and, at Bulenox, by account type. The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, automation and third-party trading. Each is short, each is enforced, and none is reliably on the comparison page.

Save a dated copy of every answer. It takes ten minutes and it is the only document that settles a dispute six weeks later, when the terms page has been revised.

A thirty-day plan for either

The first week is a measurement exercise at half size. Log the buffer before each entry, note what the floor does after a winning trade, and record the spread you actually paid. By Friday the account's behaviour should be a table of numbers rather than an impression.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recompute the buffer each morning and halve the unit when it falls below one twelfth of the starting threshold. On the live trail this is where compression is felt.

Week four is consolidation: a third of the unit, the result protected, and the payout file assembled. Pressing after the target is met is the classic way to hand back a finished attempt, and on a 30-day product there is no reason to trade out the clock.

Across all four weeks, three numbers belong on a card: buffer, unit and session cap. The products differ in how they move the first one; neither can do anything about a trader who does not look.

Common questions

Do both firms trail the loss floor?

Both offer a trailing model and an end-of-day model. Apex's intraday trail follows peak balance including open profit and stops at the target on Rithmic and WealthCharts but not on Tradovate. Bulenox's Option 1 trails live; Option 2 calculates the floor once a day at the close.

Does Bulenox have a daily stop?

Yes on Option 2, where the daily loss limit pauses trading for the day instead of closing the account. Option 1 has no daily limit and relies on the trailing drawdown.

Is Apex a monthly subscription?

No. A current Apex evaluation is a one-time payment for 30 days of access with no automatic renewal. The Performance Account activation fee is also one-time. Legacy subscription accounts still exist but are a different product.

Are the platforms identical?

No. Apex supports Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader among others. Bulenox runs on Rithmic-based platforms such as NinjaTrader, R|Trader Pro, Quantower, Sierra Chart and ATAS, and does not issue Tradovate credentials.

Can either be passed in a week?

Yes. Neither firm requires a minimum number of trading days, so a pass is possible as soon as the target is reached and the drawdown rules have been respected. Both evaluations still expire after 30 days.

How much should I risk per trade on $50,000?

With a $2,000 Apex drawdown and a ten-loss tolerance, about $200 per trade before slippage. Apex's $50k EOD model also has a $1,000 daily limit, so the session cap binds at five such losses. Bulenox's $2,500 drawdown gives $250 per trade on the same tolerance.
