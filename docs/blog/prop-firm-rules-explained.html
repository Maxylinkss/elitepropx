Prop Firm Rules Explained · reviewed October 2026

Prop Firm Rules Explained

Most traders can recite the headline rules of their challenge and still breach one, because knowing a rule and operating under it are different skills. This page is about the second skill: how a rule becomes a breach, which rules override which, where the written terms and the platform dashboard disagree, and how to reduce the whole rulebook to a one-page manual you can check before every session.

Key takeaways

  • Rules have a hierarchy. Account-ending rules outrank day-ending rules, which outrank payout-delaying rules.
  • A breach is a sequence, not a moment: measurement point, threshold, timing and enforcement all matter.
  • When two clauses conflict, the narrower and more specific clause usually wins — and the firm's reply is the only reliable tie-breaker.
  • The one-page manual is the practical output of reading a rulebook. If you cannot write it, you do not yet understand the rules.

Rules versus guidelines

Every prop firm publishes two kinds of text: rules that are enforced mechanically, and guidance that describes how the firm would like you to trade. The distinction is not always labelled, and treating guidance as a rule — or a rule as guidance — produces different kinds of failure.

Mechanical rules are objective. A maximum loss of 10% is calculated from a defined reference point; a daily loss of 5% is measured against a defined balance; a minimum of five trading days is counted by a defined method. These rules are usually visible on a dashboard, enforced automatically, and impossible to argue with after the fact. They are the rules that end accounts.

Guidance is subjective. "Trade responsibly", "avoid excessive risk", "maintain a consistent approach" — these phrases appear in terms and marketing but rarely have a numerical threshold. They become enforceable when a human reviewer decides that a pattern looks like abuse, which means they are enforced inconsistently and often after a payout request. A trader who ignores guidance because it is not a number is taking a risk that does not show up in the dashboard.

The practical response is to sort every sentence into one of three buckets: a number that is checked automatically, a number that is checked manually, or a phrase with no number at all. The first bucket belongs on your index card. The second belongs in your process notes, because you need to be able to explain your behaviour if asked. The third belongs in your risk assessment, because it is the category most likely to produce a surprise.

The four-level rule hierarchy

Not all rules are equally important. Sorting them by consequence is the fastest way to decide where to spend attention.

Level one: account-ending rules

These are the maximum loss, the trailing threshold and any conduct clause that allows the firm to void the account. Breaching one ends the evaluation or the funded relationship. They deserve the most conservative interpretation and the largest buffer. If a rule can close the account, assume the strictest reading until the firm confirms otherwise in writing.

Level two: day-ending rules

The daily loss limit is the main example. Breaching it usually ends the session, and at some firms ends the account. Even where the account survives, the day is lost, which costs time and can force a faster pace for the rest of the challenge. The daily rule is the one that most often turns a manageable drawdown into a failed attempt.

Level three: payout-delaying rules

Consistency requirements, minimum trading days, profit thresholds and KYC conditions do not usually end the account. They delay the money. A trader can pass the evaluation and still wait weeks for a first withdrawal because a payout rule was not satisfied. These rules are easy to underestimate because the account looks healthy while the payout is blocked.

Level four: conduct and style guidance

News policies, weekend rules, hedging restrictions, EA permissions and "responsible trading" language sit here. They may be enforced automatically, manually or not at all until a dispute. The risk is asymmetric: a breach can void a funded account, but the rule may never be mentioned during the evaluation. Treat level four as a diligence question before buying, not a detail to discover after.

Early in a static-drawdown challenge, the daily rule is usually the binding one. Near the target on a trailing account, the maximum loss is. At payout, the consistency rule can become the binding one even though it was irrelevant during the evaluation.

How a rule becomes a breach

A breach is not a single event. It is a sequence of four steps, and each step is a place where a trader can misjudge the risk.

Step one: the measurement point

Every rule measures something. The daily loss may measure equity including floating positions or closed balance only. The maximum loss may measure from the starting balance, from peak equity, or from the previous day's close. The measurement point is the most important sentence in the rule and the one most often skipped.

Step two: the threshold

The threshold is the number the measurement is compared against. A 5% daily loss on a $100,000 account is $5,000. A 10% maximum loss is $10,000. The arithmetic is simple, but the threshold can be expressed as a percentage of a moving base, which changes the dollar figure as the account grows or shrinks. A target described as 10% of the current balance requires more dollars after a drawdown than before it.

Step three: the timing

Timing decides whether a breach occurred before or after a reset, before or after a close, or during a period when the rule was suspended. A position that is $4,900 down at 23:59 on a $5,000 daily limit is inside the rule; the same position at 00:01 is in a new day. A trade opened before a news window and held through it may breach a holding rule even if it was opened legally.

Step four: the enforcement

Enforcement decides what happens next: an automatic closure, a warning, a manual review, or a payout denial. Two traders can breach the same rule and receive different outcomes depending on the enforcement path. This is why the written terms and a written support reply matter more than anecdotal reports from other traders.

The practical value of the sequence is diagnostic. When an account is closed unexpectedly, the trader can reconstruct which step was misunderstood. Was the measurement point different? Was the threshold moving? Was the timing at a reset? Was enforcement discretionary? The answer usually points to a clause that was read once and never converted into a number.

The five numbers to know every morning

A rulebook becomes manageable when it is reduced to five figures. They change as equity moves, which is why they are recalculated daily rather than memorised once.

  1. Distance to the failure floor. The dollar amount between current equity and the level that ends the account. On a trailing account, this is measured from the current floor, not the starting balance.
  2. The daily budget. The dollar amount that can be lost before the session ends. This is the number that governs position size for the day.
  3. The target remaining. The dollars still required to pass, and the number of R that represents at your normal risk.
  4. Trading days used and required. The gap between the minimum and the elapsed days, so you know whether a fast finish is eligible.
  5. Payout conditions outstanding. The consistency cap, profit threshold and KYC items that must be satisfied before a withdrawal.

These five numbers fit on an index card. The act of writing them is the difference between a trader who is managing a rulebook and one who is reacting to a dashboard. The card is dated, because a card from last week may describe a floor that has moved or a rule that has changed.

There is a sixth number that is useful but not on the card: the distance between the daily budget and the remaining room to the failure floor. If the daily budget is larger than the room, a single bad day can end the account. That ratio tells you whether the daily rule is a session constraint or an account constraint in disguise, and it changes the size you should use.

When two rules disagree

Rulebooks are written by different people at different times, and they sometimes contradict each other. The contradictions are not always accidental; a marketing page may describe a generous version while the terms describe a narrower one.

Headline versus footnote

A headline says "no time limit"; a footnote says a phase must be completed within sixty days. The narrower clause usually wins, because the terms are the contract and the headline is an advertisement. The practical rule is to read the footnote before believing the headline, especially on time limits, drawdown type and consistency.

Challenge page versus funded terms

The challenge page describes the evaluation; the funded terms describe the account you receive. When they disagree, the funded terms govern the money. A consistency rule that appears only in the funded terms is still binding, even if the challenge page never mentioned it.

Dashboard versus terms

The dashboard is a calculation, not a contract. If the dashboard and the terms disagree, the terms usually win, but the dashboard is what the firm's system will enforce. In practice, the firm's system is the arbiter, which is why a written clarification is valuable: it creates a record that can be used if the system produces an unexpected result.

Support reply versus published terms

A support reply can clarify an ambiguity, but it cannot override the published terms unless the firm confirms that it does. Ask for the clarification to be confirmed in writing by someone with authority, and keep the reply. A generic answer from a first-line agent is better than nothing, but it is not a contract amendment.

The resolution method is always the same: identify the specific clause, ask a specific question, get a specific answer, and keep it. A trader who does this for the three rules that matter most — maximum loss, daily loss and payout conditions — has covered most of the money at risk.

A practical rule-mapping exercise

Here is an exercise that takes about twenty minutes and produces a rule map for any firm. It is deliberately mechanical, because the goal is to remove interpretation from the process.

Step one: list every clause that contains a number

Copy the clause, the number, the reference point and the period into a table. Do not paraphrase yet; use the firm's words. A clause that says "6% maximum drawdown" goes in as written, with a note that the reference point is unresolved.

Step two: mark the measurement point

For each number, write what it is measured against: starting balance, current balance, peak equity, day's opening equity, or closed profit. If the terms do not say, mark it as "ask". A number without a measurement point is not a rule yet.

Step three: mark the reset

For each periodic rule, write when it resets: midnight server time, daily close, per session, or never. The reset is part of the rule, not an implementation detail.

Step four: mark the enforcement

For each rule, write what happens on a breach: account ends, day ends, warning, payout delayed, or manual review. This is the consequence column, and it determines how much buffer to leave.

Step five: convert to dollars and R

For your account size, convert each number into dollars. Then convert the target and the loss limits into R at your normal risk. The R figures tell you how many normal trades the challenge requires and how many losses it can absorb.

How rule changes reach you

Firms amend terms. Sometimes the change is material — a new consistency rule, a different drawdown calculation, a tighter news policy — and sometimes it is administrative. The trader's problem is that the change may be published without an email, buried in a terms page, or applied only to new accounts.

Where to look

Check the terms page, the FAQ, the announcements section and the dashboard notices before starting and again before a payout request. A change that appears only in the FAQ is still a change, and a dashboard notice can disappear after you dismiss it. Save a dated copy of the terms at the start of the evaluation and compare it before requesting money.

What to do when a change affects you

Ask the firm which version applies to your account. Some firms apply changes prospectively to new accounts; others apply them to all accounts immediately. The answer is usually in the terms, but the terms may be ambiguous, and a written reply is the only way to know. If the change makes the challenge materially harder, ask whether you can close the account and receive a refund or a reset. The answer may be no, but the question is worth asking before the change costs you the fee.

The version-control habit

Keep a folder with the terms, the support replies and the dashboard screenshots, dated. This is not paranoia; it is the same discipline you would apply to any contract. When a dispute arises, the trader with a dated copy of the terms is in a different position from one relying on memory.

Rules that punish success

Some rules become harder as the account performs. They are not designed to be cruel; they are designed to manage the firm's risk. But they change the optimal behaviour, and a trader who does not notice will give back a good start.

The trailing maximum loss

As profit accumulates, the failure floor rises. On an intraday trailing account, even unrealised profit raises it. The result is that the room available after a good run is smaller than the room at the start, while the target is closer. The correct response is to reduce size in the middle phase, not to press for the finish.

The consistency rule

A large winning day can create a distribution problem. The more successful the day, the more the trader needs to dilute it with other profitable days. This can encourage additional trading after the best result, which is the opposite of the usual advice to stop when ahead. The solution is to plan the number of sessions before starting, so the distribution is built in rather than repaired later.

The scaling plan

Scaling plans reward consistent profit with larger accounts, but they often require a minimum number of profitable months or a minimum payout history. A trader who makes a large profit in one month may still be ineligible for a scale-up if the plan requires consistency. The rule is not a obstacle to success; it is a definition of success that differs from the trader's.

The common thread is that these rules measure the shape of performance, not just the total. A trader who understands that can plan the shape: steady sessions, controlled size, a distribution that satisfies the consistency rule, and a drawdown that stays roughly constant as the account grows.

Rules that punish speed

Other rules make fast passes ineligible or expensive. They are less common than the success-punishing rules, but they are more likely to be missed because the marketing emphasises speed.

Minimum trading days

A five-day minimum means a pass in three sessions is not eligible for verification until two more days are recorded. A trader who reaches the target on day three must keep trading, which adds exposure after the goal is met. The correct plan is to spread the target across at least the minimum number of days, not to finish early and then trade flat.

Time limits

A thirty-day limit punishes slow progress, but it also punishes a plan that relies on waiting for perfect setups. The trader has to balance patience with the calendar, which often means taking slightly more risk than a no-limit challenge would justify. The rule changes the optimal pace, and the pace changes the size.

Phase gates

Some challenges require a minimum profit in phase one before phase two begins, or a minimum number of trades. A trader who passes phase one with a single large trade may find that the phase gate is not satisfied. The fix is to read the gate conditions before starting, not after the first phase is complete.

The practical response to speed rules is to plan backwards from the calendar. If the minimum is five days and the limit is thirty, the target should be reachable in five to twenty days at normal risk, with a buffer for a flat week. A plan that requires a perfect week is a plan that has not accounted for the rule.

The unwritten rules

Some of the most important rules are not in the rulebook at all. They are the behaviours that firms treat as abuse even when no clause explicitly prohibits them.

Overtrading after a loss

A burst of trades immediately after a loss, with increasing size, looks like revenge trading. It may not breach a written rule, but it can trigger a manual review and it is a leading indicator of a blow-up. The fix is a hard stop after two losses, written before the session.

Hedging across accounts

Taking opposite positions in two accounts at the same firm can look like risk-free profit extraction, even if each account is traded independently. Many firms prohibit it explicitly; those that do not may still treat it as abuse. If you run multiple accounts, keep the strategies independent and document the reason for any opposite exposure.

Exploiting platform quirks

Latency arbitrage, stale prices and order-fill quirks are prohibited at most firms, and the prohibition is often enforced after a payout. A strategy that depends on a platform defect is not a strategy; it is a dispute waiting to happen.

Treating the evaluation as a game

Some traders use the evaluation account to test reckless ideas because the money is simulated. The firm sees the behaviour, and the funded account is the reward for a process the firm can trust. A trader who behaves differently on a simulated account than on a funded one is telling the firm which version of them will manage real capital.

These unwritten rules are the reason a passing service has to be conservative about conduct. A provider that treats the evaluation as a target to hit, without regard to how the trading looks, is exposing the client to a review that can arrive after funding. The written rulebook is the floor, not the ceiling, of acceptable behaviour.

The one-page operating manual

The output of all this reading is a single page. It is not a summary of the rulebook; it is the set of decisions you will make before each session, written when you are calm.

  1. The five numbers, dated. Failure floor distance, daily budget, target remaining, days used and required, payout conditions outstanding.
  2. The size rule. Risk per trade as a fraction of the daily budget, and the maximum aggregate exposure.
  3. The stop rule. The point at which the session ends, in dollars and in consecutive losses.
  4. The calendar rule. The latest date for each phase and the minimum number of days still required.
  5. The conduct rule. The news, weekend, hedging and platform behaviours that are prohibited, and the ones you have confirmed in writing.
  6. The escalation rule. What you do if a rule changes, a dashboard disagrees with the terms, or a payout is delayed.

Six lines, checked before the first trade and updated after any change in equity or terms. The manual works because it removes the decision from the moment of stress. A trader who has to interpret a rulebook while a position is open will interpret it in the direction that justifies the position.

There is a final test: give the page to another trader and ask them to explain your plan back to you. If they cannot, the page is not specific enough. The rulebook is only understood when it can be operated by someone who has not read the original document.

Frequently asked questions

Which prop firm rule ends an account?

The maximum loss or trailing threshold is the main account-ending rule, along with conduct clauses that allow the firm to void the account. Daily loss limits usually end the day, but some firms treat them as account-ending too.

What is the difference between a rule and a guideline?

A rule has a measurable threshold and an enforcement mechanism. A guideline is a phrase such as "trade responsibly" that is enforced by human judgement. Guidelines can still void an account, so they belong in your risk assessment.

What should I do when two clauses contradict each other?

Identify the specific clauses, ask the firm which applies to your account, and keep the written answer. In general the narrower, more specific clause wins, and the funded terms govern the money.

How often should I recalculate my numbers?

Every morning before the first trade, and again after any significant equity move. On a trailing account, the distance to the floor changes with profit, so a card written once at the start is stale within a week.

Do firms change rules during an evaluation?

Some reserve the right to amend terms. Keep a dated copy of the terms and check the announcements before a payout request. Ask which version applies to your account if a clause changes.

What is the most commonly breached rule?

The daily loss limit, usually through position size or aggregate exposure rather than a single oversized trade. It is the rule most often monitored incorrectly because the measurement point and reset time are overlooked.

Can a passing service handle the rule mapping for me?

It should. Ask for the mapping for your specific firm and account size, including the measurement points and reset times. A provider that cannot produce it in figures is describing an intention rather than a process.
