FTMO vs Take Profit Trader: which rulebook fits your trading

One product evaluates forex on a static floor across two phases and a verification stage; the other evaluates futures on a trailing threshold with a rulebook built around payouts. The market decides the session and the sizing unit before any rule is compared, and the floor decides whether a position can be held through a retracement. The billing differs as well: one-off on one side, monthly on the other. Figures describe each rulebook's published shape — confirm the live plan terms before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Different markets: weekday forex on FTMO, US futures on Take Profit Trader.
  • FTMO's floor is static from the starting balance; Take Profit Trader trails it, so a gain narrows the buffer.
  • FTMO applies no consistency rule to the evaluation; Take Profit Trader applies one.
  • Billing differs in kind: one-off per attempt against a monthly subscription.
  • Sizing is lots against pips on one side and contracts against ticks on the other.
  • Neither platform list overlaps, so the workflow follows the market decision.

What each product measures

Both evaluations measure progress, survival, session behaviour, pace and convertibility. The differences are in how survival is defined and what the calendar costs, and those two rows decide more than any target figure.

FTMO measures survival against a static floor taken from the starting balance. On $100,000 with a 10% floor the failure point is $90,000 for the whole path, and profit adds room above it. A position can be held through a retracement without moving the failure point, which is the feature that suits a method with deep but recoverable drawdowns.

Take Profit Trader measures survival against a trail. The failure point follows the high-water mark, so a winning run narrows the buffer available to the next losing sequence. A position that runs into profit and gives it back consumes room even when it closes flat, which penalises holding for a larger move.

The billing follows a similar split. FTMO charges once per attempt, so duration adds nothing to the cost and a longer calendar is free. Take Profit Trader bills monthly, so the calendar is a cost centre and a slow attempt is an expensive one. Two products with similar headline allocations therefore reward opposite pacing.

A static floor against a trail

A static floor is a fixed dollar distance. On $100,000 with a 10% floor the room is $10,000 from the first trade to the last, and a $500 unit tolerates twenty losses. That is beyond any normal distribution, so the binding constraint becomes the target and the calendar rather than survival.

A trail is a moving distance. On $100,000 with a $3,000 trail the room starts at $3,000 and the failure point rises with every new high. A $500 unit tolerates six losses at the start; after the account reaches $104,000 the failure point is $101,000 and the room from the balance is $3,000, still six losses, but only $1,000 above the original start.

The difference shows up in holding behaviour. On a static floor a trader can hold a position through a retracement, because the failure point did not move. On a trail the same retracement consumes buffer that the high already claimed, so holding for a larger target is a risk decision rather than a patience decision.

There is a second-order effect worth pricing. On a trail, a trader who is up $3,000 and takes a normal losing week can finish at break-even with the failure point $500 below the balance. The same week on a static floor leaves $7,000 of room. Identical results, very different room to continue.

Both need a self-imposed rule the rulebook does not supply. On the static side it is a drawdown stop from the equity high, because the floor permits a slow bleed. On the trail it is a taper, because the last third of a target is bought with less room than the first.

A $100,000 account on each

On FTMO, assume an 8% target per phase and a 10% floor. Phase one needs $8,000 and phase two another $8,000 from the new balance. A $500 unit at a 2:1 payoff returns $1,000 per winner, so each phase needs eight net winners, spread across a few weeks.

On the trailing product, assume a 6% target and a $3,000 trail. The target is $6,000 and the room starts at $3,000, so the target is twice the initial buffer. That ratio is the number to notice: on a trail, the target can be larger than the room, which means the account must earn before it can afford to lose.

The practical consequence is sequencing. On the trailing account the first task is to build buffer above the starting balance, not to reach the target. A trader who tries to run at the target from day one is taking maximum risk with no cushion, and a normal losing streak ends the attempt before any progress is made.

A simple rule captures it: while the account is within half a buffer of its starting balance, trade at two thirds of the planned unit. Once it is a full buffer ahead, move to the planned size. That single sequencing rule removes the most common failure on a trail, which is a promising start followed by a give-back that ends the attempt.

Both accounts should therefore be planned in two blocks: a build block at reduced size until the buffer is comfortable, and a push block at full size once it is. The taper runs in the opposite direction on the trailing side, which is why the two blocks are not symmetric.

Two phases and a verification against one evaluation

FTMO's path is three stages with a target at each and the same floor throughout. The structure rewards a trader who can repeat a process rather than one who can produce an exceptional month, and the verification stage is where a lumpy pass is exposed.

Take Profit Trader's path is a single evaluation with a trailing threshold and a rulebook oriented toward payouts. There is one target, one floor and one decision, and the payout conditions do more of the filtering than a second stage would.

The planning difference is where the risk sits. A three-stage path spreads risk across a longer calendar, so the chance of encountering a bad month somewhere is higher. A single-stage path concentrates the risk in one window, so the chance of a bad month ending everything is higher.

A trader with a steady method will prefer the longer path because the extra stages cost nothing but time. A trader with a lumpy method will prefer the shorter one because a bad month in a three-stage path is more likely to arrive somewhere.

The stage structure also changes how a failure is diagnosed. A trader who fails the first phase has a size problem or a strategy problem. A trader who fails verification has a consistency problem: the method produced a result but not a repeatable one. The second diagnosis is more useful and it is unavailable on a single-stage product.

Daily stops and their bases

FTMO's daily stop is equity-based, so floating losses count as they occur. A position that is $2,000 underwater has consumed 40% of a 5% allowance before it is closed. That produces better behaviour and tighter statements.

The futures product applies a daily stop on most plans, and the basis should be confirmed. An equity basis counts open risk; a realised basis permits a larger floating loss and a different behaviour at the close.

A personal cap does the real work on both. With a $500 unit, three losses is $1,500, which is 1.5% of the account and inside any published limit. Three losses end the session, and a second session is allowed only if the weekly budget has room.

The reset time differs by market. Forex rolls at a fixed hour tied to New York; futures roll at their own defined time. A position carried across either roll can be attributed to a different day, which matters more on a trailing product where the buffer is already tight.

Lots against contracts

On FTMO, size in lots. One standard lot of a USD-quoted major is about $10 per pip, so a 25-pip stop risks $250 and a $500 unit buys two lots. A 50-pip stop buys one.

On the futures side, size in contracts. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 and a $500 unit buys sixteen contracts before the cap. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100 and the same unit buys five.

The dollar risk is identical and nothing else about the positions is similar. A two-lot forex position and a sixteen-contract futures position carry the same risk, different commissions, different spreads and different session behaviour, which is why a trader cannot translate a size between markets.

Both need a buffer. Spreads widen and stops slip, and the fill is what the floor sees. Ten to twenty percent of headroom changes the position by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the unit — more important on the trail, where the buffer is already moving.

The consistency condition

A consistency test limits how much of the profit one day may contribute. It exists to distinguish a process from a single position, and it changes the shape of a correct attempt: the target has to be reached through ordinary sessions.

FTMO applies none to the evaluation, which leaves the distribution free until the payout review. The futures product applies one, which makes the distribution a planning input from week one. A trader whose method produces occasional large days is affected by one and not the other.

The test to run is the share of profit from the best day. Take sixty sessions, total the profit, and compute that share. Above a quarter, the rule will bind and the plan needs a daily profit cap. Below a quarter, the rule is unlikely to change anything.

The design response is a cap at two or three units per session. It costs little in expectation, flattens the distribution, and satisfies a single-day rule as easily as a rolling-window one.

One-off against monthly

FTMO charges once per attempt. At an illustrative $450, three attempts cost $1,350 regardless of duration. That makes patience affordable and puts the cost of failure on the retry count.

The futures product bills monthly. At an illustrative $150 plus $50 of data, a three-month attempt costs $600 and a six-month attempt $1,200. Duration is a cost, so the optimal pace is the fastest one that respects the buffer rule.

Comparing the two requires a duration assumption. At six weeks the subscription is cheaper; at five months it is not. Since a correctly sized attempt usually finishes in six to ten weeks, the two are close enough that the rulebook should decide the purchase rather than the price.

Retries change the arithmetic. Three monthly attempts of three months each is nine months of subscription plus fees, which can be four times the headline price. Three one-off attempts cost three fees with no calendar charge.

Two platform stacks

FTMO lists MT4, MT5, cTrader and DXtrade. The futures product lists Tradovate, NinjaTrader and Rithmic. There is no overlap, so the workflow decision follows the market decision rather than competing with it.

Within the forex stack, MT4 is the familiar hedging platform with the largest expert advisor library; MT5 adds a modern backtester but defaults to netting; cTrader is the level-based option; DXtrade is the browser terminal without installation.

Within the futures stack, Tradovate is the lightest front end, NinjaTrader the programmable one, and Rithmic the data and routing layer valued for depth of market. A trader with an existing NinjaTrader strategy can deploy it without rebuilding.

Data costs differ. Forex data is usually bundled; futures data is usually billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Confirm what is included before comparing monthly costs, because a cheap fee with an expensive feed is not cheap.

Forex hours against futures hours

Forex trades from Sunday evening to Friday evening, with the deepest liquidity in the London and New York overlap. Futures trade nearly continuously, with the regular US session carrying most of the liquidity.

For a trader outside the US, the practical difference is the clock. Forex can be worked in a European or Asian day; the US futures session falls in the evening or the night. A method that requires the liquid session is therefore unavailable to some traders on one product and available on the other.

The second difference is the gap. Forex has a Sunday open that can jump a stop placed on Friday; futures have a settlement and a thinner overnight session. Both require the trader to know when the account is exposed.

The availability test is the same on both: mark the hours you can watch for four weeks and check whether the liquid block fits. If it does not, the evaluation is being traded on hope.

There is a subtler point about the daily stop. Volatile sessions consume a session cap faster, so a method that trades the open needs a smaller unit than one that trades the quiet midday block, even when both use the same account. Sizing the session against the cap rather than the unit alone is what keeps a fast method inside the rules.

Payout calendars

FTMO's first payout waits for a seasoning period that begins when the funded account does. The futures product pays on request after qualifying days. The total time from first payment to cleared funds is the number to compare.

Both review the record before releasing profit. On a trailing product the statements show a floor that moved during a session, which can look unusual to a reviewer. A journal recording the buffer at the start and end of each session answers the question before it is asked.

Identity and payment verification sit on the critical path. Names, addresses and the funding instrument have to match, and a since-replaced card means a manual check. Clear them during the attempt.

The minimum withdrawal is the other detail. A cycle that requires a balance above a threshold pushes the first payout later than the schedule suggests, which matters for a trader using the profit as income.

There is also the question of what is withdrawn. A trader who takes the full profit each cycle returns the account to its base and rebuilds the buffer from scratch, which on a trailing product means re-entering the tightest part of the rule. Withdrawing a fraction keeps the buffer and makes the next cycle easier.

Releases and gaps

Both markets react to the same macro calendar, and the reaction is violent. Minutes before a release the book thins, the spread doubles, and a stop placed at a comfortable distance becomes a fill at an uncomfortable one.

Each rulebook treats the window differently. One bans fresh entries inside a stated period, another bans carrying a position through the event, a third bans capturing the spike. The third is the hardest to interpret.

Going flat beforehand and returning after the first hour removes the dispute. Anyone who stays in should halve the position and move the stop to a level where even a gap leaves the floor untouched.

On a trailing product that calculation is stricter, because a gap can lift the floor and consume the buffer in the same move. On a static floor the gap is a loss rather than a loss plus a rule change.

Which trader fits which

FTMO fits a forex trader who wants a static floor, a longer path with a verification stage, and no distribution rule during the evaluation. It suits a method with deep but recoverable drawdowns and a trader who does not need the first payout immediately.

Take Profit Trader fits a futures trader who already works a Tradovate, NinjaTrader or Rithmic workflow, who can watch the US session, and whose method closes positions rather than holding for a large move. The trail rewards precision.

The tie-breaker is the market. A trader competent in forex will underperform on a futures evaluation regardless of the rulebook, because sizing, session and platform assumptions do not transfer. Choosing the market you already trade is worth more than any rule comparison.

Should both markets be open to you, run your own history through each rule set at identical dollar risk. The version whose simulated drawdown stays inside the floor is the one your method already suits.

What to confirm before paying

The first question is the floor type and whether it includes open profit. The second is the daily stop's basis and reset time. The third is whether a consistency rule applies and at what stage.

The fourth is the billing: one-off or monthly, and whether a retry is charged again. The fifth is the position cap, in lots or contracts.

Finish with the conduct section: event windows, permitted order types, automation and third-party trading. Short clauses, real consequences, and rarely reproduced in a comparison table.

Date every reply and keep them together. A support message from the week of purchase is worth more than any later reconstruction of what the page said.

A thirty-day plan for either

Start at half size and record rather than target. Log the buffer before each entry, note what the floor does after a winner, and write down the spread paid. By the end of week one the account has a documented behaviour.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, a daily loss cap and a daily profit cap. On the trailing side, recompute the buffer every morning and halve the unit when it falls below the threshold set in advance.

Finish at a third of the unit, on the same instrument, with the payout file read twice. The last fortnight kills more attempts than the first.

What remains after the paperwork is a card with five entries: buffer, unit, loss cap, profit cap and weekly stop. The first is revised daily, the rest follow it.

Common questions

Which one trails the loss floor?

Take Profit Trader trails it, so a winning run narrows the buffer. FTMO measures its floor from the starting balance, which stays fixed for the whole path.

Does FTMO apply a consistency rule?

Not to the evaluation. The payout review still examines the trading record, but there is no distribution test during the attempt itself.

How does billing differ?

FTMO charges once per attempt; Take Profit Trader bills monthly. That makes a longer calendar free on one side and expensive on the other.

Can I trade forex on Take Profit Trader?

No. It evaluates futures on US session hours, and its platforms are Tradovate, NinjaTrader and Rithmic. FTMO is the forex product in this comparison.

How do I size the same risk on both?

Divide the buffer by the losses you will tolerate, then convert. On forex that is pips × pip value × lots; on futures it is ticks × tick value × contracts. The dollar risk matches even though the position counts do not.

Which suits a method that holds winners for hours?

The static floor, because a retracement does not move the failure point. On a trail the give-back consumes buffer that the high already claimed.
