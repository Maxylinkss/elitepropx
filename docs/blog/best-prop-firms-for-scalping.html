Best Prop Firms for Scalping · reviewed October 2026

Best Prop Firms for Scalping

Scalping is the strategy most exposed to trading costs and least rewarded for patience. A method that earns eight pips a trade and pays two in spread is not a scaled-down swing method; it is a different business, and the rulebook that suits it charges least per trade while counting least per session. This guide prices a scalp attempt properly, then ranks ten firms by how their rules treat a high trade count.

Key takeaways

  • Commission and spread are charged per trade, so a scalper pays the evaluation fee several times over in running costs.
  • Three hundred trades is three hundred chances to breach a news window, a maximum-lot rule or a consistency clause.
  • A static loss floor suits a method that holds through noise; a trailing floor charges for every give-back.
  • One-off billing is worth more to a slow, high-frequency attempt than a discount on a subscription.
  • The best scalp firm is the one whose cost per trade is lowest, not the one with the cheapest entry fee.

What scalping actually costs on an evaluation

The evaluation fee is the smallest number in a scalper's budget. Every trade pays the venue twice, once on entry and once on exit, through the spread or the commission schedule. That cost does not care whether the trade worked. It is charged on winners, losers and scratches alike, which means it scales with activity rather than with skill.

Take a $100,000 forex evaluation and a one-standard-lot position on EURUSD. One pip is worth about $10 at that size. A round-turn spread cost of two pips is $20 per trade. Three hundred trades over the attempt is $6,000 of running cost, against a typical profit target of $8,000. The trader has to make the target and absorb a cost equal to three-quarters of it. At a 0.6-pip spread the same three hundred trades cost $1,800, and the difference between those two numbers is larger than any promotional discount on the fee.

Commission schedules work the same way. A futures scalper paying $4 per round turn on a mini contract and taking four hundred trades pays $1,600. On a micro contract the per-trade commission is smaller but the tick value is smaller too, so the ratio to the target does not improve automatically. What improves it is fewer trades per dollar of profit, which is a strategy question before it is a firm question.

Slippage is the third cost and the hardest to price in advance. A limit order that fills at the price avoids it; a market order in a fast session pays it. Scalping methods that rely on entering at the touch are more sensitive to execution quality than methods that enter on a retest, because the edge per trade is smaller. On an evaluation, that sensitivity shows up as a wider realised spread than the one advertised on the pricing page.

Put together, the three costs mean a scalper should compare firms on the cost of a hundred trades, not on the cost of a challenge. A firm with a $400 fee and a 0.8-pip spread can be cheaper across an attempt than a firm with a $150 fee and a 1.6-pip spread, because the spread is charged three hundred times and the fee once. That inversion is the single most useful idea on this page.

The arithmetic of a scalp attempt

A scalp method is usually described by its win rate and its average win and loss in pips. Those three numbers decide whether the method survives its own costs. A method with a 55% win rate, a six-pip average win and a five-pip average loss looks profitable before costs: expectancy is 0.55 × 6 minus 0.45 × 5, which is 3.3 minus 2.25, or 1.05 pips per trade.

Now charge two pips per round turn. The average win becomes four pips net and the average loss becomes seven. Expectancy is 0.55 × 4 minus 0.45 × 7, which is 2.2 minus 3.15, or minus 0.95 pips per trade. The same method, with the same entries and exits, has moved from a small edge to a certain slow loss. Nothing about the strategy changed; the venue did.

The table is the reason this ranking puts cost per trade first. A difference of 0.6 pips in round-turn cost is worth 180 pips over three hundred trades, which on a one-lot position is $1,800. No entry-fee discount on the market comes close to that. It also explains why scalpers often report that a firm was "easy" for a friend and "impossible" for them: the two were not trading the same cost structure, even when they traded the same plan.

The account size changes the arithmetic in dollars but not in pips. A 0.1-lot position pays a tenth of the spread in cash, and also earns a tenth per pip, so the ratio is unchanged. What does change is the fixed-cost burden: data feeds, VPS, platform subscriptions and any monthly plan fee are the same in dollars on a small account as on a large one. That is why a small scalping account needs a cheaper cost stack, not merely a cheaper evaluation.

Finally, count the attempts. A method with a slightly negative expectancy after costs will fail most evaluations eventually, and each failure costs the full fee. The expected number of purchases is the fee divided by the probability of passing, so a method that is marginal on cost produces a large multiplier on the fee. The cheapest-looking plan is often the one bought most often.

Why a high trade count is a rule risk

Every rule that is assessed per trade becomes more likely to be breached as the number of trades rises. A trader taking twenty trades a week for twelve weeks takes roughly two hundred and forty chances to hit a news window, to exceed a lot-size cap, to hold a position over a rollover, or to trip a consistency clause assessed at payout. A swing trader taking ten trades over the same period takes ten chances. The rules may be identical; the exposure is not.

News windows are the most common trap. Many evaluations restrict opening or closing positions within a short window around scheduled releases, and the restriction often applies to pending orders as well as market orders. A scalper who trades continuously will eventually have a position open into a release, even if the entry was hours earlier. The breach is not a matter of intent; it is a matter of coverage. Before buying, list the restricted events, the window around each one, and whether existing positions must be closed.

Consistency clauses are the second. A rule that caps any single day's share of total profit is usually assessed at payout, and a scalper who has one exceptional session can fail it even after a legitimate pass. The same clause can be satisfied by spreading profit across days, which for a scalper means deliberately reducing size or stopping early on a strong day. That is a real cost, because it caps the best days while leaving the losing days uncapped.

Maximum trade counts and maximum lot sizes are the third. Some plans cap the number of open positions, the number of trades per day, or the total volume relative to account size. A method that scales into a position in five clips can breach a cap that a single-entry method never touches. Read the rule as a limit on exposure, not as a limit on tickets, because firms often count both.

Automation and copy-trading rules are the fourth. A scalper using an expert advisor, a signal service or a trade copier across accounts needs to confirm that the plan permits it and that the detection method the firm uses will not read legitimate activity as prohibited. Where the terms require disclosure, disclose; where they prohibit the practice, no performance record makes it permitted.

What the rulebook has to do for a scalper

Five properties decide whether a rulebook is compatible with a high-frequency method. The first is a static loss floor. A trailing floor rises with equity, so a scalp that gives back part of an open gain can raise the failure point against the trader. A static floor leaves the failure point where it started, which means a normal retracement is a cost rather than an event.

The second is a daily stop measured on the right basis. An equity-based stop counts open positions, so an intraday drawdown on a position that later recovers can still end the session. A balance-based stop does not. For a scalper holding several positions at once, the difference between those two definitions is larger than the difference between two fee levels.

The third is a minimum-day rule, or its absence. A plan with no minimum can be passed in a session by a method that works; a plan with a ten-day minimum forces ten sessions of exposure regardless of how quickly the target is reached. On a subscription that is ten days of billing; on a one-off fee it is ten days of risk. Both belong in the calendar before the purchase.

The fourth is the cost schedule: spread during the sessions the method trades, commission per lot or per contract, and any swap or financing charge on positions held overnight. A scalper who closes before the session ends avoids swaps; one who holds into the next session does not. The schedule should be checked at the hours the method actually trades, because spreads widen outside the London and New York overlap.

The fifth is the payout condition. A consistency test at payout, a minimum number of trading days before the first withdrawal, or a cap on the share of profit from a single instrument can each reduce what a successful attempt is worth. Those conditions are not part of the challenge fee and do not appear in the comparison table, but they decide the return on the attempt.

FXIFY

FXIFY appears first because its product is built around a fast path, not because it is the cheapest per trade. The plans offer one-step and two-phase structures, and the one-step route removes a phase at the cost of a tighter effective allowance. For a scalper, that trade-off is legible: fewer sessions of exposure against less room to absorb a normal losing streak.

The loss floor on the plans compared here is trailing, which matters more to this style than to a swing method. A scalp that runs a winner for twenty pips and gives back eight has raised the failure point without banking the gain. A trader who closes at a fixed target and does not let positions run is barely affected; a trader who scales out is affected on every winner. Price the plan as though the fee were higher if the second description fits.

One-off billing is the advantage. A high-frequency attempt can take longer than expected, and a fixed fee means the calendar does not add cost. That is worth more to a scalper than a deeper discount on a subscription, because the subscription charges for exactly the delay a marginal method produces.

Before buying, confirm the spread during the sessions the method trades, the commission per lot, and whether the trailing floor follows intraday equity or end-of-day equity. Those three details change the expected cost more than the entry price.

The5ers

The5ers sells a ladder of programmes rather than a single challenge, from low-cost entry programmes to higher-stakes evaluations. For a scalper, the relevant question is which programme is being compared, because the balance, the loss limit and the consistency condition change between them. A brand-level comparison is meaningless here.

The static floor on most programmes is a genuine advantage for a high-frequency method. A fixed dollar distance to failure means the risk per trade can be calculated once, and a string of ordinary losses does not move the failure point closer. The lower balances on the entry programmes are the counterweight: the dollar target is smaller, but the fixed costs are not, so the cost per dollar of profit is higher.

Billing is one-off per programme, which suits a slow attempt. Payouts follow a fixed schedule rather than an on-demand request, so the first withdrawal arrives after the programme's cycle. A scalper who intends to withdraw quickly should compare cycles rather than splits, because a higher split paid later can be worth less than a lower split paid sooner.

The platform is MT5, which most scalpers already use. Execution quality during the sessions the method trades is the thing to test on a demo before buying, since the published spread is an average and the method lives in the moments when the average is wrong.

Lux Trading Firm

Lux Trading Firm sells a 1-step evaluation with a fixed drawdown and a one-off fee that is refunded after passing. It is a plain product, which is exactly what a scalper wants from the rulebook: fewer clauses to breach per trade and a failure point that does not move.

The static floor is the main attraction. A high-frequency method produces many small outcomes, and a floor that stays put converts those outcomes into a predictable drawdown profile. A trailing floor would convert the same outcomes into a moving target, with every give-back charged against the remaining room.

The caution is platform choice. A smaller platform list can mean less flexibility in execution, and for a scalper execution is the product. Test the platform's order handling, the spread at the open and the behaviour of stop orders around releases before committing to an attempt.

Billing is one-off, so a slow pass does not accumulate fees, and a full refund of the fee after passing changes the expected cost of a successful attempt to zero. The payout schedule is fixed, which is normal for the category but worth checking against the trader's own cash timeline: a cheap attempt that pays in eight weeks is not cheap for someone who needs the first withdrawal in four.

Blueberry Funded

Blueberry Funded is attached to a brokerage, which is the reason it appears on a scalping list. Brokerage-grade execution is not a marketing line for this style; it is the variable that decides whether the advertised spread is the realised spread. A firm that routes orders through its own infrastructure has more control over fills than one that resells a white-label platform.

The evaluation uses a static floor and one-off billing on the plans compared here, which keeps the arithmetic simple. A scalper can set a risk unit, count the losses the floor allows, and price the attempt without modelling a moving threshold.

The caution is that an evaluation account is not the same product as a live brokerage account, even under the same brand. Spreads, commission and execution can differ between the two, and the published schedule for the evaluation is the one that applies. Test the evaluation conditions specifically rather than assuming the brokerage's retail terms carry over.

For a method with a small average win, the difference between a 0.5-pip and a 0.9-pip realised spread is the difference between a positive and a negative expectancy. That makes a demo test on the actual evaluation server more valuable than any comparison table.

MENT Funding

MENT Funding competes on low-cost static evaluations. The combination of a static floor and one-off billing is the right shape for a high-frequency method, and the low entry price makes it a reasonable place to test a scalp strategy against a published rulebook for the first time.

The caution is public documentation. A smaller firm publishes less third-party evidence about payout timing, dispute handling and rule enforcement, so the buyer relies more heavily on the terms page. That is manageable, but it should be done before payment: read the payout clause twice, save a dated copy, and ask support a mechanics question to see how quickly and precisely it answers.

The low fee also changes the arithmetic of failure. If a method is marginal on cost, the expected number of attempts is high, and a low fee per attempt softens the multiplier. That is a real advantage, provided the rules do not add a consistency condition or a minimum-day requirement that lengthens each attempt.

Check the commission and spread schedule as carefully as the fee. A low entry price attached to a wide spread is not a cheap evaluation; it is a cheap ticket to an expensive attempt.

GOAT Funded Trader

GOAT Funded Trader offers a large headline balance at a low entry cost, which is attractive on a comparison page and needs care on a scalping desk. A large balance increases the dollar value of a mistake as well as the dollar value of a win, and a trailing floor means the failure point follows the equity high.

For a scalper who closes at a fixed target and does not let winners run, the trailing mechanic is manageable. For one who scales out over twenty or thirty pips, it is expensive: each give-back raises the floor without banking the gain. The method, not the fee, decides which description applies.

The large balance is useful if the risk unit is sized in dollars rather than percentages. A $200 risk unit on a $200,000 account is 0.1% and behaves like a $100 unit on a $100,000 account, but the fixed costs are the same in dollars. In that sense a large account is more efficient per dollar of cost, provided the trader does not scale the risk unit up with the balance.

Confirm the exact floor definition before buying: intraday trailing, end-of-day trailing and static are three different products, and the difference between them is larger than any discount on the fee.

Smart Prop Trader

Smart Prop Trader offers a static evaluation with explicit consistency rules. The explicitness is the feature: a rule that is written down can be planned for, while one that is discovered at payout cannot. For a scalper, that means the best-day share can be modelled before the attempt rather than after.

A consistency clause and a high trade count interact in a specific way. The clause caps the share of total profit that may come from one day, so a single exceptional session can leave a profitable evaluation unpaid or partially paid. A scalper can manage this by reducing size after a strong start or by spreading profit across more sessions, but both are costs against the method's natural behaviour.

The static floor is the counterweight. A fixed failure point means the risk unit does not have to shrink as profit accrues, which is what a high-frequency method needs to compound a small edge. The two features together describe a product that suits a disciplined, moderately sized scalp method rather than a high-variance one.

Read the consistency rule at the payout stage, not only during the evaluation, and confirm whether it is assessed on balance or equity. The difference decides whether an open position counts against the day's share.

Sizing a scalp method to a static floor

Start with the floor, not the target. On a $100,000 account with a $6,000 static floor, a risk unit of 0.25% is $250, which allows twenty-four consecutive losses before the account fails, ignoring the daily stop. A unit of 0.5% allows twelve. A unit of 1% allows six. The scalp method's losing streak distribution decides which of those is survivable, and a method with a 55% win rate will produce six-loss runs often enough that the 1% unit is a bet on a good week rather than a plan.

Then apply the daily stop. If the daily stop is 5% and the risk unit is 0.5%, ten losses in a day end the session. That sounds remote until the trade count is considered: a scalper taking forty trades a day will have days where the method's normal variance produces a cluster. The daily stop should be at least three times the expected worst-day loss, or the rule becomes the binding constraint rather than the floor.

Then price the attempt. Multiply the risk unit by the number of trades the method needs to reach the target, add the spread and commission per trade, and compare the total with the fee. If the running cost exceeds the fee, the firm's cost schedule is the purchase decision, not its price list. That is the case for most scalp methods, which is why this ranking leads with cost per trade.

Finally, size for the payout. The funded account's profit split applies to profit after costs, and the first withdrawal may be subject to a minimum or a seasoning period. A method that produces a small average profit per week should be matched to a plan whose payout cycle is short enough that the first withdrawal arrives before the method's edge decays.

What to confirm before paying

Confirm the loss-floor definition on the exact plan: static, intraday trailing or end-of-day trailing. The three produce different failure points for the same trade sequence, and the difference is larger than any promotional discount.

Confirm the daily stop basis. An equity-based stop counts open positions; a balance-based stop does not. A scalper holding several positions at once is more exposed to the first definition than a single-entry trader.

Confirm the news rule. List the restricted events, the window around each one, whether pending orders count, and whether existing positions must be closed. A high trade count makes an accidental breach almost certain if the rule is not mapped in advance.

Confirm the consistency condition and where it applies. A clause assessed at payout changes the value of a pass; a clause assessed during the evaluation changes the strategy. Both should be known before the first trade.

Confirm the cost schedule during the sessions the method trades: spread, commission per lot, swap on overnight positions, and any platform or data fee. Compare the total cost of a hundred trades across the firms on the shortlist, not the entry fees.

Confirm the payout terms: split, minimum withdrawal, processing time, first-payout condition, and whether the split applies before or after commissions. Save the terms page as a dated file, because the version that applied on the purchase date is the only one that can settle a later dispute.

Questions scalpers ask

Is scalping allowed on prop firm evaluations?

On most forex and CFD evaluations, yes, subject to the plan's rules on news trading, maximum lot size, automation and consistency. Some plans restrict high-frequency strategies explicitly, so read the prohibited-strategies section rather than assuming a generic allowance.

Do I need a static floor to scalp?

No, but a trailing floor changes the cost of every give-back. A method that closes at a fixed target is barely affected. A method that scales out or trails a stop is charged for the behaviour on each winner, and should price the fee accordingly.

Is a one-step plan better for scalping?

It is faster, not easier. A one-step plan usually carries a tighter allowance, so the same risk unit produces a higher probability of a breach. Compare the expected number of purchases, not the number of phases.

How many trades should I take on an evaluation?

Enough to reach the target with the method's normal expectancy, and no more. Extra trades add cost and rule exposure without adding expected profit. If the method needs hundreds of trades to reach the target, the cost schedule is the deciding factor in the purchase.

Does a monthly plan ever suit a scalper?

Only if the attempt is expected to finish inside one billing cycle and the plan can be cancelled without penalty. A high-frequency method with a marginal edge often takes longer than planned, and each extra month is a fee that a one-off plan would not charge.
