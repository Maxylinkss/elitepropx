Legitimacy check · reviewed October 2026

Is a prop firm passing service legit?

The question sounds like one question and is really three, each with a different answer and a different person who decides it. Whether the arrangement is lawful is a matter for the law where you live. Whether it is allowed is decided by your firm's client agreement. Whether the provider will deliver is decided by evidence you can check before paying. Only the third is under the provider's control, and buyers routinely settle the first two in their head and skip the one that actually costs them the account.

Key takeaways

  • "Legit" collapses three tests: lawful, permitted by your firm, and honestly delivered. Passing one says nothing about the other two.
  • The decisive document is your firm's client agreement, not the provider's sales page and not a support reply that contradicts it.
  • The asymmetry is the whole risk: a prohibited pass can produce a funded account that is closed at the first payout request, which is worse than a failed evaluation.
  • Most fake proof fails a simple test — it cannot show a firm-issued certificate matching your firm, your account size and a date that respects the firm's minimum day count.
  • A provider can be entirely honest and still be the wrong purchase for you, because the fee buys a completed test rather than an income.
  • The safe version of the decision takes about an hour: read two clause families, get one answer in writing, check one certificate.

Three questions hiding inside "is it legit?"

First: is it lawful? In most countries, paying another person to trade an account you own is a private service contract, and there is no statute that says an evaluation must be traded by the person who bought it. What does cross into illegality is narrower and uglier — completing identity checks for somebody else, presenting a nominee as the account holder to a firm that has asked who is trading, or handling client money without a licence. A passing service that keeps the account in your name, leaves verification to you and takes a fee rather than a share of your capital is not doing any of that.

The consequence of that legal position is worth stating, because it explains the rest of this page. These providers are not regulated financial firms. There is no authorisation regime for "we will pass your evaluation", no capital requirement, no ombudsman and no compensation scheme. Your protection is the payment method and the provider's willingness to be named, which is a commercial safeguard rather than a legal one.

Second: is it permitted? This is the question that decides whether the money survives, and it is answered by a document you already have access to — the client agreement attached to the account you are about to buy. Firms are private businesses. When an agreement forbids third-party trading, their remedy is contractual: close the account, decline the payout, keep the fee. Nothing about that is unlawful, and nothing about a provider's honesty prevents it.

Third: will the provider do what it says? A delivery question, answered by evidence rather than argument. Certificates, payout records, the demo week, a client you can speak to, a business you can find. The rest of this page is mostly about this one, because it is the only part a buyer can verify before parting with money.

The trap is the order. Almost every disappointed buyer got a satisfactory answer to the third question and never asked the second, which is why the passing service page puts the terms check before the fee and refuses the job when the clause forbids it.

Does your firm actually allow it?

Firms are not consistent, and the inconsistency is not random: it usually follows how the account is monitored rather than how the rule is worded. Four positions cover almost every agreement in the market.

What the agreement saysWhat it means for a bought passWhat to do
Permits third-party or managed tradingThe arrangement is inside the rules; every other clause still appliesKeep the clause and the reply
Silent on the questionSilence is not permission; the firm can decide at payoutAsk in writing, treat no answer as no
Prohibits sharing access or multiple usersEnforced from access logs rather than from a stated ban on servicesTreat as a prohibition
Prohibits it on the funded accountThe pass is accepted and the payout is notTreat as a prohibition

The third row is the one people miss. An agreement may never use the words "passing service" and still forbid the practicalities — one login, one location, one device, no nominee. Those clauses exist to stop account sharing and they are enforced by looking at where a session comes from, which is exactly the trace a service leaves. A provider that has read hundreds of agreements knows which firms police this and which do not, and that knowledge is worth more of the fee than any claim about pass rates.

The fourth row is the cruel one. A firm can permit assistance during the evaluation and require the funded account to be traded by its holder. The pass is then genuine, the funded account is real, and the payout request is where the arrangement is discovered and refused. This is why the question has to be asked twice, about two different stages, rather than once about the challenge.

The fifteen-minute check. Open the client agreement and search for: third party, authorised, nominee, managed, on your behalf, multiple users, account sharing, location, VPN. Read every hit, including the ones in the funded-stage section. Then ask support a question that can only be answered about your situation: "May another person trade this evaluation account, and may they also trade the funded account if it is issued?" Save the reply.

One caution about that reply. A support message is evidence of the firm's intent; it is not an amendment to the contract. Where the two conflict, the agreement wins, and a firm that changes hands or rewrites its terms can leave an old message meaningless. A reply of "we cannot comment on that" is a refusal with polite wording, and the risk disclosure says the same thing in fewer words.

What the catch really is

There is a catch, and it is not the one the marketing implies. Nobody is stealing the fee. The catch is that the product is narrower than the phrase "pass your challenge" suggests, and the narrowness is where the disappointment lives.

The fee buys an attempt, not an income. What arrives after a successful evaluation is a funded account with its own conditions: a profit threshold before the first withdrawal, a minimum number of qualifying days, a consistency cap, sometimes a reduced size for the first cycle. Those conditions are untouched by who traded the test. A buyer who reads "funded" as "earning" has bought the wrong thing, and that misreading is the most common complaint in the category.

The account can die after it is funded. This is the real downside and it deserves the sentence in full: an evaluation passed in breach of a third-party clause can produce a funded account that is closed when the first payout is requested, months later, leaving the firm's fee and the service fee both spent and nothing recovered. The fee was for work performed, so it is not refunded. That is a worse position than failing the challenge on your own.

You skip the practice. An evaluation is a rehearsal with a small audience. A trader who pays for the pass has not spent three weeks learning how they behave at 4% drawdown with a target in sight. That lesson is then delivered on the funded account, where it is more expensive, which is why the passing guide is worth reading even when somebody else is doing the trading.

The provider's other clients become your risk. A service running the same setup on several evaluations at the same firm at the same time creates correlated positions it does not control and you did not choose. Firms that treat correlated exposure as one position — and many do — may read that pattern as coordinated trading, which most agreements forbid. Your own conduct can be spotless and the pattern still belongs to your account.

You pay first, to an unregulated business. A chargeback is a remedy for non-delivery. It is not a remedy for "the pass worked and the account was voided", and it is not a remedy for a provider that answered every question pleasantly and then went quiet. The refund page covers what is and is not recoverable, and the distinction between a delivery failure and a disappointing outcome is the whole of it.

You are buying an average. A provider's completion rate describes its whole book, across firms and sizes and rulebooks. Your attempt is one case, and the case where the rulebook is unusual is precisely where an average stops being informative. Ask what happened on attempts at your firm specifically, and expect a real answer rather than a rate.

How a fake pass is manufactured

Fraud in this niche is rarely elaborate. Five shapes account for nearly all of it, and each one is defeated by the same habit: ask for the artefact that the firm issues, not the one the provider produces.

The demo account. A profitable equity curve from a broker demo looks identical to a profitable evaluation, because the platform draws the same line either way. What distinguishes them is not the shape but the account header — server, account number, account type — which is why a screenshot cropped to the chart proves nothing at all.

The edited page. A dashboard saved locally, a number changed in the developer tools, a photograph of a monitor at an angle. All of it takes minutes and none of it survives a request for the firm's own confirmation email, which is generated by the firm's system rather than by the provider's browser.

The borrowed certificate. A real certificate from a real client, name redacted. This is the most convincing fake and the easiest to break, because a certificate carries the account size, the firm, the stage and the dates. Ask for one at your firm and your size, then check the elapsed days against the firm's published minimum day count. A "passed in six days" claim on a plan requiring ten qualifying days is arithmetic, not opinion.

The impossible timeline. Marketing that promises a fixed completion window without asking which firm you are on. Completion is set by the firm's day count and target, not by the provider's skill, and a provider quoting a number before knowing the rulebook is quoting a sales figure.

The number without a denominator. "Hundreds of passes" is not a track record; it is a numerator. The informative version is attempts and completions over a stated period, with resets counted in the denominator. This site publishes roughly one reset per twenty managed evaluations and a completion rate near 95% including retries, and both numbers are only meaningful because the second one is stated alongside the first — the reviews and outcome record keeps the resets in the same table as the passes for that reason.

Four artefacts, and what each one proves

Verification is not a feeling about a provider's website. It is a small set of documents, each of which proves something specific and nothing more. Knowing which is which prevents both gullibility and pointless suspicion.

ArtefactWhat it establishesWhat it does not
Firm-issued certificateThat account completed that stage at that firm on those datesWho was at the keyboard
Stage change in your own loginThe account exists in your name and the stage advancedThat a payout will be approved
Dated payout recordMoney moved from the firm to a clientThat your firm will treat your account the same way
A client you can contactThe account was real and the experience was as describedAnything statistical — one client is an anecdote

The certificate is the workhorse. Every serious firm issues one when an evaluation is verified, and the layout is recognisable because it comes from the firm rather than from the provider. Redacting the client's name is normal and correct. Redacting the account size, the firm, the stage or the dates is not — those four fields are the evidence, and a certificate missing them is a decoration.

The strongest check available to a buyer is not documentary at all. A service that will run its plan on a demo account for a week, with the login in your hands, is showing you the work rather than the outcome, and no screenshot can imitate a week of decisions. The free demo trial exists for exactly that reason: it front-loads the doubt into a week that costs nothing instead of a payout request that costs everything.

One more test costs nothing and is rarely applied. Ask what happens on the evaluations that fail. A provider with genuine passes has stories about resets, ambiguous rules and platform outages, because those are unavoidable in this work. A provider with a flawless record is describing a product. The passed challenges page publishes certificates from FX2 Funding at $100,000, The5ers at $50,000 and Alpha Capital Group at $200,000 with names removed and dates intact, and the reset log sits beside them rather than in a footnote.

Why a real provider can charge $220

Price is the part buyers trust least, and it is the part with the clearest arithmetic behind it. A flat fee across every account size is a strange-looking business until the cost structure is laid out.

The work does not scale with the size of the account. A $200,000 evaluation has a bigger target in dollars and the same target in percentage, and the process that reaches it is the same process used on a $25,000 account. What scales is the firm's fee, which the client pays directly and the service never touches. That is why one price can cover every size without being a loss leader at the top end.

Retries are covered, which sounds generous and is mostly a statement about marginal cost. At a completion rate near 95% including retries, roughly one engagement in twenty consumes a second attempt that earns nothing extra. That attempt costs time rather than another fee, so the price absorbs it. A business that instead promised a cash refund on failure would be absorbing a real cost, and it would have to recover it somewhere — a higher headline fee, a size tier, or an upsell at the funded stage.

No profit split is the structural point rather than a perk. A provider paid from payouts has a reason to keep trading the account after the job is done, because the job only pays if the account keeps earning. That is a conflict built into the model, not a character flaw in any particular provider, and it is why an offer of "no fee until you are funded" should prompt the question of what the provider gets instead. Usually the answer is a share of what you make, or continued access to an account that is supposed to be yours.

What the fee does not include matters just as much: the firm's evaluation charge, any reset charge the firm applies, identity verification, and everything after handover. The comparison of pricing models puts the market's four structures side by side, and the honest summary is that the flat model is the cheapest route for a trader whose own attempts keep failing and the most expensive one for a trader who would have passed first time.

Behaviours that end the conversation

None of these is proof of fraud. Each is a reason to stop, because a legitimate provider can answer all of them and has no incentive not to.

  • Wants to keep trading the funded account. The evaluation was the product; the funded account is yours. A provider that offers to manage it is selling a different and much riskier arrangement.
  • Asks for the master password before the terms check. Access comes after the compliance answer, not before it.
  • Guarantees a pass, or a completion date, before being told the firm. Both are decided by the rulebook.
  • Will not name firms it has traded. Familiarity with a rulebook is the service. A refusal to name any is a refusal to be checked.
  • Quotes a size-tiered price and cannot say what a retry costs. The retry is where the economics of the model become visible.
  • Has no business name, no address and one payment route. Counterparty risk is the whole risk of prepayment, and anonymity maximises it.
  • Creates urgency. A price that expires today is a sales device; the firm's evaluation fee will still be there tomorrow.
  • Refuses to put the third-party answer in writing. The written answer is the only artefact that survives the conversation.

Run the same list against this site. Every item has a published answer: the flat fee and what it excludes, the certificates, the resets, the terms check that happens before payment, and a business you can reach by email rather than only through a chat window. The contact page explains what the reply contains and what it deliberately will not promise, including the refusal to state that your firm permits something it has not read.

The worst outcome, described plainly

The failure mode worth understanding is not a provider that takes the money and vanishes. It is the one that does the work, passes the evaluation, hands over a funded account, and watches the firm close it two months later when a payout is requested because the agreement prohibited somebody else from trading it.

In that sequence the client has paid the firm's evaluation fee, paid the service fee, spent two months on a funded account, and ends with no payout, no refund and no lever. The firm's decision is the firm's to make. The service performed the work it was paid for. A chargeback on a delivered service is decided in the provider's favour more often than not, and opening one can void a pending payout under terms that treat a dispute as a breach.

Everything that reduces that risk is cheap and happens before payment: the clause read in the agreement, the answer obtained in writing, the account bought in your own name, the funded-stage question asked separately, and a payment method that leaves a trace. None of it guarantees anything, because the firm can still change its terms or enforce a rule it had not enforced before. What it does is move the decision from the provider's marketing to a document you have read.

The decision rule

So: is a passing service legit? It is legitimate where three conditions hold together — the firm's agreement permits another person to trade the evaluation and the funded account, the provider can show firm-issued evidence at your size and firm, and the fee is structured so the provider is paid for the pass rather than for staying involved afterwards. Where any one of those fails, the purchase is not legitimate in the sense that matters, which is whether the money survives contact with the rules.

The version of the decision that fits on a card:

  1. Read the third-party clause, twice — evaluation and funded stage.
  2. Ask the firm in writing, and keep the reply; no answer means no.
  3. Ask the provider for a certificate at your firm and size before paying.
  4. Watch a demo week if it is offered, because it is the only unfakeable test.
  5. Confirm who owns the funded account, and who is paid from it.
  6. Pay in a way that can be traced, to a business that can be named.

That is an hour of work, and it converts a question about trust into a question about documents. A provider that finds the hour unreasonable is telling you something about how it treats the rest of the arrangement.

Frequently asked about legitimacy

Is paying someone to pass a prop firm challenge legal?

In most jurisdictions the arrangement is a service contract rather than an offence: you own the account, you take the risk, and the provider is paid a fee. The illegal versions involve identity — somebody else completing verification, or a nominee presented to the firm as the trader. That is a separate question from whether your firm's agreement permits it, and the agreement is what decides whether the account survives.

Is a prop firm passing service a scam?

Some are, and the honest ones are not hard to separate. A scam needs an unfakeable claim it cannot produce: ask for a firm-issued certificate at your firm and account size, check the elapsed days against the firm's minimum, and ask what happened on the evaluations that failed. A provider that answers all three with specifics is describable. One that answers with screenshots and a pass rate is not yet a business you can evaluate.

Will my prop firm find out that somebody else traded the evaluation?

Assume it can. Firms see login locations, device identifiers and session timing, and many check a funded account more closely than an evaluation because that is where money leaves. A provider that guarantees you will not be noticed is promising something no one can promise, and the promise is a reason to walk away rather than reassurance. What can be checked is whether the agreement permits the arrangement at all.

What happens if the funded account is closed after the pass?

The firm's decision is final and the service fee is not refunded, because the work was performed. The practical response is to appeal with the session log, which supports a request but cannot compel a reversal. The way to avoid the situation is the funded-stage clause question before payment, since a firm that permits assistance on the evaluation and forbids it afterwards is the exact profile that produces this outcome.

Can I see proof of a pass before I pay anything?

Yes, and you should ask for proof that matches your own case rather than a general gallery. A certificate from your firm at your account size, with the dates intact, tells you the provider has traded that rulebook at that size. A week of demo trading tells you more, because it shows the decisions instead of the result, and it costs nothing.

Why is a flat $220 cheaper than other providers?

Because the fee covers one process applied to different account sizes, retries cost time rather than another charge, and no share of the funded account is taken. What it excludes is significant: the firm's own evaluation and reset fees are paid by you, and the funded stage is yours to trade. Providers charging more are often pricing a different promise, such as a refund or a larger size tier.

Do prop firms ban passing services outright?

Some name the practice, more forbid the mechanics — shared logins, multiple users, trading by a nominee — and a few are silent. Silence is the most dangerous position for a buyer, because it leaves enforcement to the firm's discretion at payout time. Read the clause that governs access rather than searching for the phrase "passing service", which many agreements never use.

Is a passing service the same as a managed account?

No, and the difference is what most agreements are actually protecting. A managed account is traded for you on an ongoing basis, usually by someone handling your capital, and it is frequently a regulated activity as well as a prohibited one. A passing service completes a defined test and returns control, which is why the handover step — credentials removed, password changed, account in your name — is the line that keeps the two apart.

Is using one cheating?

That depends on the rulebook rather than on a general principle. A firm's evaluation is a private contractual test, and the firm decides who may sit it. Where the agreement permits assistance, using it is within the rules the firm wrote. Where it forbids it, the arrangement is a breach of contract with a specified penalty, which is a practical problem before it is a moral one. The document settles it faster than the argument does.

What if the provider disappears after I have paid?

Recovery depends almost entirely on how you paid and who you paid. A traceable payment to a named business leaves options that an anonymous transfer does not. The cheap precautions are a small first engagement rather than a package, a written refund policy for non-delivery, and a check that the contact address works before money moves rather than after.
