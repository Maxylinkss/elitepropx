Apex Trader Funding vs E8 Markets: which rulebook fits your trading

Apex Trader Funding and E8 Markets are not two versions of the same product. Apex evaluates futures on a trail measured against peak equity including open profit, bills monthly per account and gives no separate daily stop on the standard evaluation. E8 Markets evaluates forex and CFD on a mostly static floor, with a percentage daily stop and a one-off fee. The market decides the session and the sizing unit; the floor decides how many attempts a method gets. Figures below describe each rulebook's shape — confirm the live plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Apex trails the floor against peak equity including floating profit, so an open winner lifts the failure point before the trade closes.
  • E8 Markets is static on most plans, which keeps a constant risk unit valid across the whole path.
  • Apex has no separate daily stop on the standard evaluation; E8 has a percentage daily stop on every plan described here.
  • Sizing is contracts against tick value on Apex and lots against pip value on E8 — the numbers do not transfer.
  • Apex bills monthly per account, so duration costs money; E8 bills once per attempt, so patience is free.
  • Both gate payouts on qualifying activity, and both review the record before releasing profit.

What each rulebook actually measures

Every evaluation measures five things at once: profit progress, total survival, behaviour inside a session, pace through the calendar, and whether the result can be converted into money. Comparisons usually foreground the first and ignore the other four, which is how traders end up choosing a product that their method cannot finish.

Apex measures survival with a threshold that follows peak equity, including open profit. That is a live measurement: a position that is $1,200 in profit has already raised the failure point by $1,200, and a retracement from that high can end the account while the trade is still open. E8 Markets measures survival with a static floor on most plans, which means the failure point is fixed from the first trade and does not move when the account earns.

The second measurement is pace. Apex applies a minimum number of trading days, which forces the attempt across a calendar. E8 usually imposes no consistency condition on the evaluation, which removes that constraint and shifts the difficulty onto the floor and the target. A trader who is fast will feel the Apex rule; a trader who is lumpy will not feel the E8 one until the funded phase.

The third is cost. Apex bills monthly per account, so the calendar is a cost centre and a slow attempt is an expensive one. E8 bills once per attempt, so a longer calendar costs nothing and patience becomes affordable. Two products with similar headline targets therefore reward opposite pacing strategies.

An intraday trail against a static floor

Model a $50,000 account with a $2,500 threshold on each side. On Apex the failure point starts at $47,500 and moves up with every new equity high, including highs created by open positions. On E8 the failure point stays at $45,000 if the floor is 10%, and it does not move when the account reaches $52,000.

The behavioural difference is largest after a good trade. On Apex, a $1,500 winner lifts the failure point to $49,000; the trader now has $2,500 of room from the high but only $1,000 from the starting balance. On E8, the same winner leaves $7,000 of room from the original start. Same result, very different next decision: the Apex trader must cut size, and the E8 trader may keep it.

The second difference is intra-trade risk. An E8 trader can hold a winner through a retracement without any rule being triggered, because the floor does not follow the unrealised high. An Apex trader cannot: the moment the position prints a new high, the floor is there, and a give-back can end the attempt before the trade is closed. That is a genuine constraint on holding for a larger move.

The third difference is what happens after a loss. On a static floor, a $1,000 loss reduces the room from $10,000 to $9,000 and the unit can stay the same. On a trail, a $1,000 loss from a $2,500 buffer leaves $1,500, and the unit must fall by a third to keep the same loss tolerance. The trail converts a loss into a sizing decision, which is useful discipline and an additional task every morning.

The practical rule for each is different. On E8, set a self-imposed drawdown stop from the equity high, because the rulebook will not supply one. On Apex, recalculate the unit from the buffer at the start of every session and treat a new equity high as a reason to reduce size rather than increase it.

Why floating profit matters on Apex

The phrase "including open profit" is the most consequential in the Apex row. It means the trail updates while a position is running, so an unrealised gain is treated as if it had been banked. A trader who is $800 up on an open trade and then watches it reverse through entry has not merely given back profit; they have consumed $800 of a floor that has already moved.

The planning consequence is that stops and targets should be set so the account never depends on an open winner. A position that is allowed to run for a large target is a position that will raise the floor substantially before it resolves, and the retracement risk grows with the unrealised gain. The safe pattern is to take partial profit at a defined level and move the stop, which locks the high and reduces the chance that a give-back becomes a breach.

There is a second-order effect on the final third of an attempt. As the account approaches the target, each new high lifts the floor and shrinks the distance from the balance to failure. A trader who has earned $4,000 of a $6,000 target and is sitting at $54,000 with a $2,500 trail has a failure point at $51,500 — $1,500 below the balance. One bad session at full size can end an attempt that is two thirds complete.

The remedy is a taper that is steeper than usual: full size until half the target, half size until three quarters, and a third afterwards. On a trail that includes floating profit, the taper is not conservatism; it is the arithmetic of a floor that moves with the position rather than with the balance.

The daily stop question

Apex's standard evaluation has no separate daily stop, which means a single session has no published limit beyond the trailing floor. E8 applies a percentage daily stop, which bounds the session independently of the total allowance. The difference changes how a bad day is absorbed: on E8 the day ends at a known figure, and on Apex the day ends when the trader decides or when the floor is reached.

No daily stop is not a licence. The trailing floor still applies continuously, so a session that loses 4% of a 5% trail has effectively ended the attempt without any rule being named. A trader on Apex has to supply the daily limit personally, and the natural choice is a figure that keeps a worst day at a third of the buffer. With a $2,500 buffer that is roughly $800, which at a $250 unit is three losses.

On E8 the published limit is usually generous relative to a sensible unit. A 5% daily stop on $50,000 is $2,500, which at a $250 unit is ten losses — more than any session should use. The personal cap of three losses still does the work, and the published limit functions as a backstop rather than a plan.

The asymmetry matters for a trader who moves between the two products. On E8, forgetting the personal cap is survivable because the firm's rule will stop the bleeding. On Apex, forgetting it means the trail is the only limit, and the trail does not care whether the loss arrived in one session or five.

One evaluation against one-step or two-phase

Apex runs a single evaluation with a trailing threshold. One target, one floor, one decision, with a minimum-day condition forcing a calendar. E8 offers both one-step and two-phase structures, so the trader chooses whether the firm tests a single sample or a repeat.

The single-stage structure is faster and less forgiving of a bad month. A trader whose method needs a trend to appear has one window to find one, and a trailing floor that moves against them in the meantime. The two-phase structure is slower and gives two windows, each with its own target, against the same floor — which is a meaningful advantage for a method with long dry spells.

The diagnostic value differs too. A single pass tells the firm and the trader that the method produced a result once. A two-phase pass tells them it produced a result twice, which is much better evidence for a funded account that is intended to run for years. For a trader who intends to withdraw once and stop, the extra phase is pure cost.

Because the fee structures also differ — Apex monthly, E8 one-off — the path choice interacts with the billing choice. A two-phase E8 path costs the same whatever the calendar, while a single-stage Apex attempt costs more every month it runs. That combination pushes toward patience on E8 and pace on Apex, which is the opposite of what the structures themselves suggest.

A $50,000 account on each

On Apex, size in contracts. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 per contract. With a $250 unit that is eight contracts, before the position cap. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100 and the same unit buys two contracts. The contract count differs by a factor of four for the same dollar risk.

On E8, size in lots. One standard lot of a USD-quoted major is about $10 per pip, so a 20-pip stop risks $200 and a $250 unit buys one lot. A 40-pip stop buys a half lot. The pip value changes with the pair and the account currency, which is why the calculation has to be run per instrument rather than remembered.

The two tables look similar in dollars and behave differently in practice. A futures position of eight micro contracts can be entered and exited in seconds with a click, and the commission is per contract per side, so the cost scales with the count. A one-lot forex position carries a spread cost that widens around releases and a swap cost if held overnight.

Both calculations need a buffer. Stops slip at the open and around releases, and the slipped fill is what the floor sees. Adding ten to twenty percent to the planned risk changes the size by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the unit — which matters more on Apex, where the trail has no daily stop to absorb an overrun.

Futures hours against forex hours

Apex trades US futures hours. Liquidity concentrates in the regular session, the overnight session is thinner, and the daily settlement marks the account to market. A trader who cannot be present during the regular session is trading a different market with wider spreads and less reliable stop fills.

E8 trades weekday forex hours, from Sunday evening to Friday evening, with the deepest liquidity in the London and New York overlap. A trader who can only watch the Asian session is trading narrower ranges and wider spreads on European crosses, and the method should be built for that window rather than for the whole day.

The calendar arithmetic is different too. Futures have defined sessions, holidays and a daily settlement that can change margin requirements without a trade. Forex has a continuous week, a Sunday open that can gap through a weekend stop, and rollover costs for positions held overnight. Neither calendar is easier; they fail in different places.

The practical test is availability. Mark the hours you can genuinely watch for four weeks and check whether the liquid block of the instrument falls inside them. If it does not, the evaluation is being traded on hope, and no rulebook comparison fixes an unavailable market.

Rithmic and Tradovate against MT5 and cTrader

Apex lists Rithmic, Tradovate and NinjaTrader — three futures platforms with no overlap with E8's stack. Rithmic is a data and routing layer valued for depth of market and speed; Tradovate is the lightest front end; NinjaTrader is the programmable one. E8 lists MT4, MT5, cTrader and Match-Trader, which is a forex and CFD stack with a browser-first option at the end.

The overlap is zero, so the choice is partly a choice of workflow. A trader with years of MT5 templates and a tested expert advisor faces a real cost in moving to a futures front end, and that cost usually exceeds any difference in evaluation fee. The reverse is also true: a trader who has built a NinjaTrader strategy does not want to rebuild it on cTrader for a slightly cheaper evaluation.

Order handling differs in ways that matter in fast markets. Futures orders rest at the exchange and are matched by the engine, which produces cleaner fills during volatility. Forex orders are held by the broker's server, and behaviour in a gap depends on the execution model. If the method depends on precise stop fills around releases, the futures side is structurally cleaner — and the trail on Apex makes that precision more valuable.

Data costs complete the picture. Futures data is usually billed separately from the evaluation and the depth-of-market feed is the expensive one. Forex data is usually included with the platform. A monthly Apex subscription plus a data fee is the real monthly cost of an attempt, and it can exceed the evaluation fee itself.

Subscription against one-off, in dollars

At an illustrative $150 per month plus a $50 data fee, an Apex attempt costs $200 for one month, $600 for three and $1,200 for six. At an illustrative $450 one-off, an E8 attempt costs $450 whether it takes one month or five. The break-even is around two and a half months, which is close to the typical duration of a properly sized attempt.

That makes the comparison closer than the headline suggests. A trader who passes in six weeks is better off with the subscription; a trader who takes four months is better off with the one-off. Since a correctly sized attempt usually finishes in six to ten weeks, the two are roughly equivalent in expected cost, and the decision should be made on the rulebook rather than on the price.

Retries change the arithmetic. Three attempts on Apex at three months each is nine months of subscription plus fees, which can be four or five times the headline price. Three E8 attempts cost three fees with no calendar charge. A trader who expects to need retries is better served by the one-off structure, and a trader who expects to pass first time is indifferent.

The hidden line is the cost of time itself. A monthly product creates pressure to finish, which is useful for a disciplined trader and dangerous for one who presses. A one-off product removes the pressure, which is comfortable and can produce a five-month attempt that should have been six weeks. Neither is wrong; both require the trader to supply the pace the billing does not.

Payout qualification on each side

Apex pays from the funded account after qualifying days, with the request made from the dashboard. E8 releases a profit split on a fixed schedule after qualification. The difference that matters is the time from a profitable month to cleared funds, and the conditions that can interrupt it.

Both review conduct before releasing profit: the statements, the size relative to the rules, the distribution of profit and any pattern resembling prohibited practice. A trader with a complete journal answers the review in an afternoon; a trader without one is relying on a third party's interpretation of a statement, which is a weaker position than it sounds.

Identity and payment verification sit on the critical path on both. Withdrawals usually return to the method used for the fee, so an expired card or a changed wallet triggers a manual review and a delay measured in weeks. Complete verification before the target is reached, and keep the receipt for the fee.

The comparison that matters is total time from first payment to cleared funds. On Apex that includes the subscription months plus the qualifying days plus the review. On E8 it includes the attempt plus the fixed cycle plus the review. The totals are usually closer than the marketing pages suggest, and the variance is driven by the trader's pace rather than by the firm.

Retry economics over a year

Estimate the probability of passing each product from your own record, then divide the cost by that number. If an Apex attempt costs $600 and has a 30% pass probability, the expected cost per funded account is $2,000. If an E8 attempt costs $450 and has a 35% probability, the expected cost is $1,286. The difference is larger than any fee difference and it is driven by the structure, not the price.

The lever that improves both numbers is a smaller unit. Reducing the unit by a third typically raises the pass probability by converting a normal losing streak from a breach into a drawdown. The fee stays the same, so the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade. That is the most reliable improvement available to a trader who has already chosen a product.

The second lever is the calendar. On E8, a longer attempt costs nothing and raises the chance of encountering a normal distribution of results. On Apex, a longer attempt costs money every month, so the same patience has a price and the optimal duration is shorter. The structures genuinely reward different behaviour.

The third lever is the market. A trader who is skilled in futures will underperform on a forex rulebook regardless of the arithmetic, because the sizing and session assumptions do not transfer. Choosing the market you already trade is worth more than a percentage point of expected cost.

News, gaps and the overnight session

Both products carry event risk and neither eliminates it. Futures react to central bank decisions, inflation prints and employment data, with the largest single-session ranges of the quarter. Forex reacts to the same calendar with wider spreads and a Sunday open that can gap through a weekend stop.

Restrictions vary by plan and by definition. Some rulebooks prohibit opening positions in a window around a release, some prohibit holding through it, and some prohibit profiting from the resulting spike. The clause is usually short, rarely on the marketing page and enforced at review, which makes it a delayed failure with a complete audit trail.

The conservative plan is the same on both: flatten before the scheduled release and re-enter after the first thirty to sixty minutes, once the spread has normalised. That costs some of the largest moves and removes a category of dispute. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the floor can survive if it gaps.

The overnight session is the second scheduled risk. On Apex it is thinner and the daily settlement can change margin without a trade. On E8 it is a continuous market with rollover costs. Either way, a position held across a session boundary should carry a smaller unit than a session trade, or be flattened.

Which trader belongs on which

Apex fits a futures trader who already works a Rithmic, Tradovate or NinjaTrader workflow, who can watch the US regular session, and whose method produces a tight, defined stop. The intraday trail suits a trader who takes profit at levels and does not hold for a large move, because the floor punishes give-backs during an open trade.

E8 fits a forex or CFD trader who wants a static floor, a choice between one-step and two-phase structures, and a one-off fee that makes a longer attempt affordable. It suits a trader with an MT4, MT5 or cTrader workflow and a method with a defined stop and a modest target per trade.

The tie-breaker, if both markets are accessible, is the trailing question. A trader who gives back gains will struggle on Apex and be comfortable on E8. A trader who locks profit at the close is the reverse. That single behavioural trait predicts the fit better than any comparison of targets.

If the answer is still unclear, simulate both rulebooks on the same hundred trades: apply the trail to one and the static floor to the other, with the same dollar risk. Whichever survives with the smaller drawdown is the structure the method already fits.

Red flags in either rulebook

The first is a floor type that is not stated plainly. If the difference between static and trailing is buried, assume the stricter reading, and on Apex confirm whether the trail includes open profit — the phrase changes the entire holding strategy. The second is a daily stop without a stated basis; equity and balance produce different outcomes and ambiguity usually resolves against the trader.

The third is a payout condition that depends on a discretionary review with no published criteria. Every firm reserves the right to refuse profit from prohibited activity, which is reasonable. A rulebook that leaves the definition of prohibited activity open should be priced as a risk.

The fourth is a plan that changes mid-attempt. Firms update terms, and the version in force at purchase may not be the one in force at payout. Save a dated copy at checkout and check which version governs. That file is the difference between a dispute you can win and one you cannot.

The fifth is a billing rhythm that does not match the calendar the rules require. A monthly subscription on a rulebook with a long minimum-day condition is a product that charges for the time it forces, and that combination deserves a second look before purchase.

A thirty-day plan for either

Week one is calibration: trade half the normal unit, record the buffer before every entry, and confirm how the floor behaves after a winner. On Apex the goal is to see the trail move during an open position; on E8 it is to confirm the static floor and the daily stop behave as documented.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recalculate the risk unit each morning on Apex and monthly on E8. On the trailing side this is where the floor starts to bite; on the static side it is where the target usually appears.

Week four is consolidation: reduce size, protect the result, and prepare the payout documentation. A trader who reaches the target early and then doubles size to make the pass worthwhile is the trader who loses the account in the final week, and the pattern is identical on both products.

Across all four weeks the same three numbers belong on a card: buffer, unit and daily cap. The rulebooks differ in how they move the first one; neither can do anything about a trader who does not look.

Common questions

Does Apex measure its floor on equity or balance?

The standard evaluation trails against peak equity including open profit, so an unrealised gain raises the failure point before the trade closes. Confirm the current definition for the exact plan before relying on it.

Is E8 Markets' floor static?

Static on most plans, measured from the initial balance. That keeps a constant risk unit valid, but it also removes the discipline a trail imposes, so a self-imposed drawdown stop is worth adding.

Which one has a daily stop?

E8 applies a percentage daily stop. Apex's standard evaluation has no separate daily stop, so the trailing floor is the only published limit on a bad session and a personal cap is essential.

Can an MT5 expert advisor run on Apex?

No. Apex's stack is Rithmic, Tradovate and NinjaTrader. Automation is possible through those platforms, but an MT5 expert advisor does not run on them.

Which billing model is cheaper?

It depends on duration. A one-month subscription is cheaper than a one-off fee; a six-month attempt is not. Since a correctly sized attempt usually finishes inside three months, the two are often close in expected cost.

Do both firms permit third-party trading?

That has to be confirmed in the terms for the exact plan, not assumed from the brand. Some rulebooks prohibit it, and a pass obtained against those terms can be reversed at payout.
