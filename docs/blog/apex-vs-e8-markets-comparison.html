Apex Trader Funding vs E8 Markets: which rulebook fits your trading

Apex sells a 30-day futures evaluation for a one-time fee and offers a choice between an end-of-day drawdown and a live intraday trail. E8 sells a flat-fee product with no time limit, but it sells several of them — One, Pro, Signature and Zero — and the rules differ enough that comparing "E8" as a single brand is meaningless. Both firms charge once rather than monthly, so the decision is about the drawdown model, the daily stop and the payout gate, not the billing rhythm.

Key takeaways

  • Neither firm charges a monthly evaluation subscription in 2026. Apex is a one-time 30-day purchase; E8's products are flat-fee.
  • Apex gives a choice of EOD or intraday drawdown; E8's One products use a dynamic (trailing) drawdown and Pro uses an 8% static floor.
  • The daily limit is fixed in dollars at Apex and percentage-based at E8. Pro also caps daily profit at 2%, which changes how a fast start is treated.
  • Consistency is a payout rule at both firms, but the thresholds and the stage differ: 50% at Apex, 40% on E8 One, none on E8 Pro.
  • Neither firm requires minimum trading days. Apex's clock is 30 days of access; E8's is an activity rule rather than a deadline.
  • The markets are different: US futures against forex, indices, metals and crypto CFDs. That decides the session and the sizing unit before any rule does.

What the two rulebooks measure

Two evaluations with different asset classes are separated by three questions. Does the floor move, and if so against what — closed profit, peak equity including open profit, or the day's close? Is there a session limit on top of the floor? And what has to be true before the first withdrawal?

Apex answers the first question twice. Its EOD evaluation recalculates the threshold at the close from the ending balance and holds it fixed through the next session. Its intraday evaluation moves the threshold in real time with peak balance, including unrealised profit, and never moves it down — with the caveat that on Rithmic and WealthCharts the threshold stops rising once it reaches the profit-target balance, while on Tradovate it trails for the life of the evaluation.

E8 answers it per product. E8 One uses a 4% dynamic drawdown that rises with the highest closed balance and locks permanently at the initial balance. E8 Pro uses an 8% static drawdown that never trails. Signature uses its own structure and a 35% best-day payout rule; Zero is a different model again. A trader who reads one E8 page and assumes the rest follow has the same problem as a trader who assumes Apex has one evaluation.

The third question is where the products diverge most. Apex applies a 50% best-day rule at payout on current accounts, 30% on legacy accounts. E8 One applies a 40% best-day rule on the performance account and none during the challenge; E8 Pro states that it has no consistency rule at all. Those are not small differences in the arithmetic of a withdrawal request.

Static, dynamic and end-of-day

A static floor never moves, so the distance between the balance and the failure point grows with every profitable trade. A dynamic or trailing floor moves with the high-water mark, so a give-back consumes room that a static floor would have left intact. An end-of-day trail is the middle case: it moves, but only once a day, against realised profit.

For a trader who holds through intraday noise, the ranking is static, then EOD, then live trailing. For a trader who takes profit at levels and closes within the session, the gap narrows: a live trail costs less if positions are not carried through retracements. That is why the same rule can be workable for one method and fatal for another without any difference in skill.

Apex's intraday model is the strictest of the group because it follows unrealised profit. A position that runs $900 into profit lifts the failure point before the trade closes, and a return to entry leaves the buffer $900 smaller with no realised loss. The platform exception matters: on Rithmic and WealthCharts the threshold stops at the target balance, so a trader on a 50k account is not chased by the trail beyond $53,000. On Tradovate the trail continues, which makes the final approach to target more fragile.

E8's dynamic drawdown is gentler than a live equity trail because it moves on closed balance and locks at the initial balance. A trade that runs into profit and comes back does not move the floor. That is a meaningful advantage for a swing-style method, and it is not visible in a table that simply says "trailing".

A $50,000 attempt on either

Take Apex's EOD model on $50,000: a $3,000 target, a $2,000 drawdown and a $1,000 daily loss limit. The threshold starts at $48,000 and recalculates at each close. A day that closes at $51,000 moves it to $49,000; a day that closes at $49,500 leaves it at $49,500. The daily limit is separate: losing $1,000 pauses trading for the session without closing the account.

Now take E8 One on a $50,000 account: a 6% target ($3,000), a 3% daily drawdown ($1,500) and a 4% dynamic drawdown ($2,000) that rises with the highest closed balance and locks at $50,000. A day that closes at $51,000 raises the floor to $49,000. A trade that runs to $51,500 and closes at $50,400 leaves the floor where it was, because the high-water mark is measured on closed profit.

The arithmetic looks similar and behaves differently. Apex's intraday variant would have moved the floor to $49,500 on the unrealised high; E8 One would not have moved it at all. A trader whose edge depends on holding through retracements is choosing between two different products even when the headline drawdown percentages match.

Daily stops and daily caps

Apex's EOD evaluation has a fixed daily loss limit; the intraday evaluation has none, and risk control is enforced entirely through the trailing threshold. That is an unusual combination: the model with the more forgiving floor has no session stop, while the model with the stricter floor has one.

E8 One applies a 3% daily drawdown as a hard breach, calculated from the day's starting balance. E8 Pro applies 2.5% and adds a 2% daily profit cap, which is a different instrument entirely: it limits how much can be made in a day, not how much can be lost. A trader who expects to make the target in two large sessions will find the cap binding, and a trader who trades small and often will barely notice it.

Whether a limit is measured on equity or on closed results changes its real size. A $600 floating loss has already spent the allowance under an equity measure but not under a balance measure, which is the difference between stopping out and being stopped out by the firm. Get the basis in writing for the exact account.

Two one-time fee models

Apex's help centre is explicit: an evaluation is a one-time payment for 30 days of access, there are no monthly charges, and nothing renews automatically. The Performance Account activation fee is also one-time. A trader who budgets a monthly subscription is budgeting the legacy product, not the current one.

E8 sells its products for a flat fee per attempt. The Pro page describes itself as a flat fee with daily payouts and no consistency rule; the One product is priced per attempt with optional customisation at checkout. Neither is a monthly subscription, so the old "Apex monthly against E8 one-off" framing is wrong at both ends.

Both firms have costs beyond the evaluation. Apex charges a one-time activation fee when a passed evaluation becomes a Performance Account, and market data is billed by the platform or data provider. E8 includes market data in the account for its Classic Markets product, but leverage and instrument access depend on the market selected at checkout. The budget line that matters is the all-in cost of an attempt, not the headline fee.

Contracts against lots

Apex sizes in contracts. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100. Apex's evaluation caps are fixed: 4 contracts on $25k, 6 on $50k, 8 on $100k and 12 on $150k, and they do not scale during the evaluation.

E8 sizes in lots, with leverage set by instrument class: 1:30 on forex, 1:15 on indices and metals, 1:1 on crypto for the Classic Markets product. The lot calculation depends on the pair and the account currency, so the risk per trade has to be computed in the platform rather than assumed from a standard contract value.

The practical consequence is that an Apex trader thinks in ticks and contracts, while an E8 trader thinks in lots and pip value. Both arrive at a dollar risk per trade, but the arithmetic is not transferable. A trader moving between the two should rebuild the sizing table rather than convert it.

Deadlines and activity rules

Neither firm requires a minimum number of trading days. Apex states it on both evaluation pages and allows a pass in a single session. E8 One imposes no time limit but requires at least one trade, opened and closed, every 60 days. E8 Pro publishes no minimum-day rule.

The clocks are therefore different in kind. Apex's 30 days is a hard deadline: an unfinished evaluation expires and a new purchase is needed. E8's 60-day rule is an activity condition: a trader can take five months if they keep the account alive, but an abandoned account is closed.

That changes the pacing decision. On Apex, a plan that spreads the target over eight weeks leaves two weeks of buffer for a losing streak. On E8, the plan can be stretched, but the stretch has to include at least one trade every 60 days and the discipline not to overtrade a quiet account.

The platform stacks

Apex supports Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader among others. E8 runs on MetaTrader 5 for its Classic Markets product, with a separate environment for perpetual futures. The two stacks have almost nothing in common: different order types, different automation, different data.

That makes the platform question decisive rather than incidental. A trader with an MT5 expert advisor cannot run it at Apex. A trader with a NinjaTrader workflow cannot move to E8 without rebuilding it. The rulebook comparison only matters after that constraint is settled.

Data fees follow the platform. Futures data is usually billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. E8 includes data with the account, but the leverage schedule differs by market, so the effective risk of a position can change with the product selected at checkout.

Payout conditions

Apex applies a 50% best-day consistency rule at payout on current accounts, with 30% retained on legacy Performance Accounts. A payout request that fails the rule is refused rather than breaching the account, so the fix is more ordinary winning days before requesting again. The 50% figure is the one to plan for, not the 30% quoted in older reviews.

E8's payout rules differ by product. One requires that no single day exceeds 40% of total profit and that the requested payout is greater than 50% of the daily drawdown — on a $100,000 account with a 4% daily drawdown, that means a minimum request above $2,000. Pro states that it has no consistency rule and processes payouts daily with a 1% minimum profit. A trader comparing the two E8 products is comparing two different withdrawal experiences.

Apex and E8 both inspect the trading record before releasing money, and the questions they ask are easier to answer with a journal than with a statement alone. Record the buffer at the open and close of each session, and confirm whether the split is applied to gross or net profit.

Retry maths over twelve months

With one-time fees, the annual cost is fee times attempts. At a 30% pass probability, three attempts per funded account is a reasonable planning assumption. The lever that improves the number is a smaller unit, not a cheaper fee: cutting the unit by a third usually turns a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown.

The second lever is the model. A trader who gives back intraday profit should prefer the EOD or dynamic floor to the live trail. A trader who takes profit at levels can use the live trail without paying for the give-back. Choosing the model that matches the failure mode is worth more than a discount on the fee.

The third lever is the payout rule. A 50% best-day rule at Apex and a 40% rule at E8 One both constrain how concentrated the profit can be. A method that produces one large day a month will pass the evaluation and then wait at the payout stage, which is the most expensive outcome because the fee has already been paid.

Releases and the overnight session

The two products do not share an event calendar. Apex trades US futures, so the relevant releases are US data, central bank decisions and inventory reports. E8 trades forex and CFDs, where the calendar is global and the weekend gap is a larger risk.

E8 One allows news trading during the challenge but prohibits it in the performance account around high-impact releases, beginning five minutes before and ending five minutes after. Apex publishes conduct rules rather than a blanket news ban; the details sit in the help centre and are enforced at review.

The conservative plan is the same on both: be flat into the release and re-enter after the first reaction. On a live trail the gap can lift the floor and consume the buffer in the same move, which makes the pre-release decision a risk decision rather than a stylistic one.

Which trader belongs on which

Apex fits a trader who wants a one-time 30-day futures attempt, a choice between a fixed daily limit and a pure trail, and account sizes up to $300,000. The EOD model suits a trader who holds through intraday retracements; the intraday model suits a trader who takes profit at levels and closes within the session.

E8 fits a forex or CFD trader who wants a flat fee, no time limit, and a product family with genuinely different rules. One suits a trader who wants a 6% target with a dynamic floor; Pro suits a trader who wants an 8% static floor, a 2.5% daily stop and no consistency rule at payout.

The tie-breaker is the market and the platform, not the fee. A NinjaTrader futures trader stays at Apex. An MT5 forex trader starts with E8. Neither firm's evaluation is improved by choosing the one with the marginally lower price.

What to check before paying

The first is the exact product, not the brand: Apex EOD or intraday, E8 One, Pro, Signature or Zero. The second is the drawdown definition and whether it trails on closed or unrealised profit. The third is the daily rule, including whether it is a loss limit or a profit cap.

The fourth is the payout consistency threshold and the minimum request. The fifth is the platform support for the automation or order types the method needs. The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, third-party trading. Save a dated copy of every answer; it is the only document that settles a dispute six weeks later.

A thirty-day plan for either

Spend the opening week measuring rather than earning. Trade half the intended unit, record the buffer before every entry, and note whether the floor responds to an open winner or only to a closed one. By the end of the week the account's behaviour should be a short table: average spread, slip on stops, and the exact moment the threshold moved.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recalculate the risk unit each morning. On the live trail this is where compression is felt; on the static or dynamic floor it is where the target usually appears.

Treat the final week as administration with a small trading component: a third of the unit, documentation completed, and no attempt to improve a result that is already sufficient. On a 30-day product there is no reason to trade out the clock.

Keep three figures visible for the whole campaign: the distance to the floor, the unit that distance supports, and the session cap. E8 and Apex move those figures through different mechanisms, but both punish a trader who stops recalculating them after a good week.

Common questions

Does Apex charge monthly?

A current Apex evaluation is bought once and runs for 30 days; nothing renews automatically, and the activation fee on funding is also one-time. Only the legacy subscription accounts carry a recurring charge.

Does E8 have a static drawdown?

E8 Pro uses an 8% static drawdown. E8 One uses a 4% dynamic drawdown that rises with the highest closed balance and locks at the initial balance. The products do not share a floor.

Can an MT5 expert advisor run on Apex?

No. Apex's stack is Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader among others. Automation is possible through those platforms, but an MT5 expert advisor does not run on them.

Which payout rule is stricter?

Apex's 50% best-day rule is stricter than E8 One's 40% and applies to every current payout. E8 Pro states that it has no consistency rule, which makes it the most permissive of the four on this row.

Do both firms permit third-party trading?

That has to be confirmed in the terms for the exact plan, not assumed from the brand. Some rulebooks prohibit it, and a pass obtained against those terms can be reversed at payout.
