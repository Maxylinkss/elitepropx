How to Keep Funded Account · reviewed October 2026

How to Keep Funded Account

Most traders who lose a funded account do not lose it to a bad market. They lose it to a rule that changed after the pass, a position size that grew with the balance, a consistency condition that only applied at payout, or a withdrawal decision made in the first week. Keeping a funded account is a compliance exercise as much as a trading one — and unlike the evaluation, nobody is grading you weekly. This guide sets out what actually changes after the pass, how to size the buffer, the conduct rules that end accounts, and the review routine that catches a problem before the floor does.

Key takeaways

  • Trade the funded account at the size that survived the evaluation. Raising size after the pass is the most common self-inflicted failure.
  • Re-read the funded agreement on the day the account is issued. The floor, the daily reset, the news policy and the payout conditions can all differ from the evaluation's.
  • Take the first payout as soon as it is eligible. Unwithdrawn profit is exposed to the floor.
  • Build a buffer of two to three times your normal monthly drawdown, well above the firm's minimum balance.
  • Consistency conditions usually apply at payout, not during trading — so a concentrated profit distribution can block a withdrawal even when nothing was breached.
  • Conduct rules are enforced more strictly after the pass: account sharing, copy trading, prohibited automation and news-window violations can end a profitable account.
  • Review the account monthly against the rulebook, not just against your P&L.

What this guide is really solving

On an evaluation, the objective is unambiguous: reach the target without touching the floor, inside a fixed window. On a funded account, the objective changes completely. There is no target to reach, no deadline to beat, and no reward for finishing early. The only objective is to keep trading a rule-bound account and to withdraw from it repeatedly.

That change of objective is why so many traders pass and then lose the account within weeks. The skills overlap, but the incentives are inverted. During the evaluation, patience is a means to a target; after the pass, patience is the strategy, because the account's value is measured in payouts collected, not in peak equity reached.

Two numbers define a funded account's real value: the number of payout cycles it survives, and the average net payout per cycle. A $50,000 account that pays $700 a month for nine months is worth more than a $200,000 account that breaches in its second month after one large payout. Everything in this guide is aimed at the first number.

What changes between evaluation and funded

The funded agreement is a different document from the evaluation rulebook. In most firms it repeats most of the rules and changes a few of them, which is precisely why it gets misread: the familiar parts read as confirmation and the changes get skimmed.

ElementTypical funded treatmentWhat to confirm
Profit targetRemoved — there is nothing to completeNothing; the absence of a target is the main change
Loss floorUsually still applies, sometimes reset to the funded balanceWhether the floor is static or trails, and from which balance it is measured
Daily loss limitUsually retainedThe reset time and whether it is calculated on balance or equity
Consistency conditionFrequently introduced or tightened at payoutThe threshold, and whether it is measured at the payout request or continuously
Minimum trading daysOften replaced by a minimum payout cycleThe length of the cycle and the days required within it
News and holding rulesOften stricter than the evaluation'sWhich events, and whether weekend holding is permitted on your account type
Data or platform feeMay resume or continueWhether it is deducted from the payout or billed separately
Profit splitApplies to every payoutThe percentage on your account and whether it can increase with performance

Two rows in that table cause the majority of post-pass failures. The consistency condition is one, because it is evaluated against the shape of your profit and can block a withdrawal from an otherwise compliant month. The news and holding rules are the other, because a trader who traded freely through events on the evaluation may find that the funded account does not permit it.

Read the funded agreement on issue day, not on payout day. The differences are small in number and large in consequence, and they are only actionable before the trades that breach them.

The rules that still apply after the pass

A funded account is not an unregulated personal account. Most firms retain the loss floor and the daily stop, and add payout conditions that did not exist during the evaluation. The mental error that ends accounts is treating the pass as the end of the compliance period; in practice the pass is where compliance starts being enforced against real money.

Re-read the funded agreement on the day the account is issued and confirm five things: the floor definition, the daily reset time, the news policy, the payout conditions, and the conduct rules. Save a dated copy. If a rule is reinterpreted or changed while the account is running, the dated copy is what lets you have that conversation with evidence rather than memory.

There is also a practical category of rule that is easy to break by accident: the ones about how the account is accessed rather than how it is traded. Copy trading, shared logins, third-party automation and remote access arrangements are all conduct questions, and firms generally treat them more strictly on funded accounts than on evaluations.

The first month: a retention plan

The first funded month is where the account is most often lost, because it is the month in which expectations are least calibrated. A week-by-week plan removes most of the improvisation.

PeriodObjectiveWhat not to do
Day 1Re-read the funded agreement; set the risk unit; confirm payout eligibility rulesDo not place a trade on the first day to "test" the account
Week 1Trade at the evaluation's size and pace; bank ordinary resultsDo not increase size because the account is now "real"
Week 2Continue; begin tracking the best-day share of profit for the consistency checkDo not chase a repeat of the best evaluation session
Week 3Confirm you are on course for the first payout cycle, including minimum daysDo not add positions to accelerate the cycle
Week 4Request the first payout as soon as eligible; assess the bufferDo not hold the payout back to compound the balance

The instruction that does most of the work in that table is the one about ordinary results. A funded account is valuable because it keeps paying, and the discipline that keeps it alive is unremarkable by design. Traders who treat the funded account as an opportunity to trade more aggressively than they did on the evaluation are converting a long-dated income stream into a short-dated gamble.

The size reset problem

The most common self-inflicted failure on a funded account is a size increase. It happens for an understandable reason: during the evaluation the trader was managing the floor, and once funded they feel they have proved something. The floor, however, did not become more forgiving.

Risk per tradeLosses to reach a 5% floorEffect on payout frequencyRetention outlook
0.5% (evaluation size)10Steady, smaller payoutsHighest — one losing run is absorbable
1.0% (a common step up)5Slightly larger payoutsModerate — a normal bad week approaches the floor
2.0% (aggressive step up)2–3Higher average, far more varianceLow — two ordinary losses change the account's trajectory
5.0% (revenge sizing)1Not meaningfulNear zero

Read the table as a description of how many ordinary losses the account can absorb before the outcome is determined. At 0.5% per trade, a losing sequence is survivable and the account continues paying. At 2%, two losses consume most of the buffer and the third decides the outcome. The payout per cycle is larger in the second case, which is exactly why the incentive is misread: the trader sees a bigger cheque in exchange for a materially shorter expected life, and the exchange rate is bad.

The disciplined answer is to hold the evaluation's risk unit and let the buffer grow. A larger buffer permits a larger size later without approaching the floor, which is a size increase funded by accumulated profit rather than by optimism. That sequence — buffer first, size second — is the difference between an account that pays for a year and one that pays twice.

Withdrawal policy as a risk control

The withdrawal rhythm is a risk decision, not a cash-flow decision, and it is best made before the first funded trade rather than at the first payout request.

A trader who withdraws on every eligible cycle and leaves a fixed buffer has converted their funded account into an income stream with a defined float. A trader who withdraws nothing and lets the balance compound has an account with a higher nominal value and the same loss floor, which means a fixed percentage floor represents a larger cash loss but the same percentage buffer — the point being that compounding does not make the account safer, it only makes it bigger.

Withdrawal policyEffect on the bufferEffect on retentionSuited to
Withdraw every cycle, keep a fixed bufferStable, predictableBest — the account's risk profile never driftsTraders whose objective is income
Withdraw every second cycleGrows modestly, then resetsGoodTraders who want slightly larger payments
Withdraw the minimum, compound the restGrows slowlyModerate — the risk profile drifts upward with the balanceTraders targeting a larger account later
Withdraw nothing until a target balanceLargest exposure at any timeWorst — months of profit sit inside the floor's reachRarely justified

There is a specific failure mode in the last row worth naming. A trader who earns $6,000 on a funded account and withdraws none of it has $6,000 of realised profit inside an account that can still be closed by the floor. If the account then breaches, the entire amount is lost along with the account. Taking payouts early is not a sign of impatience; it is moving profit out of the reach of a rule that can still end everything.

Do not withdraw to the minimum. The firm's minimum balance is a limit it sets for itself, not a risk limit you should adopt. A buffer of two to three times your normal monthly drawdown is a more sensible target, because it lets the account absorb a bad month without a reset.

Sizing the buffer

The buffer is the amount of retained equity between your working balance and the firm's floor. Sizing it is a calculation, not a preference.

  1. Measure your normal monthly drawdown. Take the last three months of results and find the worst month. That figure, not your average, is the one that has to be survivable.
  2. Multiply by two, then by three. Two times the worst month is the minimum buffer; three times is comfortable. Below two, an ordinary bad month approaches the floor.
  3. Add the payout cycle length. If you can only withdraw every 21 days, the buffer has to cover 21 days of variance rather than 30.
  4. Check it against the floor in cash terms. Express the buffer as a percentage of the account and confirm it is smaller than the floor distance; otherwise the buffer is not a buffer, it is the account.

A worked example. A funded $50,000 account with a 5% floor has $2,500 of space. The trader's worst recent month was −$600. Two times that is $1,200, which is 48% of the available space — acceptable but not generous. Three times is $1,800, or 72% of the space, which leaves only $700 for the tail. That arithmetic suggests either a smaller risk unit or a withdrawal policy that leaves more retained equity, and it is the kind of conclusion that only appears when the numbers are written down.

The conduct rules that end accounts

Conduct rules are enforced against the account's operation rather than its performance, and they are the rules most often broken by profitable traders who assume good results buy tolerance.

Conduct ruleHow it is broken in practiceConsequence
Account sharingLetting another party trade, or sharing credentials for any reasonClosure and possible refusal of outstanding payouts
Third-party tradingAn assisted or outsourced arrangement the terms do not permitClosure at the payout review rather than at the time of trading
Copy trading and mirroringDuplicating an external signal stream or another trader's positionsClosure, and rejection of the profit it produced
Prohibited automationRunning an EA or bot that the terms exclude, or that exploits latency or pricingClosure and forfeiture, even when the account is profitable
News-window violationsTrading or holding positions through a restricted eventTrade voided, or account closed depending on the firm's policy
Identity and payout mismatchWithdrawing to an account not in the holder's namePayout held, then refused

The pattern across those rows is that each is discoverable, and each is checked at the point where it matters. Automation is visible in execution logs. Copy trading leaves matching timestamps. Identity mismatches surface at withdrawal. The practical defence is to ask for a written answer before doing anything in a grey area, because a profitable account that cannot be withdrawn is not a funded account — it is a display.

Keep the login private, keep the records clean, and keep the terms saved with their date. The operational discipline is as important as the trading edge after the pass, and it is the part almost nobody practises during the evaluation.

The five ways funded accounts are lost

Most failures cluster into five causes. Naming them makes them detectable in advance.

CauseEarly warning signPrevention
Size increase after the passA trade risk larger than anything taken on the evaluationHold the evaluation risk unit until the buffer supports more
Concentrated profit distributionOne day representing more than a quarter of the totalTrack best-day share weekly and stretch results across more days
Unread funded rulesYou cannot restate the payout condition without looking it upRead the funded agreement on issue day and keep the dated copy
Conduct violationAny arrangement you would not describe to the firm in writingGet a written answer before acting in a grey area
Profit left inside the floor's reachSeveral months of profit retained with no withdrawalsWithdraw on every eligible cycle and keep a fixed buffer

The monthly review routine

An evaluation gives you a weekly scoreboard. A funded account gives you nothing, so the scoreboard has to be built. Fifteen minutes a month is enough if the checklist is consistent.

  • Distance to the floor, in cash and as a percentage. If either number has fallen for two consecutive months, reduce size.
  • Best day as a share of net profit. Compare it against the payout condition. If it is close to the limit, trade more ordinary days rather than more size.
  • Buffer against the worst recent month. If the buffer is below twice your worst month, stop increasing risk until it recovers.
  • Payout eligibility and cycle position. Confirm the request will be made in the first eligible window.
  • Rule re-read. Skim the funded agreement for changes and check the firm's announcements. Rules are revised between plan generations.
  • Conduct audit. Confirm nothing about how the account is accessed has drifted: no shared credentials, no automation outside the terms, no matching external positions.

The purpose of the routine is not administration. It is to make the account's risk visible while it is still correctable. Every failure listed earlier in this article has an early warning sign, and every sign appears at least a week before the account ends. The monthly review is simply the mechanism that ensures somebody is looking.

The payout calendar, and planning around it

Payout cycles are fixed by the firm and usually cannot be accelerated. That makes the calendar a planning constraint rather than an administrative detail, because the number of cycles an account can complete is a function of how long it survives.

Cycle lengthMaximum payouts in six monthsEffect on the withdrawal strategy
14 daysAbout 13Frequent, smaller withdrawals; buffer stays stable
21 daysAbout 8–9The common middle case; each cycle is large enough to matter
30 daysAbout 6Fewer, larger withdrawals; more profit sits inside the floor between cycles
On request, subject to conditionsDepends on the conditionsConfirm what the conditions are before relying on it

The cycle length interacts with the buffer requirement in a way worth stating plainly: the longer the cycle, the longer a losing run can accumulate before the next withdrawal, and therefore the larger the buffer has to be. A trader on a 30-day cycle needs to survive a month of variance; a trader on a 14-day cycle only has to survive two weeks at a time. Where a firm offers a choice, the shorter cycle is usually the lower-risk operating choice even though the individual payments are smaller.

There is also a sequencing question in the first cycle. Minimum trading days, minimum cycle length and any qualifying-day requirement all have to be satisfied before the first request, and the requirements are cumulative rather than alternative. Reading them together on issue day, rather than discovering the interaction at the first request, is what turns the first payout from a hoped-for event into a planned one.

When to stop trading the account

Every funded account ends, and the only decision within the trader's control is whether that ending is a breach or a deliberate stop. A deliberate stop preserves the option to come back with a clean account and a documented record.

ConditionSignalAction
Floor within one risk unitDistance to the floor is less than twice the standard lossStop trading and reset deliberately if the firm permits
Buffer below twice the worst monthTwo consecutive monthly reviews show a falling bufferCut size by half rather than stopping outright
Best-day share near the payout limitOne day above a quarter of net profitKeep trading, but only ordinary size for the rest of the cycle
Three losses in a row outside the planTrades taken for reasons not in the methodStop for the week regardless of the floor
Rule ambiguity discovered mid-cycleYou cannot tell whether a position is compliantFlatten and get an answer before continuing

The last two rows are the ones traders ignore, and they are the ones that convert a survivable account into a breach. Three losses outside the plan are not a variance event; they are evidence that the method has been abandoned for the session. And a rule ambiguity mid-cycle is the worst possible time to guess, because the position that is in doubt is the one that will be reviewed.

The deliberate stop deserves a word of its own. A trader who stops an account at a buffer level they chose has lost an account and kept their process. A trader who trades until the floor arrives has lost the account and learned nothing that the next attempt will not repeat. Since the funded account's value is the payouts it produced rather than its existence, stopping early after several good cycles is a rational decision rather than a surrender.

If the account breaches anyway

Breaches happen to disciplined traders. The recovery decision matters more than the breach, and it is usually made badly because it is made in the first hour.

  1. Identify the rule that ended it. Read the firm's message or your own records and name the exact condition. "I got unlucky" is not a diagnosis; "the trailing floor caught a winner I held too long" is.
  2. Separate variance from structure. A single losing sequence that breached a correctly sized plan is variance. The same breach twice is structure, and the fix belongs in the plan rather than in the next account.
  3. Check whether any profit is still payable. Profit from a completed cycle that was already eligible may still be due. Do not assume the whole balance is lost.
  4. Do not buy the next account the same day. The fee is the cheap part of a repeat failure; the pattern is the expensive part.
  5. Write the one change you will make. A single specific change — a take-profit rule, a smaller risk unit, a hard session stop — is actionable. A general resolution to be more careful is not.

Two of those steps do the real work. Naming the rule that ended the account converts an emotional event into a mechanical one, and writing the single change converts it into something the next attempt can actually test. A trader who breaches twice with the same named cause has not had bad luck twice; they have had the same problem twice, which is a much cheaper thing to fix than it feels like at the time.

What a funded account is worth, over its lifetime

The value of a funded account is the sum of the payouts it produces, so retention and payout policy are the same decision viewed from two angles. A worked twelve-month comparison makes the point concrete.

Trading styleAverage net payoutCycles completedTotal collectedWhat happened
Conservative, 0.5% risk per trade$5509$4,950Account still running at month twelve with a healthy buffer
Moderate, stepped up to 1% after month two$9004$3,600Breached the floor in month seven during a normal losing run
Aggressive, 2% risk per trade$1,7002$3,400Breached in month three; the largest single payout, and the shortest life
Conservative with no withdrawals$00$0Compounded to a larger balance, then breached; all profit lost with the account

The pattern in that table is the single most useful thing on this page. The aggressive style produced the largest individual payout and the smallest total, because retention multiplies with time while position size multiplies only with variance. The conservative style collected the most over a year without ever taking a position it could not explain.

The fourth row is the most painful and the most avoidable. An account with a large retained balance and no withdrawals has all of that profit inside the reach of a rule that can still close everything. Withdrawing on every eligible cycle is not a lack of ambition; it is how the value of the account is actually captured.

It is worth restating the arithmetic behind that conclusion, because it is the one most often resisted. Retention is multiplicative: each additional month the account survives adds another payout cycle. Position size is not multiplicative in the same way, because it increases the probability of ending the sequence rather than lengthening it. The conservative trader in the table never had a spectacular month, and they collected roughly forty per cent more than the aggressive trader over the year — while sleeping through the same markets. The funded account rewards survival, and the payout schedule is the mechanism that converts survival into money.

Common questions

Can I lose a funded account after passing?

Yes, and most traders do. The loss floor, the daily stop, the payout conditions and the conduct rules still apply, and several of them are stricter than they were during the evaluation. Trading the funded account as though the compliance period ended at the pass is the most common cause.

Do evaluation rules apply to the funded account?

Not always. The funded agreement can change the floor definition, the daily reset, the news and holding policy, the minimum days and the payout conditions. Read it on the day the account is issued rather than on the day you request a withdrawal.

How much should I leave in the account?

A buffer of two to three times your worst recent month, and comfortably above the firm's minimum balance. The firm's minimum is its limit, not yours. Express the buffer as a share of the distance to the floor to confirm it is actually a buffer.

Should I increase position size once funded?

Not immediately. The evaluation's risk unit is the size that survived a rulebook, and the funded account is governed by the same limits. Increase size only when the buffer supports it — that is, when accumulated profit can absorb the larger loss, rather than optimism.

When should I take the first payout?

In the first eligible window. Unwithdrawn profit remains inside the reach of the loss floor, so a large retained balance is exposed to a rule that can still close the account. Taking payouts early is a risk control rather than impatience.

Why was my payout blocked when I did not breach a rule?

Most likely the consistency condition. It evaluates the shape of your profit rather than its size, so a day representing too large a share of the total can block a withdrawal from an otherwise compliant month. The remedy is more ordinary days, not more profit.

Does copy trading or an EA end a funded account?

It depends on the firm's funded terms, which are often stricter than the evaluation's. Copying an external signal stream, sharing access or running prohibited automation can close the account and forfeit the profit it produced. Get a written answer before relying on any of them.

How long can a funded account realistically last?

Expected value is typically measured in months rather than years, which is why the payout cadence matters so much. An account that pays four times before it stops is worth more than one that runs longer with no withdrawals in it. Plan for a defined number of payouts rather than an indefinite account.
