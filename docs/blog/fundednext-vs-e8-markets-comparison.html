FundedNext vs E8 Markets: which rulebook fits your trading

FundedNext and E8 Markets are compared on the rows that decide an attempt: the loss floor, the daily stop, the billing rhythm and the payout conditions. They evaluate the same broad market, so the decision turns on mechanics and commercial terms. Numbers below describe each published rulebook's shape; confirm the current plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • E8 Markets: static on most plans, one-off per attempt.
  • Daily stops: yes, percentage of balance versus yes, percentage based.

The comparison that matters is not which firm has the larger target. It is which rulebook changes your behaviour. FundedNext uses a static on the two-phase model, trailing on some one-step variants floor, yes, percentage of balance daily stop and one-off per attempt; E8 Markets uses a static on most plans floor, yes, percentage based daily stop and one-off per attempt. Those differences decide how much you can risk, how fast you can pass and what a failed attempt costs.

One menu, two plan generations

FundedNext and E8 Markets both sell one-step and two-phase forex evaluations, and both present a static floor on their standard plans. The comparison turns on the variants: FundedNext's one-step model can use a trailing floor, while E8's standard plans are usually static. A trader who compares the cheapest one-step price at both firms may be comparing a trailing product with a static one, which is not a like-for-like comparison.

The platform list is also slightly different. Both offer MT4, MT5 and cTrader, and E8 adds Match-Trader. A trader who uses Match-Trader has a concrete reason to pick E8; a trader who does not should treat the platform row as neutral and compare the floor and payout terms instead.

The cost of choosing speed

On a $100,000 account, a 10% static floor leaves $10,000 of room. A 6% one-step floor leaves $6,000. At a $500 risk unit, the difference is eight losses of room: twenty on the two-phase plan against twelve on the faster plan. If the one-step variant also trails, a $1,000 gain lifts the floor and reduces the room to $5,000 from the original balance, which is ten losses at the same size.

The fee does not change with the calendar at either firm because both bill once per attempt. That means the faster plan is not automatically cheaper across a year: a tighter floor can cause more retries, and each retry costs a full fee. A trader who expects two attempts on the fast plan and one on the slow plan should compare two fees against one, not the headline prices.

Payouts are on a fixed cycle after the first withdrawal at both firms. The consistency condition is often absent on the evaluation at E8 and varies by model at FundedNext, so a trader with an uneven distribution should read the payout terms before deciding.

Which trader fits which menu

FundedNext fits a trader who wants to choose between a one-step and a two-phase structure and is willing to read the model name carefully. E8 Markets fits a trader who wants a static floor on most plans and values the extra Match-Trader option. A trader who needs a static floor should avoid any one-step variant described as trailing, regardless of the brand.

If both offer the same static two-phase structure at a similar price, the decision comes down to operational details: payout review time, support response, and whether the platform you use is supported natively. Those are the parts of the product you will use after the pass.

Common questions

Are FundedNext and E8 Markets the same product?

No. Both sell one-step and two-phase forex evaluations, but the plan menus differ and some FundedNext one-step variants use a trailing floor. The plan name decides the mechanics.

Which firm offers Match-Trader?

E8 Markets lists Match-Trader alongside MT4, MT5 and cTrader. FundedNext lists MT4, MT5 and cTrader. Platform access is a practical tie-breaker if you already use one of them.

Does a one-step plan always cost less overall?

Not necessarily. The one-step fee is lower in time, but a tighter floor can cause more retries, and each retry is a full fee. Compare the expected total across attempts.
