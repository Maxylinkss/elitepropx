Prop Firm Minimum Deposit · reviewed October 2026

Prop Firm Minimum Deposit

A minimum deposit is the smallest amount a firm will accept before issuing an evaluation. It sounds like a simple entry price, but it is usually a different product from the evaluation fee — held under different terms, refunded under different conditions, and sometimes withdrawn only after a payout. This guide separates the three amounts traders routinely confuse, models the true cost of reaching a first payout, and shows which deposit terms are worth walking away from.

Key takeaways

  • A deposit, an evaluation fee and trading capital are three separate amounts. Confirm which one is refundable before paying.
  • The headline deposit is rarely the total cost. Resets, platform fees and payout processing can exceed it.
  • Withdrawal conditions on the deposit are part of the price. Money you cannot access is not the same as money you can.
  • Compare routes on the cost per funded dollar, not on the smallest entry figure.

The three amounts traders confuse

When a prop firm page says "start from $49", the figure can refer to at least three different things. Confusing them is the most common way traders misjudge the cost of an evaluation.

The first is the evaluation fee. This is the price of the attempt itself: you pay it, you receive access to a simulated account, and the money is normally non-refundable whether you pass or fail. It is revenue to the firm, not capital of yours.

The second is the minimum deposit. Some firms require a deposit before issuing an account, particularly on instant-funding or live-capital products. The deposit may be held as trading capital, may be refundable on request, or may be converted into the account balance. The terms decide which, and the difference is not cosmetic: a refundable deposit is an asset you can recover; a converted deposit is money at risk in the market.

The third is trading capital. This is the notional balance the firm uses to calculate targets and drawdowns. On most evaluations it is entirely simulated and you never deposit it. On some funded or instant products, part of it is your own money. The marketing often blurs the line by saying "trade up to $200,000" without distinguishing the notional balance from the cash you actually send.

The practical rule is simple: never pay an amount until you can label it as one of those rows. A $99 payment that is described as a deposit but is treated as a fee in the terms is a fee. A $200 payment that is described as a fee but is credited to your balance is a deposit. The label on the checkout page is marketing; the label in the terms is the contract.

Why firms ask for a deposit at all

Deposits exist for reasons that are partly commercial and partly operational. Understanding them helps separate a legitimate requirement from a cash-flow trick.

Commitment and adverse selection

A free evaluation attracts people who are curious rather than serious. A deposit filters for traders who intend to trade, which reduces the firm's support and platform costs. It also reduces the number of accounts that are opened, abandoned and reopened, each of which carries an administrative cost.

Funding the live account

On instant-funding products, the deposit may be the capital that backs the account. The firm is not simulating a balance; it is giving you access to a funded account with your money at risk. That is a different product from an evaluation, and the deposit is the price of admission rather than a fee for a test.

Payment processing and fraud control

Card payments can be reversed, and identity fraud is a real cost in this industry. A deposit creates a payment trail and makes chargeback abuse less attractive. This is a legitimate operational reason, but it also means the refund process can be slower than the payment process.

Working capital for the firm

Some firms use deposits as working capital. That is not automatically improper, but it changes the counterparty risk: if the firm fails, the deposit may be at risk alongside the firm's other liabilities. A deposit held in a segregated account is a different risk from a deposit spent on operations, and the terms rarely make the distinction clear.

The useful question is not "why do you charge a deposit?" but "what happens to the money while I hold the account, and what happens to it if I fail, pass, or ask for it back?" A firm that can answer those three questions in writing is behaving like a financial product. A firm that answers only the first is behaving like a checkout page.

The four numbers that decide affordability

Affordability is not the size of the first payment. It is the total cost of reaching a first withdrawal, divided by the probability of reaching it. Four numbers capture most of that total.

Number one: the entry payment

This is the deposit plus the evaluation fee, or whichever of the two the firm actually charges. It is the number on the checkout page and the least informative of the four, because it ignores everything that follows.

Number two: the expected number of attempts

If the pass rate on the plan is low, the entry payment is paid more than once. A $100 fee with a 30% pass rate has an expected cost of about $333 before resets, because the average trader needs more than three attempts. A $250 fee with a 60% pass rate has an expected cost of about $417 on the same arithmetic. The cheaper headline is not always the cheaper route.

Number three: recurring costs

Platform fees, data subscriptions and monthly charges continue between attempts. A $50 monthly platform fee on a challenge that takes three months adds $150, which can be larger than the difference between two entry fees. Futures evaluations are more likely to carry these costs than forex evaluations, because market data is licensed per user.

Number four: the cost of getting paid

Payout processing fees, currency conversion and withdrawal minimums reduce the net amount you receive. A $30 processing fee on a $300 first withdrawal is 10% of the payout. On a $3,000 withdrawal it is 1%. The fee structure matters most for small accounts, which is exactly where traders are most price-sensitive.

The sum of those rows is the honest cost of the route. It is usually one and a half to three times the headline entry payment, which is why comparing firms on the first number alone produces bad decisions. The comparison should be made on the total cost per funded dollar of exposure, not on the smallest number on the pricing page.

Three routes to a first payout, modelled

The arithmetic is easier with an example. Three traders each want a funded account and each has $500 available. The figures are illustrative and deliberately round; the point is the structure, not the specific fees.

Route A: the cheap challenge, repeated

A $99 evaluation on a $50,000 account, a 30% pass rate, and a reset fee of $79. The expected number of attempts is about 3.3, so the expected fee spend is roughly $99 plus 2.3 resets at $79, or about $281. Add three months of a $40 platform fee and a $25 payout fee, and the route costs about $426 before any profit. The headline was $99; the realistic cost is more than four times that.

Route B: the mid-priced challenge, passed first time

A $250 evaluation on the same account, a 55% pass rate, no platform fee and a $25 payout fee. The expected fee spend is about $250 divided by 0.55, or $455, plus $25, or $480. The headline is higher than Route A, but the expected total is close, and the variance is lower: fewer attempts means less time and less exposure to rule changes.

Route C: the deposit-backed instant account

A $300 deposit on an instant-funding account, no evaluation, and a payout fee of $30. The cost is $330, but the deposit may be at risk as trading capital, and the account may have stricter payout conditions. The headline is the highest of the three, and the risk profile is different: there is no evaluation to pass, but there is also no simulated buffer between the deposit and the market.

The lesson is not that one route is best. It is that the headline ranking (A cheapest, C most expensive) reverses on expected total (C cheapest, B middle, A most expensive). A trader who chooses on the headline is optimising the number that matters least. A trader who chooses on the expected total is optimising the number that actually leaves the bank account.

There is a second lesson about variance. Route A has a wide range of outcomes: one attempt at $99, or six attempts at $570. Route C has a narrow range but a different kind of risk, because the deposit is exposed to the market. Neither is inherently better; they suit different temperaments and different amounts of available capital.

Deposit versus account size

A larger account is not automatically a better buy, and a smaller deposit is not automatically cheaper. The relevant ratio is the cost per dollar of drawdown room, because drawdown room is what the account actually gives you.

Take two illustrative accounts. A $25,000 account with a $150 fee and a 10% maximum loss gives $2,500 of room for $150, or about $0.06 per dollar of room. A $100,000 account with a $400 fee and the same 10% maximum loss gives $10,000 of room for $400, or $0.04 per dollar of room. The larger account is cheaper per unit of room, even though the headline is higher. If the trader's strategy risks a fixed percentage per trade, the larger account also produces larger dollar profits for the same percentage gain, which improves the economics further.

The ratio improves with size because the fee does not scale linearly with the account. That is the argument for buying the largest account you can trade competently. The counter-argument is behavioural: a larger account produces larger dollar swings, and a trader who is not used to a $2,000 daily move may make worse decisions than one trading a $25,000 account. The optimum is the largest account on which your process is unchanged — not the largest account you can afford.

There is also a concentration issue. One $100,000 account and four $25,000 accounts can have the same total room, but the four-account structure allows sequential attempts and diversification across firms. It also multiplies the administrative overhead and the number of rulebooks to track. For a first evaluation, a single mid-sized account is usually the better learning environment; for a trader with a tested process, scaling across accounts can be more efficient than scaling the account size.

Refundability and withdrawal conditions

A refundable deposit is only as good as the conditions attached to the refund. Those conditions are where the real cost hides.

Condition one: the trigger

Some deposits are refundable on request at any time; some only after a first payout; some only if the account is closed without a breach; some never. The trigger determines whether the deposit is liquidity or a sunk cost. A deposit refundable only after a payout is effectively a performance bond, and it should be modelled as part of the cost of the challenge rather than as cash you can spend.

Condition two: the processing time

A refund that takes sixty days is not the same asset as one that takes three. If the money is needed for a second attempt, a slow refund can force a new deposit elsewhere. Ask for the stated processing time in business days, not the marketing phrase "fast refunds".

Condition three: the deductions

Processing fees, currency conversion and administrative charges can reduce the refund. A $300 deposit refunded as $275 after fees is a $25 cost, which belongs in the total. The terms should state the deductions; if they do not, ask.

Condition four: the account state

Some deposits are refundable only if the account has never been traded, or only if it is closed within a window. Others are forfeited on a breach. The account state at the moment of the refund request is part of the contract, and it is the condition traders most often discover too late.

The practical test is to write the refund clause as a sentence: "I get $X back if Y happens within Z days, minus W fees." If you cannot fill in all four variables from the terms, the deposit is not fully understood, and the safe assumption is that it is not refundable in practice.

The opportunity cost of tied-up capital

A deposit is capital that cannot be used elsewhere while it is held. That cost is easy to ignore because it does not appear on any invoice, but it is real.

Suppose a trader deposits $1,000 for a funded account and holds it for six months. If the alternative is a savings account yielding 4% annually, the foregone interest is about $20. That is small. But if the deposit is part of a trading float that could otherwise fund a second evaluation, the opportunity cost is the return on that second attempt, which can be much larger than the interest rate.

The calculation matters most for traders who are scaling. If $1,000 is tied up in a deposit, it cannot be used as the fee for a second account that might produce a faster payout. The question is not only "what does the deposit cost?" but "what else could this money be doing, and for how long?" A deposit that is refundable in a week has a low opportunity cost; one that is locked for a year has a high one, even if the principal is eventually returned.

There is a behavioural cost too. Money that is tied up in a deposit creates a sense of obligation to trade the account, which can lead to overtrading. A trader who has paid a deposit may feel they must "get their money's worth", which is exactly the mindset that breaches a daily loss limit. The cheapest deposit is not always the one with the lowest number; it is the one that does not distort your decisions.

Deposit terms and firm risk

When you pay a deposit, you are lending money to a business. The firm's solvency and the segregation of client funds are therefore part of the deposit terms, even if they are not on the pricing page.

Segregated versus operational funds

A segregated account holds client deposits separately from the firm's operating capital. If the firm fails, segregated funds are more likely to be returned. An operational account commingles deposits with the firm's expenses, which means a failure can consume them. The distinction is rarely advertised, and it is a legitimate question for support.

Jurisdiction and recourse

Where the firm is registered determines what recourse you have if a refund is refused. A firm in a jurisdiction with a accessible small-claims process is a different risk from one in a jurisdiction where enforcement is impractical. This is not legal advice, but it is a practical part of the cost: a deposit you cannot recover is a loss, regardless of the clause that says it is refundable.

Payment method and chargebacks

Card payments offer a chargeback route that bank transfers do not. That protection has limits and should not be treated as a guarantee, but it changes the risk profile of the payment. A firm that accepts cards is not necessarily safer, but the payment method gives you one more option if the terms are breached.

Age of the firm and deposit history

A firm that has been paying withdrawals for several years has a track record that a new firm does not. That does not make the new firm dishonest, but it makes the deposit riskier. If the deposit is large relative to your capital, the age and reputation of the firm should carry more weight than a slightly lower fee.

Eight deposit red flags

Most deposit problems are visible before payment if you know what to look for. These eight are the ones worth treating as disqualifying until explained in writing.

  1. The deposit is described as a fee in the terms. The label on the checkout page and the label in the contract must match. If they do not, the contract wins.
  2. No stated refund trigger. "Refundable" without a condition, a timeline and a deduction schedule is a marketing word, not a term.
  3. The deposit is refundable only after a payout that requires a consistency rule you cannot meet. The refund is conditional on a performance you may not be able to deliver, which makes it a cost.
  4. The deposit is held in an operational account. If the firm cannot say whether client funds are segregated, assume they are not.
  5. The deposit is non-refundable on a breach, and the breach rules are ambiguous. Ambiguity plus forfeiture is a bad combination.
  6. The firm requires a deposit before showing the full terms. You should be able to read the contract before paying for it.
  7. The refund processing time is measured in months with no written schedule. A slow refund is a cost even when the principal is returned.
  8. The deposit is paid to a personal account or a payment processor unrelated to the firm. This is a fraud signal, not a cost issue.

None of these is automatically fatal. A firm may have a legitimate reason for a condition, and a clear written explanation can resolve the concern. The test is whether the explanation is specific and consistent with the terms. A vague reassurance is worse than a clear "no", because it leaves you with the risk and no recourse.

A five-question decision framework

Before paying any deposit, answer these five questions in writing. If any answer is missing, the deposit is not ready to be paid.

  1. What exactly am I paying? Label it as fee, deposit or trading capital, and note which parts are refundable.
  2. What is the total cost to a first payout? Include the expected number of attempts, any reset or platform fees, and the payout cost.
  3. What is the cost per dollar of drawdown room? Divide the total cost by the maximum loss in dollars. Compare that ratio across accounts, not the headline fee.
  4. Under what conditions do I get the deposit back? Write the trigger, the timeline and the deductions as a single sentence. If you cannot, ask.
  5. What happens if the firm fails or changes its terms? Check segregation, jurisdiction and the amendment clause. Keep a dated copy of the terms.

The framework is deliberately boring. It replaces the feeling of a good deal with a set of numbers that can be compared, which is the only way to avoid paying for a headline that does not survive contact with the terms. A deposit is not expensive because it is large; it is expensive because it is unclear. The five questions are how you make it clear before the money leaves your account.

Frequently asked questions

Is a prop firm deposit the same as the evaluation fee?

No. An evaluation fee buys access to a challenge and is usually non-refundable. A deposit may be held as trading capital or returned under stated conditions. Check which one the checkout page is charging.

What is the smallest deposit a prop firm accepts?

It varies widely by firm and product, and the smallest figure is often for a small account with limited drawdown room. Compare the cost per dollar of room rather than the entry figure.

Can I get my deposit back if I fail the challenge?

Depends on the terms. Some deposits are forfeited on a breach; some are refundable only after a payout; some are refundable on request. The refund trigger is the clause to read before paying.

Why is a larger account cheaper per dollar of drawdown?

Because the fee does not scale linearly with account size. A $100,000 account often costs less per dollar of room than a $25,000 account, even though the headline fee is higher.

Are deposits held in segregated accounts?

Some firms segregate client funds and some do not. The terms rarely state it clearly, so ask support directly and keep the answer. Segregation changes the counterparty risk if the firm fails.

Does a deposit affect the profit split?

Usually not. The profit split is a separate term between you and the firm. A deposit may affect payout eligibility or refundability, but it should not change the split unless the terms say so.

Is a bigger deposit always a better account?

No. A larger account gives more room and better cost per dollar, but it also produces larger dollar swings. The best account is the largest one on which your process and position sizing remain unchanged.
