MyFxbook-verified passing: what the proof actually shows

"Verified" is the most overused word in prop trading and the only one that can be checked independently. This page explains what a MyFxbook-verified record does and does not prove about a passing service, and the four checks that separate a real track record from a screenshot.

Key takeaways

  • A MyFxbook account is a read-only record of closed and open trades, synced from a broker — not a picture that can be edited.
  • Verification proves a trading history existed. It does not prove that the history came from a prop firm evaluation, or that it will repeat.
  • The four checks that matter are the sync method, the account age, the drawdown profile and the trade distribution.
  • Any service claiming verification should be able to show the live link, not a PDF of a link.

What "verified" means in practice

MyFxbook works by connecting to a broker or trading account with read-only access and importing the trade history. The resulting record is hosted on MyFxbook's servers and updated as trades occur. Because the service holds only read access, the account owner cannot edit history after the fact, and because the record lives on a third-party domain, it cannot be quietly adjusted when a bad month arrives.

That property — an external, read-only, time-stamped record — is the entire value. It is not that MyFxbook is authoritative about skill; it is that it is authoritative about what trades were placed and when. Most claims in this market fail on that much narrower test.

The three common fakes

First, the screenshot. A cropped image of a platform with a balance and a profit figure is unverifiable by construction, and it takes about ninety seconds in an image editor. Second, the demo account. A MyFxbook page can be attached to a demo as easily as a live account, and the distinction is shown on the account page rather than in the marketing copy. Third, the selective window. A verified account is real, but the link provided may cover only the profitable period, with an earlier link quietly retired.

All three are defeated by the same habit: follow the link, check the sync status, check the account type, and look at the full history rather than the headline percentage. None of that requires technical knowledge, and all of it takes about two minutes.

What a verified prop account would look like

A prop firm evaluation is usually traded on a firm's own platform or a broker feed under the firm's account, and not every firm's accounts can be linked to MyFxbook. Where they can, the record will show the account's balance and equity curve, the trade list with timestamps, and the drawdown profile. Where they cannot, verification has to come from the firm's own dashboard or certificate instead.

That is an important qualification and it is the reason this page does not present verification as a single standard. A certificate from the firm proves a pass on a date. A MyFxbook record proves a trading history. Neither proves the other, and a service that conflates them is either confused or hoping you are.

What verification cannot prove

An honest reading of a verified record stops at four boundaries, and every one of them matters to a buyer.

It cannot prove the outcome will repeat

A verified history is a sample. A sample of forty trades with a positive expectancy is evidence of a process; it is not a forecast. The stronger claim — that a service will pass the next evaluation — is not something a track record can support, which is why a retry clause matters more than a highlight reel.

It cannot prove the trades were placed on a funded account

Unless the account is labelled by the firm, a verified record is a trading record. The step from a profitable trading account to a passed evaluation involves a rulebook: daily limits, maximum loss, consistency at payout. A trader can be profitable and still fail an evaluation by breaching a limit, and a track record does not show which rules were respected.

It cannot prove whose trades they were

A verified account belongs to an account holder, and a service is an arrangement between people. The link proves the account exists and performed; it does not prove that the person operating it is the person who will trade your evaluation. That gap is closed by the certificate and by the funded account being issued in your name, not by the track record.

It cannot prove the absence of a rule breach

Firms audit after the fact. A record can look clean and an account can still be closed for a position held over a weekend or a strategy the terms prohibit. The only defence is reading the firm's rules before the work starts, which is why the rulebook question comes before the evidence question in any sensible due diligence.

Four checks on any verified account

Given a live link, four things are worth checking and each takes under a minute. They are ordered by how often they expose a misleading claim.

Check one: sync status and account type

MyFxbook shows whether an account is synced automatically or updated manually, and whether it is live or demo. A manual update means the owner chooses when the record advances. A demo label means the record describes a simulation, however profitable. Both are legitimate for testing a strategy and neither is evidence of a prop firm pass.

Check two: age and continuity

Look at the first trade date and the gaps. A record that starts three weeks before the screenshot and stops at the peak is a window, not a history. A record that runs through losing months is far more informative than one that does not, because the drawdown profile is what a firm's rules actually test.

Check three: maximum drawdown against the rule

Find the worst peak-to-trough decline in the record and compare it with the daily and maximum loss limits of the firm being discussed. A record with a 14% drawdown cannot have been produced on an account with a 10% maximum loss without a reset, whatever the total return says.

Check four: distribution of winning days

Sort the daily results. If 60% of the profit came from two sessions, the record describes a trader whose results depend on rare days — which is precisely what a consistency rule at payout is designed to catch. A record with a flatter distribution is more compatible with a funded account that has to survive months, not weeks.

Questions to ask before you trust any claim

  1. Can I see the live link, not a screenshot? A provider that cannot produce a clickable record is asking for trust instead of showing evidence.
  2. Which firm and which account size does it correspond to? A certificate is specific; a record is not, and the two need to match.
  3. What happened to the attempts that failed? The answer describes the denominator, and the denominator is where claims are inflated.
  4. Does my firm permit this arrangement? Ask both the provider and the firm, in writing, and keep the reply.
  5. What is the retry policy if a rule is breached? The failure case is where a client has no leverage, so it should be settled before payment.

Five questions, ten minutes, and the answers either hold together or they do not. That is a better use of an afternoon than comparing testimonials, because testimonials are written by people with a completed transaction and questions are answered by people who expect more.

Reading an equity curve: five features that matter

A verified record is usually presented as one number — total gain or percentage — and that number is the least informative thing on the page. Five features carry almost all of the useful information, and each maps onto a rule that a firm will apply.

Feature one: the shape of the drawdowns

Two records can show the same total return with completely different risk. One climbs steadily and gives back 2% at a time; the other gains 18% in a fortnight and then loses 12% before recovering. The first is compatible with a 10% maximum loss. The second cannot have been produced on such an account without a reset, and the difference is visible in the curve within ten seconds.

Look for the worst peak-to-trough decline in percentage terms, and compare it with the firm's maximum loss once floating equity is counted. If the record's worst decline exceeds the limit, the account either was not subject to that limit or was reset after the fact.

Feature two: trade frequency and holding time

Frequency tells you which rulebook the record is compatible with. A record with forty trades a day describes a scalping approach, which suits a firm with intraday rules and no overnight holding. A record with one position held for a week describes a swing approach, which requires an account type that permits weekend exposure. A record that mixes both without explanation describes someone who has not settled on a process.

Feature three: the win rate and the payoff ratio together

Either number alone is meaningless. A 30% win rate is excellent with a 3:1 payoff and catastrophic with 0.5:1. Reading them together tells you whether the approach survives a normal losing sequence, which is what a daily limit tests. A record with a 25% win rate and a 4:1 payoff will contain runs of six losses, and a trader using 2% risk on a 5% daily limit cannot survive a run of six losses inside a day.

Feature four: concentration by instrument

If 80% of the profit came from one instrument, the record is a statement about that instrument rather than about a general process. That is not disqualifying — specialists exist — but it changes what a buyer should expect: the same approach may not transfer to a different market, and the evaluation must be traded on the instrument the record describes.

Feature five: the flat stretches

Long flat periods — weeks with no trades or with results clustered around zero — are the most honest part of any record, because they show how the trader behaves when nothing is working. A record with no flat stretches either covers a short period or has been filtered to remove them, and the willingness to sit out is a better predictor of surviving a two-month evaluation than any win rate.

How a verified record is produced, step by step

Understanding the mechanics removes most of the mystique and most of the trust that providers ask for.

  1. The account is connected. The trader supplies read-only investor access to MyFxbook. No trading permissions are granted, which is why the record cannot be curated later.
  2. History is imported. Closed trades and balance movements are pulled from the broker or platform feed, and new trades are added as they occur.
  3. The record is published under a permanent URL. That URL is what a buyer should be given. A PDF of a screenshot is not the same artefact, however professional it looks.
  4. The account runs in public. The value of the exercise is that a bad month appears on the record as it happens, in the same place, with no opportunity to swap accounts quietly.

The practical consequence for a buyer is that the link must be live and must be the same link that was published earlier. A provider whose verification link changes each month has a verification problem, not a marketing one.

Where prop firms do not allow it

Many prop firms run proprietary platforms and do not permit external account linking. In that case the honest substitute is the firm's own dashboard, a certificate with the firm's branding and account number, and payout receipts with dates. That is weaker evidence in one respect — it depends on the provider not doctoring an image — and stronger in another, because it ties the record to a specific firm, size and date, which an external tracker does not.

Payout evidence: closing the loop

A pass is not money. The loop closes when a payout arrives, and that is the claim worth asking for first, because it is the hardest to fake and the most frequently omitted.

A useful payout record shows the date, the amount, the method and the account it relates to, with personal details redacted. Requests, approvals and receipts are all part of it. One receipt is a data point; a series of them over several months is evidence that a firm's payout process actually works for that trader, which is the thing a new client is really buying into.

It is also worth asking about payouts that did not happen. Every funded trader eventually has a request delayed, refused or reduced, and a provider who claims otherwise is describing a market that does not exist. The informative answer names a case, the rule that caused it, and what was done — which is usually nothing, because the rule was in the terms.

Matching evidence to the claim

Claims in this market come in four sizes, and each needs its own evidence. "I passed an evaluation" needs a certificate. "I passed evaluations on this firm at this size" needs several certificates with matching firm and size. "I get paid" needs payout receipts. "I can pass yours" needs the first three plus a written retry policy, because the only claim that matters to a buyer is the forward-looking one, and that can never be fully evidenced.

Ten minutes of diligence, in order

Most buyers spend their first ten minutes on price and their next ten on testimonials, which are the two least informative things available. The order below uses the same ten minutes on the only questions that change an outcome.

  1. Read the firm's rulebook first. Daily limit, maximum loss mechanic, target, minimum days, payout conditions. Two minutes, and it determines whether the rest of the conversation is worth having.
  2. Ask whether the firm permits third-party trading. In writing, to the firm. If the answer is prohibition with enforcement after funding, stop.
  3. Ask the provider for a certificate from a comparable assignment. Same firm, same account size, dated, with the account number visible.
  4. Follow any verification link rather than viewing a screenshot. Check sync status, account type, age and worst drawdown against the firm's limit.
  5. Ask what happens on a breach. The retry terms, in writing, before money moves.
  6. Ask who the funded account belongs to. The account should be issued in your name, with payouts paid to you and no split.
  7. Ask about the failure case in the last twelve months. What went wrong, which rule, and what changed as a result.
  8. Only then discuss price. Price is the last variable, not the first, because a cheap attempt on an unsuitable rulebook is the most expensive option available.

Eight steps, ten minutes, and they produce a decision that can be explained to someone else. That is the practical test of due diligence: if the reasoning cannot be written in three sentences, it was not reasoning.

What to do when a claim turns out to be wrong

Claims in this market are sometimes wrong without being fraudulent. Rules change, accounts get closed after review, a certificate that was accurate in March is misleading in June. The informative thing is what a provider does when the gap is pointed out.

Three responses are acceptable: a correction with the date, a removal of the claim, and an explanation of the rule that changed. One response is not: silence followed by a new claim. A buyer who has already paid is dependent on the first three, which is why asking about a past error is a reasonable question before paying rather than after.

Six arguments for weak evidence, examined

Providers with no verification usually offer an argument instead. The arguments recur, and each has a short answer.

"We keep our accounts private for tax reasons"

An external tracker does not disclose tax information or banking details; it shows trades. Privacy about personal finances is legitimate and unrelated to whether a read-only link can be published.

"Screenshots are between us and our clients"

A certificate with the account number redacted and the firm, size and date visible is enough to establish a pass. Unwillingness to share even that is unwillingness to be checked.

"Verification takes time to set up"

It takes about twenty minutes, once, and then updates itself. The setup cost argument describes a provider who has never done it, which is the same as having nothing to verify.

"Our results speak for themselves"

Results that cannot be inspected are claims, and claims are what the buyer is trying to avoid relying on. The phrase is a request for trust dressed as a statement of fact.

"Past performance does not guarantee future results"

Correct, and irrelevant. The claim being checked is not that the future is certain but that the past happened. That is a factual question with a checkable answer.

"Everyone in this market does it this way"

Which is precisely why the buyers who insist otherwise get treated differently. The market's low standard is an opportunity for anyone willing to meet a higher one.

Worked example: reading a record that fails the checks

A hypothetical record will make the method concrete. A provider publishes a verified account showing a 34% gain over seven weeks, a 71% win rate, and a maximum drawdown of 11.4%. The marketing describes a $100,000 evaluation passed in nineteen days.

Four checks, four problems. The drawdown of 11.4% exceeds a 10% maximum loss, so the account cannot have been that evaluation without a reset. The win rate of 71% combined with a small average win implies a payoff structure where a single loss costs several wins, which is incompatible with a 5% daily limit if two losses land in one day. The record's trade count — over nine hundred trades in seven weeks — is a scalping profile, which does not fit a nineteen-day pass on a firm with a four-day minimum unless the position sizing was tiny relative to the target. And the record shows no losing week at all, which across seven weeks is either remarkable or filtered.

None of those findings proves fraud. Together they show that the record and the claim describe different things, and the correct response is to ask which is which rather than to accept the pair as a package.

What a healthy record looks like

The same claim, done properly, would show a drawdown comfortably inside the firm's limit, a trade frequency consistent with the evaluation length, at least one flat or negative week, and a distribution of daily results that would survive a consistency check at payout. It would be accompanied by a certificate naming the firm, the size and the date, and by a payout receipt if the service claims to have been paid.

That combination is unglamorous and it is the whole point: verification is not a marketing asset, it is a reconstruction of what happened. A buyer who can reconstruct the account from the evidence knows more than a buyer who has read a testimonial page twice.

The one-line summary

A verified record is a fact about the past and a weak predictor of the future, and both halves of that sentence matter. Treating it as worthless is as wrong as treating it as a guarantee: the provider who can show one has at least done the work of being checkable, and the buyer who insists on one has eliminated the easiest category of dishonesty in the market.

The rest of the decision — whether the rulebook suits the strategy, whether the fee structure aligns the incentives, whether the funded account lands in your name — sits outside the evidence and inside due diligence. Verification tells you who you are talking to; the rulebook tells you what you are buying.

One further note on how to hold this standard without becoming impossible to please. A small provider may have three certificates and no public tracker, and three dated certificates from three different firms are stronger evidence than a single verified record of a personal account. The question to ask is not "do you have MyFxbook" but "what can I check, and can I check it before I pay". The answers to that question separate providers far more reliably than any single tool.

That is the standard applied here: what can be checked is offered before it is requested, and what cannot be checked is described as such rather than dressed up.

Frequently asked about verified passing

Can a MyFxbook record be faked?

The record itself is hosted externally and synced read-only, so the history cannot be edited after the fact. What can be misrepresented is what the account is: a demo or a personal trading account can be presented as though it were a prop firm evaluation.

Is a certificate better evidence than a verified record?

They prove different things. A certificate fixes the firm, account size and date of a pass. A verified record shows the trading that produced a result. The strongest claim uses both, plus a payout receipt.

Do prop firms allow accounts to be linked to MyFxbook?

It depends on the platform. Firms running on MT4 or MT5 can usually be linked; firms with proprietary platforms often cannot. Where linking is impossible, the firm's own dashboard and certificate are the available evidence.

Can you guarantee a pass?

No, and no one can. Evaluations contain variance that budgeting controls reduce but do not remove. The retry clause exists for exactly that reason.

What if my firm bans third-party trading?
