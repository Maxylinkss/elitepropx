Prop Firm Payout Times · reviewed October 2026

Prop Firm Payout Times

A payout time is not one number. It is four stages stacked on top of each other: qualification, review, approval and settlement. Traders compare the marketing claim — "paid in 24 hours" — against the clock on their first request and conclude they were misled. Usually they were not; they measured a different stage. This guide breaks the timeline into the parts you can actually forecast, shows the arithmetic on a $100,000 account, and lists the conditions that hold a request even when the trading was clean.

Key takeaways

  • The payout clock starts at qualification, not at the moment you click request. Days still required on the calendar are the largest and most predictable part of the wait.
  • First payouts are slower by design: identity, payment-method and trading-history checks happen once and are reused afterwards.
  • Four conditions hold a request that is otherwise compliant: an open position, an unmet minimum trading-day count, a consistency threshold, and a balance floor that must remain.
  • Fixed fees hurt small withdrawals disproportionately. A $30 flat fee on a $400 payout is 7.5%; on a $4,000 payout it is 0.75%.
  • The useful plan is a cash-flow calendar built backwards from the payout, not a hope that the rail is fast.

Two traders can request a payout on the same afternoon at the same firm on the same rail and receive the money six days apart. One of them finished the qualification window on the Friday before; the other had a position open over the weekend and a consistency threshold that only became satisfied on the Wednesday. Neither was treated unfairly. The difference was in stages they had not thought of as stages.

The four stages of a payout

Every payout passes through the same four gates. Each has its own clock, its own owner inside the firm, and its own definition of "done". Treating them as one number is what makes the process feel unpredictable.

StageWho controls itTypical lengthWhat resets it
QualificationThe rulebook0–30 days after the target is hitA trading-day or seasoning requirement; a new drawdown day
Request and reviewCompliance desk1–5 business daysAn incomplete document set; a flagged trade; a conduct query
Approval and queueFinance0–3 business daysPayment batch dates; a fraud or identity re-check
SettlementThe railInstant to 5 business daysBank cut-offs, correspondent banks, conversion steps

The marketing number is almost always drawn from the last two rows. When a firm says "24-hour payouts", it means the internal review and approval were completed in a day. It does not mean the money is in your bank account the next morning, because the rail is not the firm's process. When you forecast, add all four rows, then add the weekend.

The arithmetic on a $100,000 account

Percentages hide the size of a payout decision. On a $100,000 account with an 8% target and a 90% split, the payout is a specific dollar figure that a fee is either large or small against.

LineCalculationResult
Profit target8% of $100,000$8,000
Trader share at 90%$8,000 × 0.90$7,200
Minimum balance retainedIllustrative plan requirement$2,000
Withdrawable first request$7,200 − $2,000$5,200
Flat processor feeIllustrative $30$30
Net received$5,200 − $30$5,170
Fee as share of payout$30 ÷ $5,2000.58%
Effective split after fee$5,170 ÷ $8,00064.6%

The last row is the one traders miss. A headline 90% split became 64.6% of the profit, not because the firm changed anything, but because this example withdrew the profit once and left $2,000 behind. A second payout later recovers that retained amount and the effective split rises toward 90%. Splits are cumulative across the life of the account; a single withdrawal is a snapshot that always looks worse than the headline.

Stage one: the qualification window

Qualification is the part of the wait that is written down and therefore plannable. It usually takes one of four forms, and firms often combine two of them.

  • Immediate. The target is met and the payout can be requested the same day. This is the fastest structure and the rarest.
  • Minimum trading days. A count, often five to ten, that must be recorded before the request. A pass completed in three sessions waits for the calendar to catch up.
  • Calendar seasoning. A fixed period after the first funded trade, usually fourteen to thirty days, intended to show that the result was not a single lucky sequence.
  • Profit-cycle dates. Fixed payout dates, sometimes twice monthly, on which all eligible requests are processed together. Requesting on the wrong side of the date costs up to two weeks.

The trading-day variant is the one that catches experienced traders. It rewards slow, deliberate trading during the evaluation and then punishes it at the payout, because a disciplined trader who took four clean trades to hit target has to wait for days five through ten to register. Read that rule before the evaluation, not after, and if the count is close to your normal activity, add a few small, fully planned positions to reach it rather than letting a finished account sit idle for a week.

Calendar seasoning interacts badly with fixed payout dates. A fourteen-day seasoning period that ends two days after a payout date converts into a twenty-eight-day wait, because the next processing date is a month away. That arithmetic is worth doing on a calendar before you buy, since it can double your expected time to first cash.

Stage two: review, and what triggers a manual check

Most requests clear an automated check in hours: the target is met, the drawdown was never breached, the trading-day count is satisfied, the identity documents match. The automated path is fast because it validates a handful of fields against the account record.

Manual review is triggered by exceptions. Understanding what they are lets you avoid them rather than complain about them afterwards.

TriggerWhat the reviewer is checkingHow to avoid it
Large single-day profitWhether a consistency threshold was breached, or a prohibited event was tradedKeep the best day inside the published share and stay flat through restricted windows
Unusual trade clusteringWhether one position was split into many, or several accounts traded the same ideaTrade the account as a single independent book
New payment methodWhether the destination belongs to the verified account holderUse the same method and name as verification
Incomplete documentsWhether identity, address and tax details are currentComplete verification before the request, not during it
Technical rule flagsWhether a stop was server-side, or a platform action violated an order ruleConfirm server-side stops; avoid actions the plan forbids on the platform

A manual review is not an accusation. It is a slower path through the same gate, and the usual cause is a request that could not be matched automatically. The trader's job is to make the automatic path available: complete verification early, use a consistent payment method, and keep the trading distribution inside the published limits.

Stage three: the payment rail

The rail is where the largest single variation lives, and it is the stage the firm controls least. Each rail has a different failure mode, and the choice should follow the purpose of the money rather than the headline speed.

RailTypical settlementMain costMain risk
Local bank transfer1–3 business daysLow or none, sometimes a flat feeIntermediary banks may deduct an incoming fee
International wire2–5 business daysFlat fee plus a conversion spreadCorrespondent deductions; compliance holds at the receiving bank
Card processor1–3 business daysA percentage, often 1–3%Refund routing to a card that has since expired
Crypto transferMinutes to hoursNetwork feePrice moves between request and conversion; address errors are irreversible
Payment walletHours to 1 business dayWithdrawal fee from the walletRestricted jurisdictions and account limits on the wallet side

The conversion spread is the hidden cost in cross-currency payouts. A firm may quote a payout in dollars while the receiving account is in another currency, and the conversion can be several tenths of a percent away from the interbank rate. On a $5,000 payout, a 0.8% spread is $40 — larger than many flat fees and almost never mentioned in a comparison table.

For a first payout, the goal is completion rather than speed. Choose the rail that is least likely to be rejected by the receiving institution, confirm the name matches the verified account, and keep the reference number. Once you know the firm's rhythm, optimise the fee against the amounts you actually withdraw.

The four conditions that hold a request

These are the reasons a compliant request still waits. All four are checkable before you press the button, and three of them are frequently overlooked.

An open position. Many plans require the account to be flat, and some require no trades on the request day at all. An open swing position can therefore delay a payout by the length of the hold, regardless of the profit inside it.

An unmet trading-day count. Already covered, but it is the most common avoidable delay. The count is visible in the dashboard on most platforms; check it before requesting rather than after the request is rejected.

A consistency threshold. If the best day must be under a share of total profit, a single excellent session can make the account temporarily ineligible. The fix is arithmetic, not argument: add further winning days until the share falls below the limit, or withdraw less than the maximum.

A balance floor. Some plans require a minimum remaining balance, and the request amount must leave it intact. Requesting the full profit produces a rejection that looks like a hold and is actually a specification error.

Why the first payout is the slowest

The first request establishes facts the firm will reuse for every later one: that the account holder is who they claim, that the payment destination belongs to them, that the trading history matches the account record, and that the plan's requirements are satisfied by this style of trading. On later requests those facts are already known, so the review shrinks.

The practical consequence is a two-speed experience. A trader who plans cash flow around the interval they observed on the first payout will under-forecast the second and over-forecast the third. The better forecast uses the first payout as a one-off cost of onboarding and later payouts as the recurring rhythm.

There is a second, less obvious effect: the first payout often sets the firm's expectation of your account. Requests that are complete, documented and inside the published limits build a history that makes later reviews straightforward. Requests with missing documents or amounts that conflict with the terms create a pattern, and patterns attract manual attention.

Fee drag at different withdrawal sizes

Fixed fees are a percentage problem disguised as a fixed problem. The same $30 fee consumes a different share of a payout at each size, which means the optimal withdrawal size is usually the largest one the plan allows.

The crossover sits around $1,500: below it a percentage fee is cheaper, above it a flat fee is. If the plan offers both rails, the rational choice changes with the amount. Most traders pick one method once and keep it, which is fine for reliability and quietly expensive at the small end.

The other fee that matters is the one charged by the receiving side. An incoming wire fee of $15 on a $250 withdrawal is another 6%, and it is invisible in the firm's fee schedule. Add every fixed cost in the chain before comparing a rail against the payout size.

Building a payout calendar

Forecasting is arithmetic once the stages are separated. Work backwards from the cash you need on a date.

  1. Pick the date you want the money available.
  2. Subtract the rail's worst observed settlement, in business days.
  3. Subtract the review window the firm states, and add two business days for a first request.
  4. Subtract the qualification requirement — trading days, seasoning or the next payout date, whichever is later.
  5. The result is the date the target must be met, not the date the request is made.

A worked example: you want cash on the 30th, the rail takes three business days, the review takes two, the first request adds two more, and the plan requires fourteen days of seasoning. The target has to be met by roughly the 9th to leave a margin for a weekend and a query. Traders who work forwards — hit target, then hope — discover that the answer is a month away and blame the firm rather than the calendar.

The calendar is also a risk tool. If the payout date is fixed and known, the temptation to force a result in the final days is the single largest cause of a breach after a long, careful evaluation. Knowing the date in advance removes the reason to rush.

An illustrative sequence on a $100,000 two-phase account, with the days counted in business days. The numbers are a model, not any specific firm's schedule.

Twenty-one business days, roughly four calendar weeks, for a request that a marketing page describes as a 24-hour payout. Nothing in the sequence is a delay. The 24 hours were day 15 to day 17, and they were exactly as advertised. The remaining three weeks were qualification and the rail, both of which were published before purchase.

Compare the same account with a fixed payout date at the start of each half-month. The seasoning ends on day 14, the payout date was day 10, so the request now waits until day 25 and settles around day 30. The only thing that changed was the processing convention, and the wait grew by 40%.

When the payout is late: what to send

A late payout is resolvable when the request can be reconstructed. Send one message that contains everything a reviewer needs, rather than a series of questions.

  • The account number, the request date and the reference number.
  • The amount requested and the rail used.
  • The rule basis: which qualification requirement was satisfied and when.
  • Evidence: dashboard timestamps, the trade history export, and the verification confirmation.
  • The specific question — has it been approved, or is it held, and on which clause.

That last line matters. "Where is my money" invites a template reply. "Is the request held under a specific clause" invites a specific answer, and a specific answer is the only thing that tells you whether to wait or to correct something.

Keep the whole exchange. If a held payout later becomes a dispute, the timeline of what you asked, when you asked it and what you were told is the record that carries weight. Firms respond to documented sequences far more readily than to frustration.

Withdraw or compound: what frequency does

Payout frequency is a cash-flow decision and a growth decision at the same time, and the two pull in opposite directions. Every dollar withdrawn is a dollar not compounding inside the account; every dollar left in is a dollar exposed to the next drawdown. Which matters more depends on whether the account is being run as income or as capital.

For most funded traders it is income, and the withdrawal is the point. Leaving profit in only compounds usefully where the drawdown floor grows with the balance, because otherwise the larger balance carries the same fixed loss allowance in dollars while adding risk. On a static floor, a bigger balance is more capital against the same floor, which is a worse risk ratio rather than a better one.

CycleProfit at 2%Withdraw everythingLeave everything in
1$2,000$2,000 cash, balance $100,000No cash, balance $102,000
2$2,040$4,000 cash, balance $100,000No cash, balance $104,040
3$2,081$6,000 cash, balance $100,000No cash, balance $106,121
4$2,122$8,000 cash, balance $100,000No cash, balance $108,243

After four cycles, withdrawing everything has produced $8,000 of cash and left the account at its starting size. Leaving everything in has produced an account worth $108,243 and no cash at all. The compounding path is ahead by about $243 over four months, which is small — and it was achieved by carrying four cycles of drawdown risk on a growing balance while receiving nothing.

That arithmetic is why most funded traders withdraw on a schedule and why "let it compound" advice usually comes from people who are not relying on the income. Compounding is a real effect, but on a funded account the retained profit is exposed to the same rules as the original capital, and the floor does not usually move with it.

Three payout structures compared

The interval is only one part of the structure. The trigger, the resets and the amount available at each cycle change behaviour as much as they change the calendar.

StructureTriggerEffect on tradingEffect on cash flow
Fixed cycleA date each month or half-monthCreates a cutoff; encourages protecting profit into the datePredictable, with up to a month of delay
On demand, with a minimum-day countEligibility once the count and target are metRewards finishing early but requires continued activityFast, but the count sets the earliest date
MilestoneFirst payout after a set profit levelConcentrates the first withdrawalOne large payment, then a normal cycle

The fixed cycle is the most common and the most misunderstood. It is not a delay imposed on a finished account; it is a batching convention, and it applies to every account at once. A trader who finishes on the day after the cutoff waits a full cycle, which is why the payout date belongs in the evaluation plan from the first week rather than the last.

The on-demand structure with a count is the most flexible on paper and the most demanding in practice. Eligibility depends on continued activity, so a trader who has hit the target still has to trade to reach the count. That is the structure where the payout plan and the trading plan have to be written together.

The same plan at four account sizes

Fixed costs do not scale with account size, which makes small accounts structurally less efficient. The same 8% target, 90% split and 2% retained balance produces very different first payouts.

AccountTarget at 8%Trader share at 90%Retained balanceFirst payoutRetained as share
$25,000$2,000$1,800$500$1,30027.8%
$50,000$4,000$3,600$1,000$2,60027.8%

The retained share is constant here because the retained balance is a percentage. That is not always how plans are written. A fixed retained amount — say $2,000 regardless of size — is 55% of the first payout on a $50,000 account and 14% on a $200,000 account, which makes the smaller account far less attractive than its fee suggests.

The fee side compounds the effect. A $40 flat processor fee is 3.1% of a $1,300 payout and 0.4% of a $10,400 payout. A trader comparing two account sizes on the evaluation fee alone is comparing the wrong number; the fee as a share of the first payout is the figure that describes what the product actually costs.

Common questions

How long does a prop firm payout take from target to cash?

Four stages: qualification, review, approval and settlement. Qualification is usually the longest and ranges from zero to thirty days depending on the plan. Review is typically one to five business days, and the rail adds hours to five business days. A realistic first payout sits between one and four weeks after the target is met.

Why do firms advertise 24-hour payouts if mine took three weeks?

The advertised number normally describes internal review and approval, not the whole chain. Qualification requirements and bank settlement sit outside that window and are usually published separately in the plan terms.

Does an open position block a payout?

At many firms it does. Plans frequently require the account to be flat, and some require no trades on the request day. Close the position and wait for the settlement to register before submitting.

Can I request the full profit as my first withdrawal?

Only if the plan has no minimum remaining balance. Where a floor applies, the request amount is the profit minus the amount that must stay in the account, and requesting more is rejected as a specification error rather than held for review.

Which payment method is cheapest?

Below roughly $1,500 a percentage-based rail usually wins; above it a flat-fee rail wins. The calculation has to include fees charged by the receiving bank or wallet, which are often larger than the firm's own fee at small amounts.

Is a second payout faster than the first?

Usually yes. Identity, payment method and trading history are already verified, so the review window shrinks. The qualification requirement may still apply to each cycle, so check whether the seasoning or trading-day count resets.

What happens if a payout is held after a clean pass?

Most holds are administrative: an incomplete document, a new payment method, an unmatched trade record or a consistency threshold. Ask which clause the hold references and supply the evidence for that clause. A hold without a cited clause is worth escalating in writing.

Do weekends count toward the payout clock?

Rarely for review or settlement, which run on business days. Trading-day counts exclude weekends by definition, but seasoning periods expressed in calendar days do include them. Mixing the two is a common source of a two-day forecasting error.
