Hardest prop firm challenges · reviewed October 2026

The hardest prop firm challenges in 2026, scored by rule

Difficulty is not a feeling about a firm. It is the product of six mechanics: how the loss floor moves, whether a daily stop exists, whether consistency is enforced, how long the attempt must last, what the payout review examines and how the target compares with the allowance. Score those six and the hardest challenges are not the ones with the biggest numbers on the landing page. They are the ones that leave the least room for a normal losing streak.

Key takeaways

  • A trailing floor is harder than a static floor by roughly the size of a typical give-back, not by a fixed percentage.
  • An intraday trailing floor is harder than an end-of-day one because open profit raises the failure point while the trade is live.
  • A daily stop converts a bad day into a failed attempt, which matters more than the annual loss limit for most methods.
  • Minimum trading days make a challenge slower, not harder, but on subscription billing slower is more expensive.
  • The hardest challenge for a scalper is not the hardest for a swing trader. Difficulty is interactive.

How difficulty is scored

Each mechanic is scored from zero to three. Zero means it does not constrain a normal method; three means it is the dominant reason attempts fail. The scores are then weighted, because the mechanics do not matter equally: the floor and the daily stop decide survival, while the calendar and the payout review decide cost and timing.

A trailing floor scores three when it trails intraday and two when it trails only at the close. A static floor scores zero. A daily stop scores two when it is measured on equity and one when it is measured on realised balance, because the equity basis counts a losing position before it is closed.

Consistency scores two when it applies during the evaluation and one when it applies only at payout. Minimum days score one, because they slow an attempt without making any individual day more likely to fail, and a maximum time limit scores one for the opposite reason: it removes the option to wait for a better week.

The target-to-allowance ratio is the last input and the one most often ignored. A 10% target on a 10% floor requires the account to double its room before it can be withdrawn, which is a different task from a 5% target on the same floor. The ratio converts the headline percentages into a statement about how many risk units the trader has to earn.

Mechanic one: the moving floor

A trailing floor follows the equity high-water mark. On a $100,000 account with a 6% trail, the failure point starts at $94,000. A run to $108,000 lifts it to $101,520, which is above the starting balance. The account can now be in profit overall and still be closed for a breach, because the floor is measured from the peak rather than from the start.

The cost of that mechanic is the give-back. A method that regularly retraces a third of its open profit pays a third of its peak buffer on every cycle, and the failure point ratchets upward with each new high. A static floor never does this, which is why a static plan at the same percentage is a materially easier product for any method that holds positions.

Intraday versus end-of-day is the difference between a floor that moves while the trade is open and one that moves after the close. An intraday trail turns an open winner into a liability: the further the trade runs, the higher the failure point, so an ordinary pullback can end the account before the trader has a chance to react. An end-of-day trail allows the position to breathe during the session and only adjusts once.

The practical response is not to avoid trailing plans but to size for them. If the trail is 6% and the method's worst give-back is 3% of equity, the effective room is 3%, not 6%, and the risk unit should be halved. Traders who size from the headline percentage rather than from the effective room are the ones who describe a trailing challenge as impossible.

Mechanic two: the daily stop

A daily stop ends the attempt, or at least the day, when the loss from the day's starting equity exceeds a percentage. On a $100,000 account with a 5% daily stop, the limit is $5,000. With a $500 risk unit, that is ten consecutive losses, which sounds generous until the correlation is counted: three correlated positions at $500 each are $1,500 of simultaneous risk and can breach in a single move.

The equity basis is what makes the mechanic bite. A floating loss counts immediately, so a position that is $4,000 underwater has consumed 80% of the allowance before it is closed. The trader has not lost $4,000 yet; the rule does not care. This is the mechanic that turns a normal drawdown into a failed attempt.

The reset time adds a second trap. Forex rolls at a fixed hour, futures sessions have their own boundaries, and a position held across the boundary may be attributed to one day or the other. A trader who holds overnight should confirm which session the rule uses, because the difference can double the risk attributed to a single day.

Where there is no daily stop, difficulty falls sharply and the risk moves to the trader. A plan with a trailing floor and no daily stop returns the responsibility for ending a bad day to the person trading, and most traders are worse at that than a rule is. The absence of a daily stop is not automatically easier; it is easier to survive a single bad session and harder to survive a month of them.

Mechanic three: consistency

A consistency rule caps the share of total profit that can come from one day, one trade or one week. A common form is that no single day may exceed a stated percentage of the total profit; another is that the largest day must be below a multiple of the average. The definitions are not interchangeable and firms implement them differently.

During an evaluation, a consistency clause changes how the target must be reached. A trader who produces the whole target in three large days may satisfy the profit requirement and fail the distribution requirement, which means the attempt has to continue and the extra trading adds risk. The clause therefore converts a fast pass into a longer one.

At payout, the same clause can reduce the withdrawal rather than fail the attempt. A funded account that earns $10,000 with $4,000 on one day may be allowed to withdraw only the portion that satisfies the distribution. That is a softer penalty and a harder one to notice, because the account remains open and the trader discovers the reduction at the withdrawal stage.

The mechanic is easiest to satisfy by design rather than by correction. A daily profit cap of two or three risk units, applied from the first trade, keeps the distribution flat without requiring any change to the method. The cost is giving up the occasional large day; the benefit is that the clause never becomes the reason an otherwise successful attempt fails.

Mechanic four: the calendar

Minimum trading days set a floor on elapsed time. A five-day minimum cannot be compressed by passing in two days; the account has to remain open and trade on the required number of days. That does not make any individual day harder, but it does mean a trader who reaches the target early must keep trading, and keeping trading after the target is reached is how completed targets turn into breaches.

The safest response to a minimum-day rule is to reduce size once the target is met and trade the remaining days at a fraction of the normal unit. The objective in that phase is survival, not profit. A trader who keeps full size to "finish strong" is adding risk for no additional reward.

A maximum time limit is the mirror image. It removes the option to wait for a favourable week, which matters for methods with a low trade frequency. A ten-day limit on a method that produces two setups a week forces the trader to take marginal setups, and marginal setups are where the loss distribution widens.

On subscription billing, the calendar also has a price. A monthly plan at $150 with a ten-week attempt costs $375 in fees before any reset, while an equivalent one-off plan at $300 costs $300. The slower plan is not cheaper; it is the same product with a different payment schedule, and the schedule should be priced into the choice.

Mechanic five: the payout review

The payout review is the least documented mechanic and the one that decides what a pass is worth. It examines the trading record for size relative to the rules, distribution of profit, consistency with the stated method, and any pattern that resembles prohibited practice such as copying trades or trading on stale prices.

A hard review is not the same as a punitive one. A firm that checks the record thoroughly is protecting its own capital, and a trader with a clean journal has nothing to fear. The difficulty is for the trader whose pass depends on a pattern that is legal but unusual, such as a single instrument traded at high size for a short period.

The practical defence is documentation. A journal that records the setup, the risk, the stop and the exit for every trade turns a review into a verification rather than an investigation. That takes twenty minutes a day and is the cheapest insurance available against a delayed payout.

Read the review clause before buying, not after passing. The questions to answer are what the review examines, how long it takes, whether it applies to every payout or only the first, and what happens if a condition is missed. A firm that answers all four in writing is easier to trade with than one that answers none.

Mechanic six: target against allowance

The ratio of target to allowance is the cleanest single measure of difficulty. A 10% target on a 10% floor requires the trader to earn one full allowance before withdrawing; a 5% target on a 10% floor requires half. The first is twice as hard for any method with a fixed risk unit, because it needs twice as many net units of profit from the same room.

Expressed in risk units, a $100,000 account with a $10,000 floor and a $500 unit has twenty units of room. A $10,000 target is twenty units of profit, so the trader has to net twenty units without ever drawing down more than twenty. That is a demanding distribution, and it explains why a plan can look generous on the floor and still be hard in practice.

The ratio also reveals plans that are hard for a hidden reason. A one-step plan with a 10% target and a 6% trailing floor requires the trader to earn ten units while the effective room is six, minus the give-back. The headline numbers suggest a normal challenge; the arithmetic says the trader needs a near-perfect sequence.

A trader comparing two plans should compute the ratio before reading anything else. If one plan requires twice the profit from the same room, the fee difference is irrelevant until the probability difference is priced. Difficulty is a probability, and the ratio is its closest available proxy.

GOAT Funded Trader

GOAT Funded Trader runs a large account ladder with a trailing floor on the plans in this comparison. The ladder is the difficulty driver: larger balances carry larger dollar targets, and the trailing mechanic means the failure point rises with every new high. A trader who scales up before the method is stable finds the larger account harder in a way the percentages do not show.

The trailing floor penalises a specific behaviour: holding a winner through a pullback. On a volatile instrument, a position that runs 2% in profit and then retraces 1.5% can take the account from comfortable to close to failure without any change in the trader's plan. The response is to take partial profit earlier or to size smaller, and both reduce the expected return.

The account ladder is an advantage for a trader who has already proven the method. Moving from a small account to a larger one on the same rulebook preserves the learning, and the fee per dollar of allowance improves. For a first attempt, the larger rungs are a trap.

Check the daily-stop basis on the specific plan. A trailing floor plus an equity-based daily stop is a strict combination, and the two interact: an open winner that reverses can breach the daily stop and the trailing floor at the same time.

My Funded Futures

My Funded Futures is a single-phase futures evaluation with a trailing threshold and broad platform support, including TradingView execution. The single phase is the difficulty: there is no second stage in which accumulated profit can act as a buffer, so the whole target has to be produced from the starting balance.

The trailing threshold responds to open profit, which means a trade that runs in the trader's favour raises the failure point while it is still open. On a futures contract with a tick value of $12.50 or $50, a normal intraday swing can move the threshold by hundreds of dollars, and a reversal can breach it before the trader closes.

The monthly subscription adds a calendar cost. A slow attempt costs more each month, which pushes the trader toward a faster pace, which means larger size, which raises the breach probability. The pricing model and the rulebook push in the same direction, and that combination is what makes the plan hard.

Platform breadth is the compensating advantage. TradingView execution reduces adaptation cost for a trader already using it, and the ability to trade from a familiar chart is worth more than a small fee difference on a single-phase plan.

Blue Sky Trading

Blue Sky Trading is a futures evaluator with a trailing threshold, a daily stop and monthly subscription billing. The rulebook is short, which is not the same as easy: the trailing threshold does most of the work, and the lack of public documentation means the trader has to verify the details directly rather than infer them from a comparison.

The combination of a trailing threshold and a daily stop is the hardest part. A position that is working can raise the threshold and then reverse into the daily limit, so the trader can lose the day and the attempt on the same move. Sizing for the tighter of the two constraints is the only reliable approach.

Subscription billing makes the calendar part of the difficulty. A trader who needs three months pays three months, and the pressure to finish sooner is a pressure to trade larger. The rational response is to buy the smallest account that supports the method and accept a longer calendar rather than to press for a fast pass.

The firm's size is a factor in the decision. Smaller firms revise terms more often, so the version of the rulebook that applies at payout may differ from the one bought. Save a dated copy of the terms and check the version before each payout request.

Earn2Trade

Earn2Trade pairs a futures evaluation with an educational programme, and the fee covers the course whether or not the trader needs it. That makes the plan expensive for an experienced trader and reasonable for a beginner who would otherwise buy a course separately.

The floor trails at the end of the day rather than intraday, which is materially easier than a continuous trail. A position can breathe during the session without the failure point following it tick by tick, and the adjustment happens once, after the close. For a swing method this is the difference between a workable plan and an impossible one.

Minimum day requirements are the main calendar constraint. They force the attempt to last a set number of sessions, which prevents a fast pass but also prevents the overtrading that a fast target encourages. For a beginner, the enforced pace is a feature rather than a cost.

The educational component is where the value sits. A trader who completes the programme and trades the plan has a framework for the evaluation rather than a set of rules to survive. A trader who already has a framework is paying for material they will not use.

TickTick Trader

TickTick Trader offers trailing-threshold futures evaluations across several account sizes with monthly billing. The structure is conventional for the futures side of the market, and the difficulty comes from the combination of a small account and an intraday trail.

Small accounts have less absolute room, so the same dollar give-back consumes a larger share of the floor. A $300 retracement on a $50,000 account is 0.6% of the balance; on a $10,000 account it is 3%. The mechanic is identical and the experience is not, which is why a trader who passed a large account can fail a small one on the same method.

The monthly subscription compounds the small-account problem. The fee is a larger share of the potential payout, so the trader is paying more per dollar of target while also having less room to earn it. That is the least favourable combination in the market for a beginner.

The plan is a reasonable fit for a trader who wants to test a futures method at low absolute cost and is willing to treat the first attempt as tuition. It is a poor fit for a trader who needs the payout to cover the subscription.

AquaFunded

AquaFunded sells one-step and two-phase forex evaluations with a trailing floor on the plans compared here. The one-step product is the harder of the two, because the target has to be reached in a single attempt without the buffer that phase one provides on a two-phase plan.

The trailing floor makes the one-step plan harder still. Without a second phase, there is no stage in which accumulated profit can absorb a bad week, and the floor rises with every new high. A trader who gives back a third of open profit is effectively trading a smaller account than the balance suggests.

The two-phase plan is the more forgiving option and the better default for a first attempt. Phase one's profit sits above the floor in phase two, which reduces the effective risk per trade even though the percentages are unchanged. That buffer is the main reason two-phase plans have higher pass rates.

The choice between the two should be made on variance, not on speed. A trader with a steady distribution can use the one-step plan efficiently; a trader with lumpy results should take the two-phase path and use the buffer.

Elite Trader Funding

Elite Trader Funding runs many futures plans with frequent promotions, and the difficulty varies by plan rather than by brand. Some plans trail intraday, some trail at the close, and the account sizes and targets differ enough that a single difficulty score would be misleading.

The promotion culture is the practical hazard. A trader who buys the discounted plan without checking which trail it uses can end up with the strictest product in the family at the lowest price. The discount is real; the rulebook attached to it may not be the one the trader intended to buy.

The subscription model adds the usual calendar pressure. Frequent promotions also encourage repeated purchases, and repeated purchases are how a cheap plan becomes an expensive habit. Set a budget for attempts before the first purchase and treat each promotion as a reason to check the rulebook rather than a reason to buy.

For a trader who reads the plan terms carefully, the range of options is an advantage: it is possible to find a plan whose trail matches the method. For a trader who buys on price, the same range is a series of avoidable mistakes.

TradeDay

TradeDay uses an end-of-day trailing threshold on futures accounts with monthly billing and minimum trading days. The end-of-day trail is the easier half of the mechanic; the minimum days and the subscription are the harder half.

An end-of-day trail allows a position to be held through an intraday pullback without the failure point moving, which suits a method that holds through the session. The adjustment at the close can still catch a trader who ends the day near the high and gives back the next morning, but the intraday noise is removed from the calculation.

Minimum days force a pace. A trader who reaches the target in a week must continue for the required sessions, and continuing at full size after the target is the classic way to fail a nearly completed attempt. Reducing size to a quarter of the normal unit for the remaining days removes most of that risk.

The subscription makes the calendar expensive. A plan with a ten-day minimum and a monthly fee is effectively a two-week product with a one-month price, and the trader should compare it with a one-off plan on that basis.

FundedNext and FundedNext Futures

FundedNext sells both forex and futures products, and the two rulebooks differ enough that they should be treated as separate firms for the purpose of difficulty. The forex plans offer a choice between one-step and two-phase structures; the futures plans use a trailing threshold and monthly billing.

On the forex side, the two-phase plan is the easier product for most methods because the first phase's profit becomes a buffer. The one-step plan is faster and carries a tighter effective margin, and it is the plan that produces the most failed attempts per purchase.

On the futures side, the trailing threshold and the subscription create the same pressure as elsewhere in the market: the floor rises with open profit and the calendar costs money. A trader who can trade the US session consistently is the natural fit; a trader who cannot is paying a monthly fee for an account they rarely use.

The brand's advantage is familiarity and platform breadth. The disadvantage is that plan names do not share one rulebook, so a comparison that treats the brand as a single product will be wrong on at least one of the plans.

Hardest by trading style

For a scalper, the hardest challenge is one with a daily stop measured on equity and a consistency clause. The stop converts a normal sequence of small losses into a failed day, and the clause caps the share of profit that can come from the many small wins the method produces. A trailing floor matters less, because a scalper rarely holds a position long enough for the trail to move.

For a swing trader, the hardest challenge is an intraday trailing floor with a maximum time limit. The trail punishes the retracement that a swing position routinely experiences, and the time limit removes the option to wait for the trade to work. A daily stop matters less because the position is held across sessions and the daily attribution is ambiguous.

For a news trader, the hardest challenge is one with a news restriction and a daily stop. The restriction removes the events that produce the edge, and the stop turns the widened spread around a release into a breach. No amount of skill compensates for a rule that forbids the strategy.

For an algorithmic trader, the hardest challenge is one with a consistency clause and a manual review. The clause is hard to satisfy with a strategy that produces clustered results, and the review adds a discretionary step that a clean automated record can still fail if the documentation is poor.

The general lesson is that difficulty is a property of the pair, not the product. The right question is not "which challenge is hardest" but "which mechanic is hardest for the way I trade", and the answer usually points to a small number of plans rather than to a ranking.

Questions buyers ask

Is a harder challenge always worse value?

No. A harder plan with a lower fee can be better value for a trader whose method already satisfies the hard mechanic. The comparison that matters is expected cost, which is the fee divided by the pass probability, and a trader who is unaffected by a trailing floor does not pay the difficulty premium.

Can I pass a trailing challenge by trading smaller?

Yes, and that is usually the correct response. Halving the risk unit halves the size of the give-back in dollar terms and doubles the number of losses the floor can absorb. The cost is a longer calendar and a lower expected return, which is the price of survival.

Do minimum trading days make a challenge harder?

They make it slower rather than harder. The risk is behavioural: a trader who has reached the target keeps trading to satisfy the calendar and gives back part of the gain. Reducing size after the target removes most of that risk.

Which mechanic fails the most attempts?

The daily stop, in most rulebooks, because it converts an ordinary adverse move into an immediate breach. The trailing floor is second, because it moves the failure point while the trade is open. Consistency is third, and it more often reduces a payout than fails an attempt.

Should a beginner start with the easiest plan available?

The easiest plan is usually a static floor with no daily stop and no consistency clause, and it is a reasonable first purchase. The caveat is that an easy rulebook can teach bad habits: without a daily stop, nothing forces a trader to end a bad day, and that habit is expensive on a funded account.
