Passing service cost · reviewed October 2026

Prop Firm Passing Service Cost in 2026

A passing service quote is rarely just a number. It is a pricing model, a definition of what is included, and a set of conditions under which the provider gets paid again. In 2026 the market offers flat fees, profit shares, monthly retainers and hybrid structures, and the cheapest headline is often the most expensive route. This guide breaks down the models, calculates the total cost of ownership, and shows how to judge a quote on value rather than on the first figure.

Key takeaways

  • There are four pricing models: flat fee, profit share, monthly retainer and hybrid. Each shifts risk and incentives differently.
  • The total cost includes the firm's evaluation fee, resets, the service fee and the cost of a failed or voided account.
  • A flat fee is the easiest to compare and the least likely to reward rushing a target.
  • Judge a quote on the probability of a clean, compliant pass multiplied by the value of the funded account, not on the headline price.

The four pricing models

Passing services price their work in one of four ways. The model determines who carries the risk, how the provider is incentivised and how easy it is to compare quotes.

Flat fee per evaluation

The provider charges a fixed amount for a single evaluation, regardless of account size, firm or outcome. The fee may include retries on rule breaches or may be charged again for each attempt. The advantage is predictability: the trader knows the cost before starting, and the provider has no incentive to rush the target because the fee does not depend on the result. The disadvantage is that the provider carries the risk of a long or difficult evaluation, which may lead to a higher fee or a more selective acceptance policy.

Profit share

The provider takes a percentage of the funded account's profit, either for a fixed period or indefinitely. The headline may be low or zero, which makes it attractive to traders without cash upfront. The disadvantage is that the provider's incentive is to reach the target quickly and to keep the account producing, which can encourage larger size and less margin around the rules. The total cost can also be much higher over time: a 20% share of a year's payouts can exceed a flat fee many times over.

Monthly retainer

The provider charges a monthly fee for ongoing management, often including the evaluation and the funded account. The advantage is that the cost is spread and the provider is paid for time rather than for a single result. The disadvantage is that the trader pays whether or not the account performs, and the total cost can be high if the evaluation takes several months. A retainer also creates an incentive to keep the relationship going rather than to finish quickly.

Hybrid

A smaller upfront fee plus a profit share or a success fee. The hybrid attempts to align incentives: the provider is paid something for the work and something for the result. The disadvantage is complexity: the trader has to compare an upfront number and a future share, which are not directly comparable. A hybrid can be the best model for a trader who wants commitment from the provider, but it requires a clear written definition of the success fee and its duration.

Over a year of payouts, the profit share can cost several times the flat fee. The trader should compare the expected total cost under each model, not the first payment.

What changed in 2026

Three shifts have changed the pricing conversation since the early years of passing services. The first is competition. There are more providers than there were, and the headline fees have compressed at the low end. The second is scrutiny. Firms have become more careful about conduct and third-party trading, and payout reviews are more thorough than they were. The third is sophistication. Traders increasingly compare the total cost of ownership rather than the first payment, which has made scope and compliance part of the sales conversation.

The compression at the low end has not made the market cheaper in every case. A lower headline fee can come with a narrower scope, a per-attempt charge or a profit share that the buyer did not notice. The spread between the cheapest and the most expensive quote has widened even as the average headline has fallen, because the quotes are no longer describing the same service. A trader who compares only the first number is comparing different products.

The scrutiny shift matters more than the price shift. A firm that reviews conduct after funding can void an account that a cheap provider passed without reading the terms. The cost of that outcome is far larger than the difference between two fees. In 2026 the compliance work is not a premium add-on; it is part of the minimum viable service. A provider that does not do it is offering a cheaper product with a larger tail risk.

The third shift is informational. Traders can now find reviews, comparison tables and public discussions of providers, which makes it easier to check a claim. The information is not always reliable, but it is more abundant than it was. The practical effect is that a provider's reputation for handling retries and compliance is part of the price, even if it is not on the invoice.

What the fee should cover

A passing service is an execution business, and the fee should buy a defined scope of work. If the scope is vague, the fee is not comparable with another provider's. Six items belong in the scope.

Rule mapping

The provider should read the firm's terms and produce a written map of the rules that matter: target, maximum loss, daily loss, calendar, consistency and conduct. The map should include the measurement points and reset times, not just the percentages. This is the foundation of the work and the first thing to ask for.

Risk plan

The provider should translate the rule map into a risk plan: risk per trade, maximum aggregate exposure, session stop and a plan for the final phase near the target. The plan should be specific to the account size and the firm, not a generic template. A provider that cannot explain the size in dollars is not selling a process.

Execution

The evaluation is traded by the provider, on the firm's platform, within the firm's rules. The execution should be documented with a session log: closing equity, distance to the floor, percentage of target completed and trading days used. The log is the evidence that the process was followed.

Reporting

The provider should report progress at an agreed cadence, including flat and losing sessions. A report that only shows the winning days is a marketing update, not a process report. The trader should know how the evaluation is going before the final result.

Retries

If the evaluation breaches a rule, the provider should state whether a retry is included, and under what conditions. A retry policy is part of the price: a provider that charges again for every attempt has a higher expected cost than one that includes retries, even if the initial fee is lower.

Handover

The funded account should be handed over in the trader's name, with the credentials and a summary of the funded-phase rules: payout conditions, consistency, minimum days and KYC. The handover is the end of the service and the beginning of the trader's responsibility. A provider that keeps control of the funded account is selling a different product.

The total cost of ownership

The service fee is one line in a larger cost stack. The total cost of reaching a first payout includes the firm's fee, the service fee, resets, platform and data costs, and the cost of a failed or voided account.

The last row is the one that is rarely priced. If the firm treats the arrangement as a breach of its third-party trading clause, the funded account can be voided and the fees lost. The probability may be low, but the cost is the entire investment plus the foregone payouts. A provider that does not check the conduct clause is transferring that risk to the trader without pricing it.

The total cost should be calculated on the expected number of attempts, not on a single successful attempt. If the pass probability is 70%, the expected number of attempts is about 1.4, so the expected firm fee is 1.4 times the single fee. If the provider charges per attempt, the service fee also multiplies. The expected cost is the number to compare, not the best-case cost.

Worked comparisons

Three quotes for the same $100,000 evaluation illustrate how the models differ. The figures are illustrative and round; the structure is the point.

Quote A: flat $220, retries included

The trader pays $220 to the provider and $250 to the firm. If the evaluation breaches, the provider retries at the same firm and size without a new service fee, but the firm may charge a reset. Assuming one reset at $150, the total cost to a pass is $620. If the pass probability is 70%, the expected cost is about $886 before the payout fee.

Quote B: 20% profit share, no upfront fee

The trader pays $250 to the firm and nothing upfront to the provider. If the account produces $10,000 in the first year, the provider takes $2,000. The total cost is $2,250 plus any resets, which is more than three times Quote A. If the account produces nothing, the provider earns nothing, which is the attraction of the model for a trader without cash.

Quote C: $150 per month, unlimited attempts

The trader pays $150 a month and $250 to the firm. If the evaluation takes two months, the cost is $550 plus resets. If it takes six months, the cost is $1,150. The model is cheaper than the profit share for a fast pass and more expensive for a slow one. The provider is paid for time, so the incentive is to keep the relationship, not necessarily to finish quickly.

Why the cheapest quote can be the most expensive

A low headline price is attractive, but it often hides a cost that appears later. Five mechanisms explain the gap.

Per-attempt pricing

A provider that charges a low fee per attempt but does not include retries can be more expensive than a higher flat fee with retries. If the first attempt fails and the second costs the same, the total is double. The expected cost is the fee divided by the pass probability, not the fee.

Narrow scope

A cheap quote may cover only the execution, not the rule mapping, the risk plan or the reporting. The trader then has to do the missing work, or pay for it separately. The total cost is the quote plus the value of the missing scope.

Hidden profit share

A quote that looks flat may include a success fee or a profit share in the terms. The trader should read the agreement, not the sales page. A 5% share of payouts can be a meaningful cost over a year.

Compliance risk

A provider that does not check the firm's conduct clause may accept a firm that prohibits third-party trading. The low fee is achieved by skipping the compliance work, and the cost is a voided account. The cheapest quote can be the one that ignores the most important risk.

Quality of execution

A low fee may reflect a less experienced provider, a smaller risk budget or a less disciplined process. The cost of a failed evaluation is not just the fee; it is the time, the firm's fee and the opportunity cost. A provider that passes on the first attempt with a slightly higher fee can be cheaper than one that fails twice at a lower fee.

The correct comparison is the expected total cost of a clean, compliant pass. That number includes the probability of success, the retry policy and the compliance risk. A quote that does not allow that calculation is not a comparable quote.

The compliance cost nobody prices

The biggest cost in a passing service is not the fee. It is the possibility that the firm treats the arrangement as a breach and voids the funded account. That cost is rarely priced because it is hard to quantify, but it should be part of the decision.

Some firms prohibit third-party trading outright. Others require the account holder to place every trade personally, or restrict account sharing. The clause is usually in the conduct section of the terms, not on the challenge page. A provider that does not read it is accepting a risk that the trader will bear. A trader who does not ask is accepting it too.

The cost of a voided account is the firm's evaluation fee, the service fee, the time spent, and the foregone payouts. If the account is voided after a payout, the firm may also claw back the payment. The financial cost can be several times the service fee, and the reputational cost with the firm can be permanent.

The practical mitigation is to ask the provider what it knows about the firm's policy, and to get the answer in writing. A provider that says "the firm allows it" without a source is not providing a compliance check. A provider that says "the terms are ambiguous, here is the clause, and we will decline if you prefer" is pricing the risk honestly. The second answer is worth a higher fee.

Judging value, not price

A passing service is an investment in a funded account, so the relevant question is the expected value, not the fee. The value can be estimated with three inputs: the probability of a clean pass, the value of the funded account, and the total cost.

The probability of a clean pass

The probability is not just the chance of reaching the target; it is the chance of reaching it without a conduct breach or a payout denial. A provider that passes 80% of evaluations but ignores the conduct clause has a lower clean-pass probability than one that passes 70% and checks the terms. The trader should ask for the provider's record and the basis for the claim.

The value of the funded account

The value is the expected net payouts before the account fails or the trader stops. A $100,000 account that pays $2,000 a month for six months is worth about $12,000, not $100,000. The value should be discounted for the probability of failure and the time to the first payout.

The total cost

The total cost includes all the lines in the cost stack, not just the service fee. The expected value is the probability times the account value, minus the total cost. If the result is positive, the service is worth considering; if it is negative, the fee is too high or the probability is too low.

The calculation is rough, but it is more useful than comparing headline fees. A $220 flat fee with a 70% clean-pass probability and a $12,000 account value has an expected value of about $8,180 before costs. A $500 fee with an 80% probability has an expected value of about $9,100. The higher fee is the better value in this example, because the probability is higher and the fee is still small relative to the account value.

Pricing red flags

Some pricing structures are warning signs. These are the ones worth treating as disqualifying until explained in writing.

  1. A fee that is a percentage of the account size with no cap. The work does not scale linearly with the account, so the price should not either.
  2. A large upfront fee with no retry policy. The trader carries the full risk of a failed attempt, and the provider has no incentive to be careful.
  3. A profit share with no end date. An indefinite share can cost many times the flat fee over a year.
  4. A "guaranteed pass" with a large fee. No provider can guarantee a pass without controlling the firm's rules. The guarantee is usually conditional on terms that make it worthless.
  5. A fee that is paid to a personal account or an unrelated processor. This is a fraud signal, not a pricing issue.
  6. No written scope. If the provider cannot describe what the fee covers, the fee is not comparable.
  7. A refusal to discuss the firm's conduct clause. The provider is avoiding the compliance work, and the trader will bear the risk.
  8. A pressure to pay quickly. A legitimate provider can wait for the trader to read the agreement. Urgency is a sales tactic, not a service.

Refunds, retries and guarantees

The refund and retry policy is part of the price. A fee with a generous retry policy is worth more than the same fee without one, because it reduces the expected cost of a failure.

Retries

A retry policy should state the conditions: same firm, same account size, same plan, and a time limit. A retry that is only available within seven days is less valuable than one available throughout the evaluation. A retry that excludes conduct breaches is reasonable; a retry that excludes any breach is not a retry at all.

Refunds

A refund policy should state the trigger, the timeline and the deductions. A refund that is only available if the evaluation has not started is a cancellation policy, not a refund. A refund that is conditional on the firm's decision is outside the provider's control and should be treated as unlikely.

Guarantees

A guarantee is only as good as the entity behind it. A "guaranteed pass" from a provider with no track record is a marketing claim. A guarantee that requires the trader to follow a list of conditions may be valid, but the conditions should be written and achievable. The trader should ask what happens if the guarantee is not met: a refund, a retry, or nothing.

Questions to ask before paying

These questions turn a quote into a comparable offer. Ask them before any payment, and get the answers in writing.

  1. What exactly does the fee cover: rule mapping, risk plan, execution, reporting and handover?
  2. Is the fee flat, a profit share, a retainer or a hybrid? If there is a future share, what is the percentage and how long does it last?
  3. What is the retry policy, and does it cover rule breaches at the same firm and size?
  4. What is the refund policy, including the trigger, timeline and deductions?
  5. Does the provider check the firm's conduct clause before accepting the job?
  6. What is the provider's clean-pass record, and how is it measured?
  7. What happens if the firm changes its terms during the evaluation?
  8. Who owns the funded account, and how is it handed over?

Frequently asked questions

How much does a prop firm passing service cost in 2026?

It depends on the model. Flat fees are typically in the low hundreds of dollars per evaluation, profit shares take a percentage of payouts, and retainers charge monthly. Compare the expected total cost, not the first payment.

Is a profit share cheaper than a flat fee?

Only if the funded account produces little or nothing. On a successful account, a percentage of payouts can exceed a flat fee several times over. The flat fee is capped; the profit share is not.

What is included in a flat fee?

A proper flat fee covers rule mapping, a risk plan, execution, progress reporting and handover. If it covers only execution, the trader is paying for the missing work separately.

Are retries included in the price?

Not always. A retry policy should be in writing, with the conditions and any time limit. A fee with retries is worth more than the same fee without them.

Can a passing service guarantee a pass?

No provider can guarantee a pass without controlling the firm's rules. A guarantee is usually conditional, and the conditions should be written and achievable. Treat an unconditional guarantee as a marketing claim.

What is the biggest hidden cost?

A voided account after a conduct breach. It can cost the evaluation fee, the service fee and the foregone payouts. The provider's compliance check is part of the value.

Should I choose the cheapest quote?

No. Compare the expected total cost of a clean pass, including retries and compliance risk. A slightly higher fee with a better process can be the cheaper route.
