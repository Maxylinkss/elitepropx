FTMO passing tips that actually move the odds

Most FTMO advice is either obvious or useless — "be disciplined", "manage risk", "stick to your plan". This is the other kind: twenty-two specific behaviours, each with the arithmetic behind it, that separate accounts which reach Verification from accounts which expire quietly three weeks in.

Key takeaways

  • The 5% daily loss limit is measured on equity, floating positions included, which makes it the strictest number on the account.
  • Most failed FTMO attempts are not stopped by the target being hard — they are stopped by a single session that broke the daily budget.
  • There is no time limit on the standard challenge, so speed is a choice, and almost always the wrong one.
  • A sixteen to twenty day pass at modest size beats a nine day pass that leaves no margin for Verification.

The filter: which tips are worth following

Advice about prop trading is cheap, and most of it is unfalsifiable. "Trade with discipline" cannot be measured, cannot be checked against a result, and cannot be improved. A useful tip has three properties: it names a specific action, it can be verified from an account statement, and ignoring it has a predictable cost. Everything in this article is written to meet that standard — if a tip cannot be checked against your own trade history, it does not belong in the list.

The second filter is structural. FTMO's challenge is a rules puzzle before it is a trading puzzle. A trader with a modest edge and perfect rule compliance funds an account; a trader with a better edge and one careless Thursday does not. That imbalance is why so much of the advice here concerns arithmetic rather than entries, and why the single highest-value habit is recalculating your limits every morning instead of relying on memory.

Third, the target is not the hard part. Ten percent on phase one, at a risk level that keeps a normal losing sequence survivable, is reachable in a few weeks by most traders who can already trade. What ends attempts is the interaction between the target and the two loss limits: the closer you get, the more tempting it becomes to press, and pressing is what converts a 7% gain into a 5% daily loss on a Tuesday afternoon. Most of the tips below are defences against that specific moment.

Finally, treat every tip as a hypothesis about your own trading, not a rule handed down. The list is drawn from patterns that repeat across attempts, but your statement is the only evidence that matters about you. Adopt two or three, run them for a full attempt, and check whether the failure mode they address actually appears in your journal. A tip that solves a problem you do not have is just extra friction.

Before you spend anything

The cheapest tips are the ones applied before a fee is paid. Five things are worth settling first, because each of them costs a full attempt if discovered later.

Confirm the numbers on the day you buy

FTMO's headline structure — 10% phase one target, 5% phase two, 5% daily loss, 10% maximum loss, four minimum trading days per phase — has been stable for years, but the firm layers changes on top: different account sizes, swing versus normal, and periodic adjustments to payout terms. Read the current rulebook page for your specific account type on the day you purchase and write the numbers into your plan. Traders fail on rules they assumed rather than rules they read, and the assumption is usually a year out of date.

Choose an account size you can lose without changing behaviour

Account size does not change difficulty in percentage terms, so the only rational reason to buy larger is the payout ceiling — and the only irrational reason is the feeling of trading more. Take the smallest size whose payout would still be meaningful to you, and resist the temptation to scale up until you have passed and been paid once. A trader who is afraid of the fee trades differently from one who is not, and the difference shows up as hesitation on valid setups.

Test the platform before the clock matters

MT4, MT5 and cTrader all calculate floating equity the same way, but order handling, hedging behaviour and the exact place the stop sits differ. A week of demo trading on the same platform and instrument set removes a category of error that has nothing to do with skill: fat fingers, wrong lot sizes, stops placed on the wrong side because the chart was inverted. These are embarrassing failures and they are entirely preventable.

Decide the instrument set in advance

FTMO permits forex, indices, commodities, crypto and stocks, but permission is not a strategy. Pick one to three instruments whose session behaviour you know, and write the list down. A challenge is a poor time to discover that you cannot read gold on a Friday afternoon. The narrower the instrument set, the more comparable your trades become, and comparability is what makes a weekly review possible at all.

Set the journal up before the first trade

A journal with five fields is enough: date, instrument, direction, risk in percent, result in R. Add a sixth for the reason, in a single clause. The purpose is not record-keeping for its own sake; it is to answer one question at the end of each week — is my risk per trade stable, and are my losses smaller than my wins in R terms? An account statement shows the money; only a journal shows whether the process was repeatable.

Sizing: the four numbers to write down

Sizing is where most of the passing is done, and it can be reduced to four numbers that fit on an index card.

NumberHow to calculate it$100,000 example
Daily loss budget5% of starting balance$5,000
Per-trade riskOne third of the daily budget$1,650 (1.65%)
Trades per sessionBudget ÷ per-trade risk, capped at three3
Phase target in R10% ÷ per-trade risk≈ 6R

The fourth number is the one traders rarely compute, and it reframes the whole challenge. At 1.65% risk per trade, a 10% target is roughly six full-risk wins. Six. Spread across sixteen trading days with a normal mix of wins, losses and scratches, that is an entirely ordinary month of trading — which is why the honest advice is that FTMO's target is small and the discipline required is large.

Tip 1: risk one third of the daily budget, not one half

Half the daily budget per trade means two consecutive losses end the day, and two consecutive losses are not an anomaly — they happen in any sample of twenty trades with a 45% win rate. A third means three losses are needed to end the day, which turns a bad session into a bad session rather than a blown limit. The cost is that reaching the target takes slightly longer, and time is the one resource the challenge does not meter.

Tip 2: keep risk constant within 10% of itself

The most common sizing error is not over-sizing; it is variable sizing. A trader risks 0.8% on the setup they like least and 3% on the one they like most, and the resulting equity curve is dominated by the trades they felt best about — which is usually a smaller sample than they think. Constant risk makes the statement readable. Readability is what allows you to tell skill from luck at the end of the attempt.

Tip 3: size down as the target approaches

Above 70% of the target, halve your risk. The mathematics is unglamorous: the remaining 3% of target is worth less to you than the 5% you could lose in a single session, and the expected value of pressing at that point is negative even with a genuine edge. Traders who respect this rule pass more slowly and fail far less often. It is the single highest-yield tip in this article.

Tip 4: translate risk to lots before you enter, not after

Calculate the position size from the stop distance and the dollar risk, then place the order. Doing it the other way — picking a familiar lot size and seeing where the stop lands — is how a 1.65% trade becomes a 3.4% trade with a stop that "felt right". On a $100,000 account, 1.65% is $1,650; if the stop is 22 pips, that is 0.75 lots on EURUSD at $10 per pip, and the arithmetic should already be done before the chart is opened.

Protecting the 5% daily limit

The daily limit is measured on equity, which means floating losses count against it in real time. That single detail changes the way a session has to be managed, and it is the rule most attempts die on.

Tip 5: stop at 80% of the daily limit, always

If the daily budget is $5,000, the working limit is $4,000. The remaining $1,000 is not unused capital; it is insurance against the float. A position that is open when the market gaps, or a spread that widens at the London close, can move equity several hundred dollars in seconds. Traders who stop at 80% almost never breach a daily limit; traders who stop at 100% breach it eventually, because the limit is measured before they have a chance to close.

Tip 6: count floating loss toward the budget the moment it exists

The account is at its daily low when a position is at its worst, not when a trade is closed. If you have closed two losers and have a third position $900 in the red, your budget is $4,000 minus $900, not minus the two closed trades. Doing this arithmetic in your head during a session is unreliable, so write the running budget next to the platform and update it whenever a position is opened or closed.

Tip 7: no re-entries after two losses

Two losses in a session initiates a hard stop for the day, regardless of the remaining budget. This is a behavioural rule rather than an arithmetic one, and it exists because the third trade after two losses is the one most often taken for emotional reasons — recovering the day rather than exploiting an edge. The cost is occasionally missing a good setup; the benefit is never being in the position of defending a nearly-broken limit.

Tip 8: close before high-impact news if you cannot watch it

FTMO permits trading the news, but floating exposure during a release is a different risk from a stop measured in pips. Spreads widen, slippage is real, and a stop can fill well beyond its level. If you cannot sit through the release, be flat. If you can, reduce size to a fraction of normal and accept that the outcome is a coin flip on a wider distribution than your plan models.

Protecting the 10% maximum loss

The maximum loss is static: on a $100,000 account the failure level sits at $90,000 and never moves, whatever you earn along the way. That sounds generous next to a trailing drawdown, and it is — but it also creates a specific trap, because a static floor means a trader can lose the daily limit five days running and still be in the challenge, which encourages continuation after evidence has already arrived.

Tip 9: track maximum loss from the starting balance, not the peak

Because the floor is static, the number that matters is distance from $100,000, not distance from your highest equity. A trader at $106,000 who has a bad week still has $16,000 of room. A trader at $94,000 has $4,000. Write the current distance on the same index card as the daily budget, and update it once per session — it is the only number that tells you whether the challenge is genuinely in trouble.

Tip 10: treat three consecutive losing days as a structural problem

Three consecutive days of losses is not a variance event to trade through; it is a signal that the market regime has changed or the process has drifted. Stop for two sessions and review the journal before the next trade. The cost is two days out of an unlimited calendar; the alternative is discovering the problem at $93,000, when the remaining room forces a size that no longer resembles your plan.

Tip 11: never add to a losing position

Averaging into a loser is the fastest route from a normal drawdown to a static-limit breach, because the second entry doubles exposure at exactly the moment the first is wrong. On a challenge with a static floor the temptation is stronger than on a trailing account — the floor does not move against you, so it feels as though there is room. There is room, and using it this way is how a 2% loss becomes a 7% one.

Pacing a pass that survives Verification

FTMO's standard challenge has no overall time limit, and the minimum is four trading days per phase. Those two facts together define the strategic question: how fast should you go? The answer that maximises the probability of being funded is slower than most traders expect.

The patient row is not merely safer; it is faster in expected terms, because a failed attempt costs the fee and the whole elapsed period before restarting. An aggressive trader who passes on the second attempt in twelve days has spent more time and money than a patient trader who passed on the first in twenty.

Tip 12: set a floor for days, not a ceiling

Commit to a minimum of fourteen trading days in phase one before requesting evaluation of the target, even if you hit 10% on day eight. The purpose is not superstition: it forces a sample large enough that the result reflects process, and it prevents a lucky week from producing a trader who believes they have an edge they cannot repeat in Verification.

Tip 13: pass Verification the same way you passed phase one

Phase two has half the target and the same loss limits, which makes it feel like a formality. It is not — it is where the account is won or lost by traders who raise size because the target is lower. Keep the same risk per trade and the same instrument set. Verification at 1.65% risk needs roughly three full-risk wins, and there is no deadline at all.

Tip 14: bank the target before you stop, then stop

When the target is reached, close everything and stop trading for the day. The remaining session has nothing to offer and a full daily budget to lose. This sounds trivial and it is the most common way a completed challenge turns into an incomplete one — the target was hit, the trader kept trading, and the day closed below the finish line.

The daily and weekly routine

Structure beats motivation across a three-week attempt. Two routines carry most of the weight.

Tip 15: a five-minute pre-session check

Before the first trade, write four numbers: current equity, distance to the static floor, today's daily budget in dollars, and today's maximum position size in lots. Then check the economic calendar for high-impact releases during your session. That is five minutes and it eliminates the two most expensive categories of error — trading a stale risk figure and being surprised by a release. Traders who skip this step are not undisciplined in general; they are simply relying on memory for numbers that change daily.

Tip 16: a twenty-minute weekly review with three questions

Once a week, answer three questions from the journal. Was risk per trade stable within 10%? Were losses smaller in R terms than wins? Did any trade violate a written rule, and if so which? A single violation is a coaching note; two of the same violation is a structural problem to fix before the next session. The review is what converts a challenge from a gamble into a measurement, and it takes less time than a single trade setup.

Psychology under a deadline you invented

The challenge has no deadline, but traders impose one within the first week — usually a vague target like "pass by the end of the month" — and then trade as though it were a firm rule. Nearly every psychological failure in prop trading traces back to that self-imposed clock.

Tip 17: delete the deadline and replace it with a day count

Instead of a finish date, track a running count of trading days completed and a running count of rule violations. The first number should grow slowly and steadily; the second should stay at zero. This reframes progress from "am I close to the target" to "is the process intact", which is the only question that predicts the outcome.

Tip 18: pre-commit to a stop after a rule violation

If you break a written rule — oversized, revenge trade, holding through a release you said you would avoid — stop for the rest of the day and the next session. The penalty is decided in advance, when you are calm, precisely because the moment after a violation is when judgement is worst. A trader who enforces this ends most attempts with zero violations. A trader who forgives themselves once will forgive themselves again.

Tip 19: separate the fee from the outcome

The fee is sunk at purchase; it is not recoverable by trading harder, and treating it as a debt to be repaid is how a $540 challenge turns into a $3,000 series of resets. The correct frame is that you have bought a three-week evaluation of your process, and the fee buys information either way. Traders who think this way take the same trades in week three as in week one, which is exactly the behaviour that passes.

News, sessions and the calendar trap

FTMO's rules permit news trading, and that permission is a trap for anyone who reads it as encouragement. Two tips cover the ground.

Tip 20: trade one session, not three

London, New York and the Asian session have different volatility profiles, and a strategy calibrated on one rarely transfers. Choosing a single session reduces screen time, makes the journal comparable, and removes the temptation to force a trade at 22:00 because nothing happened earlier. The exception is a genuinely session-agnostic strategy, which most are not.

Tip 21: treat the first Friday of the month as a separate regime

Non-farm payrolls produce spreads and slippage that a normal stop distance does not account for. Either be flat, or trade at a third of normal size with a wider stop and a smaller position. The same applies to central bank decisions and CPI releases. None of these events is unpredictable — they are on a calendar you can read in thirty seconds, which makes exposure through them a choice rather than an accident.

The seven ways attempts actually end

Across attempts that fail, the causes cluster tightly. Recognising the pattern in your own behaviour is more useful than any entry technique.

  1. The single oversized trade. One position at three or four times normal risk, usually taken after a good week, that consumes the whole daily budget.
  2. The three-loss session. Risk set at half the daily budget, two losses, then a third taken to recover the day.
  3. The giving-back day. A profitable morning held into the afternoon until the position reverses through the entry and the stop.
  4. The deadline sprint. Size increases in the final week because an invented finish date is approaching.
  5. The news gap. A stop that fills beyond its level during a release, converting a planned 1.5% loss into 4%.
  6. The rule misread. A swing account traded overnight when the plan was normal, or a position held over a weekend that the account type does not permit.
  7. The abandonment. A drawdown to $94,000 followed by two weeks of no trading, then a return with a size chosen to recover the loss in one trade.

Six of the seven are budgeting failures rather than analysis failures, and all six are prevented by the same three habits: a written daily budget, a hard stop at 80% of it, and a constant risk per trade. That is an uncomfortable conclusion for anyone hoping the answer is a better indicator, and it is the conclusion the data supports.

Tip 22: review the failure, not the trade

When an attempt ends, the instinct is to review the last trade — the one that broke the limit — and look for what went wrong in the entry. That is almost never where the problem is. The last trade is the symptom; the cause is usually two sessions earlier, when the budget was spent to the last dollar, or a week earlier, when risk per trade drifted upward and nobody noticed.

A useful post-mortem asks four questions in order. How many sessions did the attempt last? On how many of them was risk per trade outside 10% of the plan? Which session came closest to the daily limit, and what was different about it? And if the attempt had ended one day earlier, would it have passed? Most failures answer the last question with yes, which is the most useful thing an attempt can teach.

Write the answers down, date them, and keep them with the next attempt's plan. A trader who can describe why the last two attempts ended in the same way has a specific problem with a specific fix. A trader who says only that the market was difficult has bought information and thrown it away.

Keep the post-mortems in one place and read them before the next purchase. Three dated notes are worth more than any article about discipline, because they describe a specific trader's specific leak — and the leak is usually the same one in all three.

That is also the test for whether any of this advice is working. If the post-mortems keep naming the same leak, nothing has been fixed and no amount of extra reading will fix it; if they start naming a different one, the process is moving and the remaining work is smaller than it looks.

There is a practical version of this test that costs nothing: read the last post-mortem before placing the first trade of a new attempt. It is a two-minute habit that closes the loop between information and behaviour, which is the loop most traders leave open. A note that is written and never re-read is a diary, not a process.

Frequently asked about FTMO passing

How long does it take to pass FTMO?

Sixteen to twenty-two trading days at 1.5% risk per trade is the realistic range for both phases combined. Faster passes are possible, and they usually mean the risk was high enough that a second attempt would have been likely.

Is there a time limit on the FTMO challenge?

The standard challenge has no overall time limit; only the four-day minimum per phase applies. Confirm the current terms for your specific account type on purchase, since promotional and swing variants differ.

What is the best risk per trade for the challenge?

One third of the 5% daily loss budget, which is about 1.65% per trade on a $100,000 account. That allows three losses in a session before the day ends and keeps a normal losing sequence survivable.

Is the 5% daily loss measured on balance or equity?

Equity, including floating positions. A trade that is open and losing counts against the daily limit in real time, which is why the working stop should be 80% of the budget rather than 100%.

Can I trade news on an FTMO account?

Yes, the rules permit it. That does not make it advisable: spreads widen and stops can fill beyond their level, so exposure through high-impact releases should be sized at a fraction of normal or avoided entirely.

What happens if I fail?

The account is closed and a new challenge must be purchased. Where a passing service trades the evaluation, a reset on the same firm and size carries no additional service fee under the flat $220 arrangement, while the firm's own reset charge is separate.

Should I take the larger account size?

Only if the payout ceiling matters more than the fee. Percentage rules are identical at every size, so a larger account is the same challenge with a bigger bill — take the smallest size you can trade without changing your behaviour.

Can I hold positions over the weekend?

Only on a swing account, where the rules permit it. On the normal account positions must be closed before the weekend, and holding through a gap is not permitted regardless of the size of the position.

Does a consistency rule apply?

Not at evaluation. FTMO does not impose a best-day consistency requirement on the challenge or verification phases, unlike many futures firms, so the profit distribution only matters for your own risk management.

What is the single most useful tip?

Halve your risk above 70% of the target. The remaining target is worth less than the budget you would risk chasing it, and pressing at that point is the most common cause of a completed challenge becoming an incomplete one.
