FundedNext vs Funding Pips: which rulebook fits your trading

On paper these two look interchangeable: forex and CFD, one-off fees, the same three platforms, weekday sessions. The differences are narrower and more consequential than that. FundedNext sells a family of models whose floor changes between the one-step and two-phase versions, while Funding Pips describes a two-phase structure on a static floor with no consistency condition on most evaluations. Getting the model right matters more than the fee. Figures describe each rulebook's published shape — confirm the plan terms before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Both evaluate forex and CFD on one-off fees, so retries rather than duration dominate the cost.
  • FundedNext's floor depends on the model chosen; Funding Pips describes a static floor on a two-phase structure.
  • Funding Pips usually applies no consistency condition to the evaluation; FundedNext's varies by model.
  • The daily stop is a percentage of balance on both, so the daily-to-floor ratio is the number to compare.
  • The platform lists are identical, which removes the migration question entirely.
  • Because the products are close, the decision is usually made on the floor and the payout terms, not the target.

Why these two are harder to separate

Most firm comparisons are decided by a market difference or a floor mechanic that points in opposite directions. Here both products evaluate the same market, both charge once per attempt, and both use the same three platforms. The surface is nearly identical, which means the decision rests on details that a summary page usually omits.

The first detail is the product family. FundedNext sells several models, and the floor changes between them: the two-phase version is static, some one-step variants trail. Funding Pips describes a two-phase evaluation on a static floor measured from the initial balance. A trader comparing a FundedNext summary with a Funding Pips summary may be comparing a trailing variant against a static product without noticing.

The second detail is the consistency rule. Funding Pips usually applies none to the evaluation, while FundedNext's answer depends on the model. That changes the shape of the profit a plan should produce, and it changes what a payout review will look at.

The third detail is the daily stop. Both express it as a percentage of balance, so the useful comparison is the ratio to the total floor. A daily limit at half the floor is a different product from one at a fifth, even when both are described as percentage based.

A family of models against one structure

A one-step model asks for one target inside one window. A two-phase model asks for two, the second usually measured from the balance the first produced. The structures fail in different ways: the one-step version punishes a method that cannot produce a result in a single window, and the two-phase version punishes one that cannot repeat it.

Funding Pips' description is the simpler of the two: a two-phase evaluation on a static floor. There is one structure to plan for and one floor mechanic to size from. A trader who knows the method takes ten weeks to produce a target can plan the calendar without ambiguity.

FundedNext's family requires an extra decision before the fee. The two-phase version on a static floor is the conservative choice for a method with deep retracements; the one-step variants are faster and, where the floor trails, stricter after a gain. The decision should be made on the same evidence a trader would use for any evaluation: the simulation of the exact rules on their own trade history.

The trap is to choose on price. Because the models sit at different fee points, a cheaper one-step variant can look like the better buy until the floor mechanic is applied to a normal losing run. Price should be the last filter, not the first.

Static floors and their variants

A static floor measured from the initial balance is fixed for the whole attempt. On a $100,000 account with a 10% floor, the failure point is $90,000 from the first trade to the last, and profit adds room above it. That makes the risk unit easy to hold constant and removes one decision from every morning.

The variant to confirm is whether the static figure ever changes. Some rulebooks reset the floor after a payout, others keep it at the original level, and a few move it to break-even once the account earns a defined amount. The first leaves the account as forgiving as at the start; the second makes the second payout harder than the first.

A trailing variant behaves in the opposite way. The failure point follows the high-water mark, so a winning run reduces the room available to the next retracement. A trader on that mechanic must recompute the unit each session and should take profit at defined levels rather than holding for a larger move.

Both mechanics can be simulated in an afternoon. Replay the last hundred trades twice, once with a fixed floor and once with a trail, and record the maximum drawdown in each run. The mechanic whose run survives is the one to buy, and the model menu resolves itself.

The daily-to-floor ratio

Both products express the daily stop as a percentage of balance. The number that matters is the ratio to the total floor, because it decides how much of the attempt a bad week can consume. A daily limit at half the floor means two consecutive bad sessions end the attempt; a limit at a fifth means five would be needed.

The ratio is usually stated indirectly. If the floor is 10% and the daily stop is 5%, the ratio is one half, which is restrictive. If the floor is 10% and the daily stop is 2%, the ratio is one fifth, which is comfortable. Those two products read the same on a summary page and behave very differently in a bad week.

The planning response is a personal cap well inside the published one. With a $100,000 account, a 5% daily limit is $5,000; at a $400 unit that is twelve losses, which no session should use. A personal cap of three losses, $1,200, is what actually shapes behaviour, and the published limit becomes a backstop.

The second response is a weekly cap at a third of the floor. Four sessions at the personal limit is $4,800, nearly half a 10% floor. A weekly cap stops the accumulation before it becomes structural, and it is the only rule in the system that nobody else enforces.

A $100,000 attempt on each

Start with a 10% floor and an 8% target. The failure point is $90,000 and the target is $108,000. A $400 unit tolerates twenty-five losses, which is beyond any normal distribution, so the binding constraint becomes the target and the consistency rule rather than survival.

Now compare a trailing variant with a $3,000 threshold. The failure point starts at $97,000 and rises with every new high. A $400 unit tolerates seven losses at the start; after the account reaches $103,000 the failure point is $100,000 and the room from the balance is $3,000, which is still seven losses but only three from the original start.

The static account is more forgiving at every point, and it is also more demanding behaviourally, because nothing forces a trader to stop giving back gains. The trailing account supplies that discipline and takes away the room to hold a position through a retracement. Neither is better; they suit different holding periods.

Both accounts need a written stop for the week. On the static side it prevents a slow bleed; on the trailing side it prevents a give-back from consuming the buffer. In both cases the rule is the same: stop at a stated drawdown from the equity high and review before resuming.

Lot sizing and pip value

Forex sizing is pips × pip value × lots. On a standard lot of a USD-quoted major, one pip is about $10, so a 25-pip stop risks $250 and a $400 unit buys one lot with room to spare. A 40-pip stop buys three quarter lots, which most platforms will express as a 0.75 lot position.

The pip value is not constant. USD/JPY depends on the exchange rate, crosses run through two conversions, and metals and indices do not use pips in the usual sense. A trader who applies $10 per pip across the board mis-sizes every non-USD-quoted instrument, and the error is largest when volatility is highest.

The account currency adds another conversion. A euro-denominated account trading a dollar-quoted pair has a pip value in dollars and a risk budget in euros, and the conversion moves with the exchange rate. Size in the account currency and verify the margin requirement before the trade.

Add a buffer of ten to twenty percent for spread and slip. Spreads widen around the London open and around releases, and the fill is what the floor sees. A planned $400 unit with a slipped fill of $440 is a 10% overrun, which across twenty trades is visible in the buffer.

Consistency where it exists

Funding Pips usually applies no consistency condition to the evaluation, so the profit can arrive in any shape. That is a freedom with a delayed cost: the funded account may apply a condition the evaluation did not, and a pass built on two oversized days can convert into a payout that does not clear.

FundedNext's answer depends on the model, with some plans carrying no minimum. The same caveat applies: the evaluation's silence says nothing about the funded stage, and the funded terms should be read at the same time as the evaluation terms.

The test to run before choosing is the share of profit from the best day. Take sixty sessions, total the profit, and compute that share. Above a quarter, a consistency rule will bind and the plan needs a daily profit cap to flatten the distribution. Below a quarter, the rule is unlikely to change anything.

Even without a rule, a smoother distribution shortens the expected time to target. A method that produces most of its profit in a handful of sessions has long dry spells between them, and the second phase has to be reached inside a similar calendar.

One-off fees and retry maths

Both products charge once per attempt, so the annual cost is the fee times the number of attempts. At an illustrative $400 per attempt, one pass costs $400, two cost $800 and three cost $1,200. Duration does not enter the calculation, which makes a measured attempt affordable on both sides.

The expected cost is the fee divided by the pass probability. At 35%, $400 becomes $1,143 per funded account; at 20%, it becomes $2,000. That is the number to compare with a realistic monthly profit, not the headline fee.

Nothing improves the probability as cheaply as trading smaller. A unit a third lower usually converts a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown, and because the fee is fixed, the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

The second lever is the model choice. On the FundedNext side, choosing the static two-phase version over a trailing one-step variant can change the probability by more than any fee difference, because it changes how a bad week is absorbed.

The same three platforms

Both list MT4, MT5 and cTrader, so the workflow decision is identical and the comparison stays on the rules. A trader with an existing MT5 setup or a tested expert advisor can move between the products without rebuilding anything.

The choice within the list is about style. MT4 is the familiar hedging platform with the largest library of expert advisors. MT5 adds a modern backtester and more timeframes but defaults to netting, which changes how multiple entries on one symbol are managed. cTrader is the level-based option with a cleaner order book and precise order handling.

Automation raises the same rulebook question on both. Some plans restrict high-frequency activity or certain order types, and an expert advisor can breach those clauses without intent. Read the activity clauses, test on a demo under the same rules, and keep the logs.

Data is bundled on both in most cases, which removes a line item that futures evaluations usually add. The remaining cost is the platform itself and the time, neither of which appears on the fee page.

Sessions and pairs

Both trade weekday forex hours, so the session map is the same. Liquidity concentrates in London and New York, with the overlap between them the deepest window of the day. The Asian session is quieter, with narrower ranges and wider spreads on European crosses.

The pair matters as much as the hour. EUR/USD and GBP/USD are liquid in London; USD/JPY is active in Tokyo and New York; AUD and NZD pairs move in the Asian session. A method built on EUR/USD's London behaviour does not transfer to AUD/USD's Asian behaviour without re-measuring the spread and the stop.

The daily stop interacts with the session because volatile sessions consume it faster. A trader whose method trades the London open will reach a personal cap sooner than one who trades the quiet afternoon, with the same unit. That is a reason to size the session against the cap, not a reason to avoid volatility.

Holding overnight adds rollover and gap risk, and the weekend adds a second gap. A position held across the weekend at full size carries a risk the stop does not measure. Either reduce the unit for those positions or flatten before the boundary.

Payout cycles and reviews

Both release a profit share on a fixed cycle, with FundedNext describing a first payout followed by a regular schedule. The difference that matters is the time from a profitable month to cleared funds and the conditions that can interrupt it.

The review examines the record: size relative to the rules, distribution of profit, number of sessions and any pattern resembling prohibited practice. A complete journal answers the questions in an afternoon; a statement without one invites interpretation.

Documentation is the step nobody plans for and the one that holds money longest. The name on the account, the address on file and the card used for the fee all have to agree; a replacement card since purchase means a human has to look at it. Do that work during the attempt, not after it.

Two questions decide what the headline split really means: whether it applies to gross or net profit, and whether the funded stage inherits the evaluation's rules. Get both answers before the fee.

Releases and weekend gaps

Forex news risk is concentrated in central bank decisions, inflation prints and employment data. Spreads widen before the release, liquidity thins, and the stop that looked safe at 13:55 is not the stop that fills at 14:30.

Restrictions vary by plan. Some prohibit opening positions in a window around a release, some prohibit holding through it, and some prohibit profiting from the resulting spike. The last is the most ambiguous and the most likely to be applied at review.

The conservative plan is to flatten before the release and re-enter after the first hour. That costs some of the largest moves and removes a category of dispute. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the floor can survive if it gaps.

Weekend gaps are the larger structural risk, because there are more weekends than releases. A position held from Friday to Monday is a position whose stop may not exist on Sunday night. Either reduce the unit or flatten, and make the choice a rule rather than a mood.

Which trader fits which

Funding Pips fits a trader who wants a single clearly defined structure, a static floor, and no consistency condition on the evaluation. It suits a method with a defined stop and a measured pace, and it removes the model-selection decision entirely.

FundedNext fits a trader who wants to choose between a one-step and a two-phase path, or between a static and a trailing floor, and who is willing to read the model document before buying. The family is an advantage when the choice is made deliberately and a hazard when it is made on price.

The tie-breaker is the floor. A trader whose method holds winners for hours and gives back part of the move belongs on a static version. A trader who takes profit at defined levels can use a trailing variant without giving up much, because the give-back risk is smaller when positions close quickly.

If both fit, simulate each on the same hundred trades with the same dollar risk and compare the maximum drawdown. Whichever survives with the smaller figure is the structure the method already fits.

What to confirm before paying

The first question is which model you are buying and what floor it uses. The second is whether a consistency condition applies to that model and at what stage.

The third is the daily stop and its basis, including the reset time. The fourth is whether the floor resets after a payout or stays at the original level. The fifth is the fee and whether a retry is charged again.

Finally, read the conduct section in full: news windows, permitted order types, automation, and whether anyone else may trade the account. Those clauses are brief, are applied in practice, and rarely survive the trip to a comparison table.

Put the answers in one dated folder — screenshots, support replies, the checkout page. Six weeks later that folder is evidence, while memory is an opinion.

A thirty-day plan for either

Spend the opening week at half size doing nothing but recording. Write down the buffer before each entry, what the floor did after a winner, and the spread actually paid at each fill. The week produces a small table of facts about the account.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recompute the buffer each morning on the trailing variant and weekly on the static one. This is where the target is earned.

Close out slowly. A third of the unit, the same instrument, and the withdrawal paperwork checked against the terms line by line. More attempts die in the final fortnight than in the opening one, usually because the target looks near enough to chase.

One card carries the whole plan: buffer in dollars, unit in lots, and the session stop converted into a loss count. Update the first number every morning; the other two follow from it.

How should I compare the daily stop?

As a ratio to the total floor. A daily limit at half the floor means two bad sessions end the attempt, while a fifth means five would be needed, even though both are percentage based.

Which is better for a method with deep drawdowns?

A static floor, because profit does not move the failure point and the room to recover survives the retracement. On the FundedNext side that means the two-phase model rather than a trailing one-step variant.
