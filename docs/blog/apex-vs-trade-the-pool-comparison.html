Apex Trader Funding vs Trade The Pool: which rulebook fits your trading

One product evaluates index futures on a trail against peak equity. The other evaluates equity day trading against a hard loss limit, on a platform built for shares rather than contracts. The markets differ more than the rules do: a futures session is nearly continuous, while an equity session has a fixed open, a fixed close and no overnight holding. This guide compares the mechanics that decide an attempt and the arithmetic that decides a position. Figures describe each rulebook's published shape — confirm the current schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Apex evaluates futures; Trade The Pool evaluates equity day trading. The sessions and sizing units do not transfer.
  • Apex trails against peak equity including open profit; Trade The Pool applies a firm-specific hard loss limit.
  • Contract sizing is tick value × ticks; share sizing is price difference × shares, with a much larger position count.
  • The daily stop depends on the product: Trade The Pool publishes one, Apex's EOD model has a fixed dollar limit, and Apex's intraday model has none.
  • Both charge a one-time evaluation fee, so time does not increase the price on either side.
  • Equity day trading adds borrow, locate and pattern-day-trader considerations that futures do not have.

What each product tests

Both evaluations ask whether a method can produce a target inside a risk budget, and both end the attempt if the budget is breached. The interesting differences are in how the budget is measured and what the trading day looks like while it is being spent.

Apex measures survival against peak equity including open profit. The failure point moves with every new high, including unrealised ones, so a position that runs into profit raises the floor before it is closed. Trade The Pool applies a firm-specific hard loss limit, which behaves like a static figure on most products: the failure point does not follow the equity high, and profit adds room above it.

The second difference is the session. Futures trade nearly continuously, with a regular session that carries most of the liquidity and an overnight session that is thinner. Equities trade a fixed day with a bell at the open and a bell at the close, and day-trading evaluations generally require positions to be closed before the close. That single rule changes the holding period, the gap risk and the number of decisions per day.

The third difference is the instrument. A futures contract has a fixed tick value and a known margin. A share position has a price, a spread, a borrow cost if short and, for some accounts, a locate requirement. The arithmetic is simpler per share and more variable per trade.

Two different day jobs

An index futures trader watches one or two instruments, sizes in contracts and manages a position that can be held for minutes or hours across a nearly continuous session. An equity day trader scans a universe, sizes in shares and has to be flat before the close, which compresses every decision into six and a half hours.

The equity day has a shape: the opening auction, the first thirty minutes of price discovery, a midday lull and a closing auction. Most day-trading methods fit one of those blocks, and the risk profile differs sharply between them. The futures day has a similar shape but a longer tail, because the overnight session continues to trade after the regular close.

The second difference is breadth. Futures offer a handful of liquid contracts; equities offer thousands of symbols with different liquidity, spreads and borrow costs. That breadth is an advantage for a scanner-based method and a distraction for a single-instrument method, and it changes how much preparation the session requires.

The third is regulatory. Pattern-day-trader rules, short-sale locates and borrow availability affect equity day trading in ways that futures do not. A strategy that shorts thin names can be profitable on paper and impossible in practice, which is a risk the evaluation will surface quickly.

A live trail against a hard loss limit

Model a $100,000 account with a $3,000 threshold. On Apex the failure point starts at $97,000 and rises with every new equity high, including highs made by an open position. A trade that is $1,500 in profit lifts the floor to $98,500; a retracement to entry leaves the account flat and the floor $1,500 higher, which is buffer consumed without a dollar lost.

On a hard loss limit the floor stays where it started. An unrealised gain does not move it, so a retracement costs nothing in terms of room, and a realised gain adds room above the floor. The account becomes more forgiving as it earns, which is the opposite of the trail.

The behavioural consequence is that a live trail discourages holding for a large target, while a hard limit permits it. A trader who lets a winner run is repeatedly raising the floor and then risking a give-back; a trader on a static limit can hold without any rule being triggered. That is a genuine structural difference, not a detail.

The second consequence is the sizing routine. On the trail, the unit must be recomputed from the buffer each morning and reduced after a new high. On the hard limit, the unit can be fixed for the whole attempt, and the only additional rule needed is a self-imposed drawdown stop, because the rulebook will not stop a slow bleed.

Contracts against shares

A futures position is sized as risk divided by (ticks × tick value). One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 and a $250 unit buys eight contracts. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100 and the same unit buys two contracts.

A share position is sized as risk divided by (entry − stop). A $40 stock with a $0.50 stop risks $0.50 per share, so a $250 unit buys 500 shares. A $200 stock with a $2.00 stop risks $2.00 per share, so the same unit buys 125 shares. The position count varies by a factor of four for the same dollar risk, and the share count is far larger than any contract count.

That difference changes execution. Five hundred shares of a liquid stock can be entered in one order, but the commission and the spread are per share and per trade respectively, so the cost structure differs from a futures round turn. On a low-priced, high-volatility stock the spread can consume a meaningful fraction of a tight stop.

Both calculations need a buffer. On the trail side a slipped fill consumes buffer directly, and on the equity side a fast move through a stop can fill several cents away. Adding ten to twenty percent to the planned risk changes the size by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the unit.

A $100,000 account, one trade each

On the futures side, assume an 8-tick stop on the E-mini S&P at $12.50 per tick. The risk per contract is $100, and a $300 unit buys three contracts. The position controls roughly $180,000 of notional exposure and the commission is charged three times per side. The stop is a known distance and the fill is a known cost within a tick or two.

On the equity side, assume a $50 stock with a $0.60 stop and 800 shares. The risk is $480, which is above the unit, so the correct size is 600 shares for $360. The position controls $30,000 of stock, the commission is charged per share, and the spread is charged once. The stop is a known distance and the fill depends on the book at that moment.

The comparison shows why the two products feel different on the first day. The futures position carries six times the notional exposure for a similar dollar risk, because the leverage is structural. The equity position feels heavier and is smaller in notional terms, which is why an equity trader moving to futures typically over-sizes and a futures trader moving to equities typically under-sizes.

Both examples need the rulebook's position cap. A contract cap or a share cap can be lower than the risk budget implies, and a size that fits the budget but breaches the cap is not a size you can take. The cap is usually in the plan table rather than the marketing page.

Daily stops and session structure

Trade The Pool applies a daily stop, which bounds the session independently of the total loss limit. Apex's standard evaluation does not, so the trail is the only published limit on a bad day. The practical effect is that an equity day trader is stopped at a figure and a futures trader is stopped when the trader decides or when the floor is reached.

The equity session structure makes a daily stop more natural. With a fixed close, a day has a defined end, and a rule that ends it early is consistent with the structure. A futures session can run overnight, so a daily rule has to define which day a position belongs to, which is why the reset time matters more on that side.

Both products still reward a personal cap. A figure that keeps the worst day at a third of the buffer is a sensible default: with $3,000 of room, roughly $1,000, which at a $300 unit is three losses. That cap does the work on both sides, and it is the only rule in either system that nobody else enforces.

The basis matters as much as the number. A limit measured on equity counts floating losses, so a position that is $1,200 underwater has consumed the allowance before it is closed. A limit measured on realised results permits a larger floating loss and a different behaviour at the close.

A fixed close against a continuous session

Equities open at a bell and close at a bell, and day-trading evaluations generally require positions to be flat before the close. That creates a hard deadline every day: a position that has not worked by 15:55 has to be resolved, whether or not the setup has played out. The rule is healthy and it is also a source of forced decisions.

Futures do not have that deadline. A position can be carried into the overnight session, where liquidity is thinner and stops are less reliable. The freedom is real and the cost is that the trader has to supply the discipline a bell would otherwise provide.

The futures settlement is the compensating structure. It marks the account to market once a day and can change margin requirements without a trade, which forces a daily reckoning even when no position is closed. On the equity side the reckoning is the close itself, and the overnight gap is not a risk because there is no overnight position.

For a trader who values a defined day, equities are the easier structure. For a trader who values flexibility, futures are. Neither is better; the mismatch between the structure and the trader's availability is what produces the losses.

NinjaTrader against Sterling and DAS

Apex lists Rithmic, Tradovate and NinjaTrader — the standard futures stack, with depth of market, exchange routing and a mature automation framework. Trade The Pool lists Sterling, DAS Trader and TradingView — the standard equity day-trading stack, with a focus on hotkeys, Level 2 and fast order entry across many symbols.

The workflows are genuinely different. A futures platform is built around one instrument and a DOM; an equity platform is built around a watchlist and rapid switching. A trader who has spent years on one will find the other unfamiliar in ways that cost money during an evaluation, which is why the migration should happen before the fee rather than after it.

TradingView appears on the equity list and is the bridge for a chart-first trader, but its execution layer is newer than the dedicated platforms and its order handling differs. A method that depends on precise stop management should be tested on the platform before it is relied on.

Data costs differ too. Futures data is usually billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Equity data is often bundled with the platform, but Level 2 and short-locate data can carry their own fees. Both belong in the monthly budget, not in the surprise column.

One-time fees on both sides

Both products charge once per attempt. Apex's evaluation is a one-time payment for 30 days of access, and its Performance Account activation fee is also one-time. Trade The Pool lists a one-time evaluation fee that ranges from the smallest to the largest account, with no monthly subscription on a funded account. Patience does not increase the price at either firm; what it risks is the access window.

The add-on costs differ. Apex requires a futures data feed, usually billed monthly by the platform or provider, and it continues regardless of whether the account is trading. Trade The Pool bundles platform access with the evaluation, but equity data, Level 2 and short-locate fees can carry their own charges. Both belong in the budget rather than in the surprise column.

Retries reset the arithmetic. Three Apex attempts cost three evaluation fees plus three data periods; three Trade The Pool attempts cost three evaluation fees. A trader who expects to need retries should price the total, not the headline fee, and should check whether either firm discounts a reset.

The behavioural difference a subscription used to create has gone. With a one-time fee, the calendar no longer bills by the month, so the discipline has to come from a 30-day access deadline on the Apex side and a 60-day limit on Trade The Pool's MAX evaluation.

Payout conditions

Apex pays from the funded account after qualifying days, with the request made from the dashboard. Trade The Pool releases a profit split on a fixed cycle. The difference that matters is the time from a profitable month to cleared funds and the conditions that can interrupt it.

Both review conduct before releasing profit. On a product with a live trail, the statements show a floor that moved during a session, which can look unusual to a reviewer. A journal that records the buffer at the start and end of each session answers the question before it is asked.

Documents and payment details are the last gate and the easiest delay to avoid. Money normally returns along the same route it arrived by, so a replacement card forces a manual check and a wait measured in weeks. Finish the checks while the target is still open, and file the fee receipt beside them.

Ask whether the split is calculated on gross or net profit, and whether the funded stage keeps the evaluation's rulebook or swaps in a new one. Those two replies are worth more than the advertised percentage, and neither tends to appear on the pricing page.

Retry economics

Estimate the pass probability from your own record and divide the cost by it. If an Apex attempt costs $250 and has a 30% pass probability, the expected cost per funded account is about $833 before data. If a Trade The Pool attempt costs $400 and has a 25% probability, the expected cost is $1,600. The difference is driven by both the price and the pass rate, which is why a cheap attempt with a poor fit is not cheap.

The cheapest way to shift both figures is to cut the unit by a third. That usually turns a normal run of losses from a breach into a tolerable drawdown, and it leaves the fee untouched, so the expected cost falls by more than profit per trade does.

The second lever is the market. A trader who is competent in futures will underperform on an equity evaluation regardless of the arithmetic, because the scanning, sizing and session assumptions do not transfer. Choosing the market you already trade is worth more than any rulebook advantage.

The third lever is the deadline. Apex gives 30 days and no extension; Trade The Pool's MAX evaluation gives 60 days and its FLEX evaluation has no time limit. A plan that needs the full window is a plan with no margin for a losing week, so the target should be scheduled to finish inside two thirds of the available time.

Events, halts and gaps

Futures react to the macro calendar: central bank decisions, inflation prints and employment data, with the largest single-session ranges of the quarter. Equities react to the same calendar plus earnings, guidance and company-specific news, and individual names can be halted mid-position.

A halt is an equity-specific risk that futures do not have. A stock can stop trading while a position is open, and the resumption price can be far from the stop. No rule prevents it and no stop protects against it; only size does. That is an argument for smaller positions in single names and for avoiding thin stocks around scheduled events.

Restrictions on news trading vary by plan on both sides. A window of two minutes around a release is a different rule from a window of thirty, and "no new positions" differs from "no holding". The clause is short, rarely on the marketing page and enforced at review.

The conservative plan is the same on both: flatten before the scheduled event and re-enter after the first half hour. On equities, flatten before the close as well, since the day-trading rule requires it and the overnight gap is not part of the product.

Who belongs on which

Apex fits a futures trader who closes within a session, takes profit at levels and can watch the US regular session. The intraday model rewards precision and punishes give-backs; the EOD model suits a trader who holds through retracements. The 30-day access period rewards a plan that finishes inside a month.

Trade The Pool fits an equity day trader who works a watchlist, uses Level 2 and hotkeys, and values a fixed close. The hard loss limit and the daily stop suit a method with a defined stop and a modest per-trade target, and the equity session provides a natural end to every day.

The tie-breaker is the workflow. A trader who already scans equities and manages many symbols will find the futures DOM restrictive; a trader who trades one index contract will find a watchlist distracting. The rulebooks are different, but the workflow difference is larger.

If both are accessible, simulate both on the same hundred trades with the same dollar risk. Whichever survives with the smaller drawdown is the structure the method already fits, and that is a better answer than any fee comparison.

What to confirm before paying

The first is the floor definition. On Apex confirm whether the trail includes open profit; on the equity side confirm whether the loss limit is hard, static or trailing, because the three behave differently. The second is the daily stop and its basis, including the reset time.

The third is the session rule. Confirm whether positions must be flat at the close, whether overnight holding is permitted and whether the settlement changes anything. The fourth is the position cap, in contracts or shares, which can bind before the risk budget does.

The fifth is billing and retries: the fee per attempt, whether a retry is charged again, and whether a promotion changes the terms. The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, automation and third-party trading.

Save a dated copy of every answer. It takes ten minutes and it is the only document that settles a dispute six weeks later.

A thirty-day plan for either

Week one is calibration: half the unit, a record of the buffer before every entry, and a note of how the floor or limit behaves after a winner. On the trail the goal is to see it move during the position; on the hard limit it is to confirm that it does not.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recompute the buffer each morning on the trail and keep it fixed on the hard limit. This is where the target is earned and where the trail begins to compress.

The last week is admin with a small trading component: a third of the unit, the result protected, and the payout file assembled. Pressing for more after the target is met is the classic way to hand back a finished attempt.

Across all four weeks, three numbers belong on a card: buffer, unit and daily cap. The products differ in how they move the first one; neither can do anything about a trader who does not look.

Common questions

Are these two products comparable?

Only at the level of rule shape. Apex evaluates futures and Trade The Pool evaluates equity day trading, so the sessions, sizing units and platform stacks differ before any rule is compared.

Which one has a daily stop?

Trade The Pool applies a daily stop. Apex's EOD evaluation has a fixed dollar limit, but its intraday evaluation has none, so the trailing floor is the only published limit on a session there and a personal cap is essential.

How do I size a share position?

Risk divided by the distance between entry and stop. With a $300 unit and a $0.60 stop, 500 shares fit. The share count is much larger than a contract count for the same dollar risk, and commissions are charged per share.

Can I hold an equity position overnight?

Day-trading evaluations generally require positions to be closed before the close. Confirm the exact rule for the plan, because holding overnight can breach the evaluation even when the trade is profitable.

Which platform should I choose?

Keep the stack you already trade well. NinjaTrader or Tradovate suit a futures DOM workflow; Sterling or DAS Trader suit equity day trading with hotkeys and Level 2. TradingView is available but its execution layer is newer.

Is either cheaper?

Both charge a one-time evaluation fee, so the comparison depends on the fee per attempt, the account size and the data costs. Price three attempts on each side and divide by your realistic pass rate; that is the number that matters.
