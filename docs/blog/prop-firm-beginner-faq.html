Prop Firm Beginner FAQ

New traders ask the same twenty-four questions in almost the same order. This page answers them without marketing language: what an evaluation actually is, how the rules interact, what the fees really cover, how funded accounts and payouts work, and which risks are worth walking away from. The answers are short enough to scan and specific enough to act on.

Key takeaways

  • An evaluation is a rules test with a fee, not a job application or a funded account.
  • The daily loss limit and the maximum loss are different rules with different consequences; learn both before buying.
  • Choose the firm by the plan document, not the brand or the headline target.
  • Budget for more than one attempt, and never pay a fee you cannot afford to lose.

Getting started

What is a prop firm challenge?

A prop firm challenge is a paid evaluation in which a trader tries to reach a profit target while staying inside a set of loss and conduct rules. Passing usually leads to a funded account, where the trader keeps a share of the profits. The evaluation balance is normally simulated; the fee is real, and the rules are contractual. The challenge is not a job interview in the ordinary sense, because the firm is not hiring the trader; it is selling access to a funded account conditional on performance. That distinction matters for how you read the marketing and the terms.

Do I need experience to start a prop firm challenge?

You need enough experience to have a tested method and a risk unit, not necessarily years of professional trading. A beginner who has never traded a demo will usually fail the loss rules before the strategy matters. A useful test is to trade a demo account for three months with the same risk plan you would use on the evaluation. If the demo survives, the challenge is a reasonable next step; if it does not, the fee is likely to be a donation.

How much money do I need to start?

Enough to pay the evaluation fee and, ideally, a second attempt, without affecting your living expenses. The fee varies by firm and account size, and it is not refundable in most cases. A common mistake is to spend the entire trading budget on the largest account, leaving nothing for a retry. A smaller account with a reserve for a second attempt is usually a better starting point than a large account with no margin for error.

Is a prop firm challenge the same as trading my own money?

No. Your own account has no profit target, no daily loss limit imposed by a third party and no conduct rules. A prop challenge adds constraints that change the optimal behaviour: smaller size, a daily stop and a payout schedule. A strategy that works on a personal account can fail an evaluation because the constraints are different, not because the edge is weaker. The first step is to test the method against the rulebook, not just against the market.

How long does a challenge take?

Most two-phase evaluations take four to ten weeks at a conservative risk unit, depending on the minimum trading days and how often you trade. A one-step plan can be faster, but it often has less drawdown room. Some plans have no time limit; others impose a deadline per phase. The calendar is part of the product, so check it before buying rather than after the first flat week.

Can I trade a prop challenge part-time?

Yes, if the plan's calendar allows it. A part-time trader should choose a plan with no time limit or a generous phase deadline, and should avoid plans with a minimum number of trades per day. The risk is not the trading itself but the calendar: a plan that requires a fast finish will push a part-time trader into larger size or rushed decisions. A slower plan with a smaller target is usually a better fit.

Choosing a firm

How do I choose a prop firm?

Start with the plan document, not the brand. Compare the maximum loss type, daily loss measurement, target, calendar, consistency rule and payout conditions. Then compare the fee and the platform. A firm with a slightly higher fee and a cleaner rulebook can be the cheaper route over a year. Reviews and videos are useful for reputation, but they are not a substitute for the terms. If a term is ambiguous, ask support in writing and keep the reply.

Are prop firms regulated?

Most prop firms are not regulated as brokers or investment advisers, because they do not hold client money for trading in the traditional sense. They sell an evaluation product and, if you pass, a profit-sharing arrangement. That does not make them illegitimate, but it means your protections are contractual rather than regulatory. Read the terms, check the firm's history and payout record, and avoid firms that cannot explain their ownership or jurisdiction.

Which prop firm is easiest for beginners?

The one whose rules you understand and whose calendar matches your availability. A firm with a static drawdown, a modest target and no consistency rule during the evaluation is usually easier to plan around than one with a trailing floor and a strict distribution rule. "Easiest" is not a universal ranking; it is a fit between the rulebook and the trader's process. A firm that is easy for a scalper may be hard for a swing trader, and the reverse.

Should I choose a forex or futures evaluation?

Choose the market you already trade. Forex evaluations are usually percentage-based with static or end-of-day trailing drawdowns; futures evaluations are dollar-based with intraday trailing thresholds and data fees. If you have no preference, a forex evaluation is often the simpler starting point because the rules are easier to express as percentages and the data costs are lower. If you already trade futures, switching to forex to chase a cheaper fee is usually a mistake.

Does the firm's country matter?

It can. The firm's jurisdiction affects your practical recourse if a payout is refused, and it may affect the payment methods available to you. A firm registered in a jurisdiction with accessible small-claims procedures is a different risk from one where enforcement is impractical. This is not legal advice, but it is part of the due diligence: a firm that cannot state its legal entity and jurisdiction clearly is a firm to approach cautiously.

Rules and drawdown

What is the difference between maximum loss and daily loss?

The maximum loss is the total drawdown that ends the account. The daily loss is the amount that can be lost in one day before the session ends, and at some firms before the account ends. The daily limit is usually smaller and is the first constraint to bind. A trader can survive a daily breach at some firms but not a maximum-loss breach, which is why the two rules need different buffers.

What is a trailing drawdown?

A trailing drawdown is a maximum loss that moves with profit. On a static drawdown, the failure level stays at the starting balance minus the limit. On a trailing drawdown, the level rises as the account makes money, and on an intraday version it rises with unrealised profit. The practical effect is that a profitable trade can permanently reduce the room available, so the position size should shrink as the account grows.

Is the daily loss measured on equity or balance?

At most firms, equity including floating positions. That means an open losing position counts against the daily limit before it is closed, and a position that recovers can still have breached the limit at its worst point. Some firms measure closed balance only. The convention is in the loss section of the terms and changes how you size positions, so confirm it before the first trade.

What is a consistency rule?

A consistency rule limits how much of the total profit can come from a single day, or how much the best day can exceed the average. A common form is a 30–50% cap on the best day's share of profit. It can apply during the evaluation, only at payout, or both. A payout-only rule can be managed by adding winning days before a withdrawal; an evaluation rule has to be planned from the first trade.

What counts as a trading day?

It varies. Some firms count any day with a closed trade; others count a day with an open position; some require a minimum number of trades. The definition is often in a footnote, and it decides whether a fast pass is eligible. Check it before planning the finish, because a target reached in three sessions may still need five eligible days before verification.

Can I hold trades over the weekend?

Depends on the firm and the plan. Some allow weekend holding, some prohibit it, and some allow it only on certain account types. Holding over the weekend adds gap risk, and a gap can breach the maximum loss at the open before you have a chance to react. If the plan permits it, size for the gap rather than for the normal daily range. If it prohibits it, close before the session ends, not after.

Costs and fees

What does a prop firm challenge cost?

It varies by firm and account size, typically from around one hundred to several hundred dollars per attempt. The fee is usually non-refundable. The total cost includes the fee, any reset fee, platform or data costs and the cost of a failed attempt. Compare the total cost per dollar of drawdown room, not the headline fee. A larger account often costs less per dollar of room, but it also produces larger dollar swings.

What happens if I fail the challenge?

The attempt usually ends, and you need a reset or a new evaluation to try again. Some firms offer a discounted reset; others charge full price. The firm's fee is separate from any service you used. A failure is not necessarily a reason to switch firms; if the failure was caused by your sizing, the same rulebook can be passed with a smaller risk unit. If it was caused by an ambiguous rule, a different firm may be the better choice.

Are there hidden costs?

Possible costs include reset fees, monthly platform or data charges, payout processing fees and currency conversion. A "free" platform may be bundled into a wider spread or commission. The way to find hidden costs is to read the fee schedule and the payout terms, not the pricing page. A firm that cannot provide a complete fee schedule before payment is a firm to avoid.

Do I need to pay for a trading platform?

Usually the platform is included with the evaluation, but data and automation can cost extra, especially on futures. Forex evaluations often include MT4, MT5 or cTrader at no additional platform fee. If a subscription is required, include it in the annual cost. A platform that costs $50 a month is $600 a year, which can be larger than the difference between two evaluation fees.

Passing and services

How do I pass a prop firm challenge?

Treat it as a rules test rather than a profit contest. Write the rule map, size from the daily budget, stop at a fixed loss, plan the calendar around the minimum days, and keep a journal. The target will come if the method has a positive expectancy and the size is small enough to survive a normal losing streak. The traders who fail usually do so because the size is too large for the daily limit, not because the strategy has no edge.

Can someone pass the challenge for me?

Some firms prohibit third-party trading outright, and others restrict account sharing or require the holder to place every trade personally. Using a service where the terms prohibit it can result in a voided account and a refused payout, even after funding. If you consider a service, check the firm's conduct clause first and get the answer in writing. The compliance question comes before the fee question, not after.

How much does a passing service cost?

Pricing models include flat fees, profit shares, monthly retainers and hybrids. A flat fee is capped and easier to compare; a profit share can cost more over a successful year. The service fee is separate from the firm's evaluation fee. Compare the expected total cost of a clean, compliant pass, including retries and the risk of a voided account, not just the first payment.

Is a passing service worth it?

It can be, if you understand what you are buying and the firm permits it. The value is the time and execution work, not a guarantee of profit. A service that maps the rules, sizes conservatively, reports progress and puts the firm’s third-party clause in writing is selling a process. A service that promises a guaranteed pass or hides the compliance question is selling a story. The difference is visible in the questions it can answer.

What is a realistic timeframe for a funded account?

Most traders who reach a funded account take weeks to months, not days. The evaluation itself may take four to ten weeks; the first payout adds several more weeks. A trader who expects a salary in the first month is likely to be disappointed. The realistic goal is a first payout within a quarter, with a reserve for a second attempt if the first fails.

Funded accounts and payouts

What is a funded account?

A funded account is the account a firm issues after a trader passes the evaluation. The trader trades it under the firm's funded-account rules, keeps a share of the profit and withdraws according to the payout schedule. The account is usually in the trader's name, but the capital is the firm's. The trader does not own the balance; they own the right to a share of the profit, subject to the terms.

When can I request my first payout?

Most firms require a minimum number of trading days, a profit threshold, a consistency check and completed KYC before the first withdrawal. The exact conditions vary by firm and plan. The first payout can take two to six weeks from funding. Check the conditions before you start trading the funded account, not after you reach a profit target, because a missing condition can delay the money by weeks.

What profit split do prop firms offer?

Most offer 80–90% to the trader, with lower tiers at some firms and higher tiers after scaling or a number of payouts. The split is applied to the profit at payout, not to the account balance. A 90% split with a $50 processing fee on a $500 payout is effectively 80% after the fee. Compare the net split on a typical payout, not the headline percentage.

Do I keep the funded account if I have a losing month?

Usually yes, provided you stay inside the funded account's loss limits and conduct rules. A losing month is not automatically a breach; a breach of the maximum loss or a conduct rule is. Some firms may reduce the account size or require a review after a large drawdown. The terms state the conditions, and the trader should know them before the first trade.

Can I run multiple funded accounts?

Many firms allow multiple accounts, and some encourage it through scaling plans. Others restrict copy trading or hedging across accounts. The rules vary, and running several accounts at once multiplies the administrative load. A trader should start with one account, learn the payout process, and add a second only when the first is stable. Diversifying across firms can reduce rule risk, but it also doubles the number of rulebooks to track.

Risks and scams

Are prop firms a scam?

Some are, most are not. The industry has legitimate firms with years of payout records and a fringe of operators that sell evaluations and make payouts difficult. The warning signs are a refusal to provide terms, a payout process that keeps adding conditions, a fee paid to a personal account, and a support team that cannot answer a specific rule question. A firm's longevity and public payout record are more useful than its marketing.

What is the biggest risk for a beginner?

Spending too much on fees before developing a process. A beginner can lose several evaluation fees learning the same lesson: the size is too large for the daily limit. The cheaper path is to trade a demo with the same rules, keep a journal, and only pay for an evaluation when the process survives three months. The second biggest risk is choosing a plan whose calendar does not fit the trader's availability.

Can I lose more than the evaluation fee?

On a standard evaluation, the fee is usually the limit of your financial exposure, because the account is simulated and the firm does not have recourse to your other assets. The exception is a funded account with your own capital, or a service arrangement where a voided account triggers a contractual claim. Read the terms for any clause that goes beyond the fee. If a firm asks for a deposit that is at risk as trading capital, the exposure is larger than the fee.

A beginner's first month

The first month should be preparation, not payment. A beginner who buys an evaluation in week one is paying to learn lessons that a demo can teach for free. The schedule below is a more useful sequence.

Week one: choose the rules, not the firm

Pick three firms and read one plan document from each. Write the maximum loss, daily loss, target, calendar and consistency rule in the same format. You are not choosing yet; you are learning the vocabulary. By the end of the week, you should be able to explain the difference between a static and a trailing floor without looking it up.

Week two: build the risk plan

Choose an account size you can afford and write the per-trade risk from the daily budget. A simple rule is one third of the daily budget per trade, with a session stop at two losses. Then apply the plan to a demo account with the same balance and the same rules. The goal is not profit; it is to see whether you can follow the stop rule for five sessions.

Week three: trade the demo with the rulebook

Trade the demo as if it were the evaluation. Record the distance to the floor, the target remaining and the days used. If the demo account breaches the daily limit, that is useful information: the size is too large or the stop rule is not being followed. Adjust the size and repeat. A demo that survives a normal losing streak is a better predictor than a demo that makes a large profit.

Week four: decide whether to buy

If the demo has survived four weeks and the process is repeatable, buy the smallest evaluation that fits your plan. If it has not, do not buy. The fee is not the cost of learning; the fee is the cost of applying a process you already have. A beginner who waits a month has lost nothing but time, and has avoided the most common and most expensive mistake in the industry.

Questions to ask support

Support replies are part of the rulebook. Ask these questions before buying, and keep the answers with the plan document.

  1. Is the maximum loss static or trailing, and if trailing, does it update intraday or at the daily close?
  2. Is the daily loss measured on equity including floating positions, or on closed balance?
  3. What time does the daily limit reset, and in which timezone?
  4. What counts as a trading day, and do weekends count?
  5. Does the consistency rule apply during the evaluation, at payout, or both?
  6. Are there any restrictions on news trading, weekend holding or automated strategies?
  7. Does the firm permit third-party trading or account sharing?
  8. What are the first-payout conditions, including minimum days, profit threshold and KYC?

A support team that answers these questions specifically is a good sign. A team that replies with marketing language or refers you to a video is a warning. The answers are not just information; they are evidence of how the firm handles disputes. If the answers are vague now, they will be vague when a payout is delayed.

Three beginner myths

"A bigger account is always better." A larger account produces larger dollar swings and a larger drawdown. A beginner who cannot follow a stop rule on a $25,000 account will not follow it on a $200,000 account. The right size is the largest one on which the process is unchanged, not the largest one the trader can afford.

"The target is the hard part." The target is the visible part. The hard part is keeping the daily loss and the maximum loss intact long enough for the target to be reached. A trader who focuses only on the target will size too large and breach a loss rule before the target matters.

"A funded account is passive income." A funded account is an active risk-management job. It requires monitoring, rule compliance and a payout process. Even with a passing service, the trader has to manage the funded account and the firm relationship. The income is real, but it is not passive in the way the marketing suggests.

What to do next

The best next step is not to buy the largest evaluation. It is to write a one-page plan for the smallest account you can trade competently: the target, the maximum loss, the daily budget, the per-trade risk, the stop rule and the calendar. Then trade a demo for a month with that plan. If the plan survives, the evaluation is a calculated purchase rather than a hope. If it does not, you have saved the fee and learned the same lesson.

When you are ready to compare firms, use the plan document rather than the brand, and ask the questions in this FAQ in writing. The answers will tell you more about the firm than any review you can read; you can also check recently passed evaluations and what buyers report before paying.
