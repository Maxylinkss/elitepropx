Prop Firm Passing Service Cost

A passing service costs a flat fee, but the fee is only one line in the budget. The evaluation itself, the reset fees, the data subscription that runs between attempts, the activation charge on a funded account and the payout processing fee all land on the same spreadsheet. This guide prices the whole route, compares it with trading the evaluation yourself, and shows the point at which each option stops being the cheaper one.

Key takeaways

  • A flat service fee is priced against your pass probability. Below roughly a 70% chance of passing first time, the arithmetic usually favours the flat fee.
  • The evaluation fee, reset fee, platform or data fee, activation fee and payout fee are outside the service fee. Add them before comparing two quotes.
  • The total cost of reaching a first payout — not the headline evaluation price — is the number to compare.
  • A flat fee is a larger share of a small account. At $50,000 the same $220 is 0.44% of capital; at $200,000 it is 0.11%.
  • Retries change everything. A service that covers retries has built the cost of failure into the price; a per-attempt service charges you again.
  • Set a budget stop before the first payment, not after the third attempt.
  • If the firm's terms prohibit third-party trading, the cost is irrelevant — the route is not available.

What the flat fee competes against

The mistake most traders make when pricing a passing service is comparing it with one evaluation fee. That is not the competing product. The competing product is trading the evaluation yourself, repeatedly, until you pass — and the cost of that is not the fee you paid, it is the fee multiplied by the expected number of attempts.

The maths is simple. If your probability of passing any single attempt is p, the expected number of attempts is 1 ÷ p. At a 40% pass probability you should budget for 2.5 attempts, not one. If the evaluation costs $155, the expected spend before a pass is roughly $388 — before you count the eleven weeks of screen time, the data subscription running underneath it, or the resets you bought along the way.

The crossover sits just under a 70% pass probability on a $155 evaluation. Above that line, a flat fee is the more expensive way to buy the same outcome. Below it, the flat fee is cheaper than the expected cost of doing it yourself — and that is before the time cost is priced in at all.

Be honest about p. Most traders overestimate their pass probability, because the sample they remember is their best attempt rather than their average one. If you have taken five evaluations and passed one, your observed probability is 20%, not the 60% you feel on a good week. Use the observed number; the fee decision depends on it.

Two details matter when you apply that table. First, the flat fee route still pays the evaluation fee once, because the firm charges for the account regardless of who trades it. Second, the comparison only holds if the pass actually happens on the first paid attempt. A flat fee with retries covered removes that variable; a flat fee charged per attempt reintroduces it, and at that point you are comparing two per-attempt products.

The five cost lines outside the fee

Every prop firm route has cost lines that are not the service fee and not the headline evaluation price. They vary by firm, by market and by plan generation, so treat the shapes below as questions to ask rather than figures to rely on.

Cost lineTypical shapeHow to verify itEffect on the total
Evaluation feeOne-off or monthly, scaled by account sizeThe checkout page for the exact planMultiplied by the number of attempts
Reset or retry feeOften discounted, sometimes full priceThe firm's rules page, not its FAQThe hidden cost of every failed attempt
Platform or data feeMonthly, common on futuresThe platform agreement shown at purchaseKeeps running between attempts
Activation or funded data feeOne-off or monthly on some programmesThe funded-account termsReduces the first payout
Payout processingFlat fee, percentage, or nothingThe payout method pageLargest relative cost on a small first withdrawal

Two of these are chronically underestimated. The data fee is the one that punishes slow traders: on a futures evaluation it runs whether you trade or not, so a four-month evaluation costs four months of subscription on top of the fee. And the reset fee is where the advertised price and the real price separate, because a $99 reset that you buy three times is a $297 evaluation.

The payout processing fee deserves its own note. On a first withdrawal of a few hundred dollars, a flat processing charge can be a meaningful percentage; on a large withdrawal it becomes irrelevant. If your plan depends on the first payout covering your costs, that fee belongs in the calculation even though it is charged months later by a different party.

Worked routes: three ways to the same funded account

Assume a $50,000 forex evaluation with a $155 entry fee and a $99 reset fee, and that all three traders end up funded within twelve weeks. The routes differ in how many attempts they buy and who trades them.

The lesson in Route C is the one that costs traders the most money. The cheapest headline evaluation fee produced the second-most expensive route, because the resets were priced close to the original fee and the data subscription ran for four months instead of one. A cheap evaluation is only cheap if you pass it quickly.

Route B is not automatically the best answer either. It is the cheapest of the three and the fastest, and it teaches nothing. A trader who intends to trade the funded account personally is buying an account without buying the experience that keeps it alive, which is a real cost that simply does not appear in the table.

Convert the weeks into money. If your time is worth $30 an hour and an evaluation consumes four hours a week of screen time plus an hour of journaling, Route A costs roughly 60 hours — about $1,800 at that rate. Set your own hourly number and the comparison changes completely. This is the only honest way to price the difference between the routes.

Cost per dollar of funded capital

Because a flat fee does not scale with account size while the evaluation fee usually does, the relative cost falls as the account grows. This table uses a $220 service fee and illustrative evaluation fees to show the shape rather than the exact market price.

Account sizeIllustrative evaluation feeService feeTotalCost per $1,000 of funded capital
$25,000$99$220$319$12.76
$50,000$155$220$375$7.50
$100,000$250$220$470$4.70
$200,000$450$220$670$3.35

Read defensively. The per-dollar column flatters large accounts, but a larger account is not automatically easier to pass, and it is not automatically more valuable: the loss floor is also larger in the rules, usually as a percentage, and the funded account still ends when the floor is touched. The funded account is worth the payouts you collect before it stops, not its nominal size.

That is the calculation that decides whether a large account is worth its higher fee. If a $200,000 account pays $2,000 a month for three months and then breaches, it produced $6,000. If a $50,000 account pays $700 a month for nine months before breaching, it produced $6,300 for a smaller outlay and a lower per-dollar cost. Size is a multiplier on your process, not a substitute for it.

The retry arithmetic

Retries are where flat fees and per-attempt pricing separate, and the crossover is worth calculating before you choose a provider. Let F be the evaluation fee, S the service fee per attempt, and n the number of attempts.

  • Per-attempt service: total = n × (F + S)
  • Flat service with retries covered: total = F + S + (reset fees, if the firm charges them)

With F = $155 and S = $220, a per-attempt service costs $375 on the first attempt, $750 on the second and $1,125 on the third. A flat service with retries covered costs $375 in total as long as the resets are waived or included. The second attempt is where the flat policy earns its premium, and the third is where the per-attempt route has cost you three times as much for the same funded account.

Attempts neededPer-attempt service ($375 each)Flat service with retries coveredDifference
1$375$375 plus reset policyComparable
2$750$375 plus reset policyFlat service ahead by $375
3$1,125$375 plus reset policyFlat service ahead by $750
4$1,500$375 plus reset policyFlat service ahead by $1,125

The phrase "retries covered" is doing real work in that table, so verify what it means in writing. It should mean a further full attempt at no additional service fee, under the same terms, within a stated period. It should not mean a discount on the next attempt, a credit toward a larger account, or a goodwill gesture at the provider's discretion.

What the fee buys that the evaluation fee does not

The evaluation fee buys access to an account and a set of rules. The service fee buys a process that is supposed to end in a pass. Those are different products, and it helps to be precise about what the second one contains.

Rule audit
The written check that your firm's terms permit the arrangement, plus the specific numbers that will govern the attempt: profit target, daily loss limit, maximum loss limit, whether the floor trails, minimum trading days, consistency rules at payout and any news or weekend restrictions.
Risk plan
A per-trade risk unit expressed as a percentage of the loss floor rather than a dollar figure, a maximum number of positions, and a stop condition that ends the attempt before the floor is touched rather than at it.
Execution
The sessions, positions and order management that move the account toward the target within the plan's risk budget.
Reporting
Progress against target and floor, so you can see the attempt is on track without monitoring it yourself.
Retry policy
A written statement of what happens if the attempt fails, which is the term most likely to be tested in practice.
Handover
The point at which the funded login, credentials and control pass to you, with no residual access and no profit share.

If a quote cannot describe those six items, it is not a quote for a process — it is a price for an outcome, and outcomes cannot be sold. The distinction matters because a provider selling an outcome has no way to price risk, so it either overcharges or disappears.

The cost nobody puts on the spreadsheet

Time is the largest omitted line in every cost comparison, and it is the one that most often decides the answer. A self-traded evaluation is not just the fee; it is the sessions, the preparation, the journaling, the review and the psychological load of holding a rule-bound account for weeks.

ActivityHours per weekTypical durationTotal hours
Screen time during sessions4–83–12 weeks12–96
Pre-market preparation1–23–12 weeks3–24
Journaling and review1–23–12 weeks3–24

A fast, disciplined pass on a comfortable account might cost twenty hours. A drawn-out attempt across three resets can cost over a hundred. Multiply by your own hourly rate and compare with the service fee; the answer is often that the fee is cheaper than the time, which is exactly the trade a time-poor trader is buying.

The counter-argument is real and worth stating: hours spent on a self-traded evaluation are not lost, because they build the judgement you need on the funded account. If you intend to trade funded capital personally, that experience has a return that does not appear in a cost table. The service is a poor buy for a trader whose bottleneck is skill and a good buy for one whose bottleneck is available hours.

Sizing a budget and setting a stop

Before the first payment, write down three numbers and keep them where you can see them.

  1. The total budget for reaching a funded account. A workable rule is two and a half times the evaluation fee plus the service fee, which covers one honest failure and one reset without touching money you need elsewhere.
  2. The stop condition. The point at which you stop buying attempts: three failures on the same plan, two on the same firm, or the budget being reached, whichever comes first.
  3. The review date. The date on which you re-read the attempt records and decide whether the route or the method needs to change. Without a date, the decision defaults to buying one more attempt.

The stop condition is the only part of the budget that consistently saves money. Traders rarely lose their budget on the first evaluation; they lose it on the fourth, bought in the belief that the third was unlucky. If you cannot state the condition under which you will stop, the budget is not a budget.

Payment, refunds and disputes

Pay in the way that leaves a record. A card or a documented transfer gives you a paper trail, a merchant reference and, in most jurisdictions, a dispute route. Payment in a channel that leaves no trace removes all three, and it is the single most common feature in the cases where a trader loses money to a provider that stops answering.

Be careful with the word refund. An evaluation fee paid to the firm is governed by the firm's terms; many firms do not refund it, and some refund only the first attempt's fee on the first payout of a funded account. A service fee is governed by the provider's own terms. Keep the two separate in your head and in your records, because a provider that promises to refund the firm's fee is promising something it does not control.

Keep three artefacts for every attempt: the checkout confirmation from the firm, the written quote or scope from the service, and the written terms check for the firm. If a dispute ever arises, those three documents are the whole conversation.

When the fee is the wrong buy

If the firm prohibits third-party trading, the service is the wrong buy at any price. The clause matters more than the cost, and it is the first thing to verify. A prohibited arrangement can end with a closed account and a refused payout, which turns a $220 fee into the loss of an entire funded account.

If the client wants to learn the rulebook, the service removes the learning opportunity, and the learning is the asset that persists after the account ends. If the client has a tested method and a high pass probability, self-trading is cheaper — the table at the top of this page says so explicitly above a 70% pass rate. And if the total budget is money that has to be somewhere else this month, no version of this arithmetic makes buying an evaluation a good decision.

The service is a fit for a trader who values hours, has a firm that permits the arrangement, and does not want to carry the evaluation personally. That is a narrow but real set of circumstances, and inside it the flat fee is usually the cheaper route. Outside it, the honest answer is that the fee buys something the trader does not need.

When the plan bills monthly

Subscription-style evaluations change the cost model completely, because the fee is no longer one-off. A plan that bills monthly is really a rental agreement: you keep the account for as long as you keep paying, and the total is the monthly fee multiplied by the number of months the attempt takes. That flips the incentive in an important way. On a one-off plan, a slow trader pays once; on a monthly plan, a slow trader pays for their own slowness.

Plan shapeCost behaviourWho it favoursCost of a four-month attempt
One-off evaluationFixed regardless of durationPatient, rule-driven traders$155 once
Monthly subscriptionScales with months heldFast traders who pass inside a month or two$90 × 4 = $360
One-off plus resetFixed per attemptTraders who reset deliberately rather than gradually$155 + reset

The monthly model is not automatically worse, and the table above shows why it can be better. A trader who passes in three weeks pays a fraction of the one-off price. The mistake is choosing a monthly plan for a method that needs two or three months, then discovering that the fourth payment is more expensive than the one-off plan would have been.

Ask about the pause policy. Some monthly plans allow you to pause or freeze the account between attempts; many do not, and the meter keeps running. If pausing is not available, price the plan on your slowest honest estimate, not your fastest.

What it costs to keep a funded account

Reaching the funded account is the first cost, not the last. The funded phase has its own economics, and they usually decide whether the whole exercise was profitable.

Funded-phase costTypical shapeWhy it matters
Recurring data or platform feeMonthly, where chargedIt is deducted before your payout is calculated
Minimum trading days before payoutOften 5–10 daysExtends the time before you see any money
Consistency rule at payoutBest day capped as a share of total profitCan force a second month of trading when one would have paid
Payout cadenceEvery 14, 21 or 30 daysSets how much capital you can withdraw per cycle
Profit splitTypically 80–90% to the traderReduces every payout by the firm's share

A worked example makes the shape clear. A funded $50,000 account on a 90/10 split paying $900 gross in its first cycle leaves $810 before any data fee. If the firm charges $60 a month for data and pays monthly, the trader receives $750. The evaluation plus the service fee came to $375. So the first payout covers the entry cost with room to spare — but only if the account survives to the first payout and the consistency rule is satisfied.

Now change one variable. If the consistency rule forces a second month of trading and the account breaches late in that month, the trader may collect nothing at all after paying $375 to arrive. This is the scenario the cost tables earlier in this article are designed to price: the funded account is worth the payouts you actually collect before it stops, and frequently that is one or two cycles rather than a year.

What moves the cost between firms

Cost is not a single market price you can look up. The same nominal account size can cost materially different amounts to reach at two firms, and the reasons are structural rather than promotional.

VariableEffect on total costWhat to check
Number of phasesTwo-step plans usually charge less per phase but take longerWhether the second phase carries its own fee or a fresh data month
Profit targetHigher targets lengthen the attempt and raise expected attemptsThe target for the exact plan, not the brand average
Drawdown typeTrailing floors end accounts earlier, lowering the value of the funded phaseWhether the floor trails on unrealised profit
Minimum trading daysExtends duration, so it multiplies any monthly costThe funded-phase requirement as well as the evaluation one
Payout cadenceLonger cycles delay cash and increase breach exposureThe first-payout waiting period
Third-party clauseDetermines whether the service route exists at allThe terms page, in writing

The drawdown row is the one that most often reverses a cheap fee. A plan with a low price and a trailing floor that follows unrealised profit has a shorter expected funded life than a more expensive plan with a static floor. Over six months of funded trading, the difference in expected payouts can dwarf the difference in evaluation price. Comparing firms on their fee alone is therefore a category error, which is why the total cost to first payout is the right unit of comparison and why the same figure should be recalculated for each firm rather than reused.

Glossary of cost terms

Evaluation fee
The amount the firm charges for access to a challenge account. Usually one-off; sometimes monthly on subscription-style programmes.
Reset fee
What the firm charges to restart a failed evaluation, often at a discount to the original fee.
Activation fee
A one-off charge on some funded programmes, sometimes deducted from the first payout.
Data fee
A recurring platform or market-data charge, most common on futures, that runs whether or not you trade.
Payout processing fee
A fee taken by the firm or its payment processor at withdrawal. Flat or percentage, and sometimes waived on the first payout.
Expected attempts
One divided by your pass probability. The number of evaluations you should budget for, not the number you hope for.
Flat fee
A single service price that does not scale with account size and, where retries are covered, does not repeat on failure.
Profit split
The share of funded profit the firm keeps. Not a service cost, but it changes the net value of the funded account you paid to reach.
Total cost to first payout
Every line in this article added together, including the payout fee. The only comparable figure across routes.

Common questions

Why is the fee flat instead of a percentage of the account?

The work is the same process at any size: read the rulebook, build the risk plan, execute, report. A flat fee prices that process rather than the size of the account, which also makes it auditable — you know the total before you pay.

Does the fee include the evaluation fee paid to the firm?

Only if the quote says so. Many quotes cover the service work and leave the firm's evaluation fee to the client. Add both lines before comparing providers, and confirm what happens to reset fees if the service needs a second attempt.

What is the cheapest way to reach a funded account?

It depends on your pass probability. Above roughly a 70% chance of passing on the first attempt, self-trading a single evaluation is cheapest. Below that, the expected cost of repeated attempts usually exceeds a flat service fee, and the gap widens with every additional attempt.

Is the service worth it on a small account?

The fee is a larger share of a small account — 0.44% of $50,000 against 0.11% of $200,000 — so the relative cost is higher. It can still be worth it when the hours saved are worth more to you than the percentage difference, but the arithmetic favours larger accounts.

Are reset fees included when retries are covered?

They should be, and the quote should say so explicitly. "Retries covered" ought to mean a further full attempt at no additional service fee within a stated period. Where the firm charges its own reset fee, confirm whether the provider absorbs it or passes it on.

How much should I budget in total?

A practical rule is two and a half times the evaluation fee plus the service fee, which funds one honest failure and one reset. Whatever the number, write the stop condition next to it: three failures on the same plan, two on the same firm, or the budget being reached.

Can the service fee be refunded?

That depends entirely on the provider's own terms, which is why they should be in writing before payment. Note that a provider cannot refund the firm's evaluation fee, because it never received that money. Keep the checkout record, the quote and the terms check.

Does the service take a share of funded profit?

It should not. The funded account is in your name and the profit is yours, apart from the firm's own profit split. A provider charging a percentage of funded profit has restructured itself as a managed account, which is a different product with different regulatory questions.
