FundedNext vs FTUK: which rulebook fits your trading

One product evaluates forex and CFD across several structural models; the other evaluates US futures on a single trailing threshold. Both charge once per attempt, so neither penalises a slow calendar, and both therefore reward sizing correctly rather than trading quickly. The real decision is the market, and after that it is which of FundedNext's models you are actually buying. Figures describe each rulebook's published shape — confirm the plan terms before paying.

Key takeaways

  • Different markets: forex and CFD on FundedNext, US futures on FTUK. Sessions and sizing do not transfer.
  • FundedNext's floor depends on the model: static on the two-phase version, trailing on some one-step variants.
  • FTUK trails against peak equity, so a winning run reduces the room available to the next retracement.
  • Both bill once per attempt, which makes retries the dominant cost rather than duration.
  • FundedNext's consistency rules vary by model; FTUK applies a consistency condition.
  • Neither platform list overlaps, so the workflow decision follows the market decision.

What each rulebook measures

Both products test whether a method can produce a target inside a risk budget, and both end the attempt when the budget is breached. The differences are in how the budget is defined, what the session looks like, and what the fee buys.

FundedNext sells several models under one brand. The two-phase model uses a static floor; some one-step variants trail it. That means the same firm can offer two very different risk structures, and the choice between them is the first decision rather than the last.

FTUK evaluates futures on a trailing threshold measured against peak equity. The floor moves up with every new high, so the buffer shrinks as the account earns. That is the stricter of the two mechanics and it rewards taking profit at defined levels rather than holding for a larger move.

The commercial structure is the same on both sides: one-off per attempt. That removes the calendar as a cost and makes the number of attempts the dominant variable. A trader who needs three attempts pays three fees regardless of whether the path takes six weeks or six months.

FundedNext's model problem

A brand that sells multiple structures creates a specific risk: the trader compares a summary and buys a variant. The one-step model with a trailing floor and the two-phase model with a static floor are about as different as two products can be while sharing a logo, and the pass behaviour differs accordingly.

The static two-phase version is the more forgiving of the two for a method with occasional deep retracements, because profit does not move the failure point. The trailing one-step version is more forgiving of a slow calendar, because there is only one target, but it punishes a give-back and rewards taking profit early.

The practical rule is to decide which mechanic fits the method before looking at price. A trader whose average winner runs for hours and gives back a third of the move belongs on the static version. A trader who takes profit at fixed levels belongs on the trailing one, because the disadvantage of the trail is smaller when positions are closed quickly.

Then check the consistency row for that exact model. "Some plans have no minimum" is a statement about a family, not about the product in the checkout basket. Ask support for the answer in writing and attach it to the terms copy.

There is a practical way to settle the model question in an afternoon. Take your last hundred trades and replay them twice: once with a static floor that never moves, and once with a trail that follows the high. Record the maximum drawdown in both runs and the number of times the account would have breached. The model whose simulation survives is the one that fits, and the fee becomes a secondary consideration.

Static, trailing and the variants between

A static floor is a fixed dollar distance from the starting balance. On a $100,000 account with a 10% floor, the failure point is $90,000 for the life of the attempt. Profit adds room above it, so the account becomes more forgiving as it earns.

A trailing floor follows the high-water mark. On the same account with a $5,000 trail, the failure point starts at $95,000, rises to $98,000 after the account reaches $103,000, and leaves the room from the peak unchanged while the room from the start shrinks by $3,000. FTUK's row specifies peak equity, which is the stricter definition because it includes open profit.

The variants in between are where most confusion lives. A trail measured from settled balance updates once a day; one measured from equity updates continuously. A trail that stops trailing once it reaches break-even behaves like a hybrid. A floor that is static during the evaluation and trailing after funding is a third pattern. Each is described by a short clause and each changes the sizing.

The planning response is identical regardless of variant: compute the buffer before the session, divide by the losses you are willing to absorb, and cap the result at your original unit. What changes is how often the calculation has to be redone, which is daily on a live trail and weekly on a static floor.

A $100,000 attempt on each

On the static version, assume a 10% floor and an 8% target. The failure point is $90,000 and the target is $108,000. A $500 unit tolerates twenty losses, which is beyond any normal distribution, so the binding constraint becomes the target and the consistency rule rather than survival.

On the trailing version, assume a $5,000 trail and a 6% target. The failure point starts at $95,000 and the target is $106,000. A $500 unit tolerates ten losses at the start, but after the account reaches $103,000 the failure point is $98,000 and the room from the balance is $5,000 — still ten losses, yet the room from the original start is only $3,000. The relative risk has grown even though the arithmetic looks unchanged.

The difference in feel is what most traders notice first. On the static account, a bad week is absorbed and the unit can stay the same. On the trailing account, a good week followed by a bad one can leave the account at break-even with the floor only $2,000 below, which is a very different psychological position from the same P&L on a static floor.

Both accounts need a self-imposed stop. On the static side it prevents a slow bleed; on the trailing side it prevents a give-back from consuming the buffer. In both cases the rule is the same: stop for the week at a stated drawdown from the equity high, and review before resuming.

Lots against contracts

On FundedNext, size in lots. One standard lot of a USD-quoted major is about $10 per pip, so a 25-pip stop risks $250 and a $500 unit buys two lots. A 50-pip stop buys one. The pip value changes with the pair and the account currency, so the calculation has to be run per instrument.

On FTUK, size in contracts. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 and a $500 unit buys sixteen contracts before the cap. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100 and the same unit buys five contracts.

The position counts look very different and the dollar risk is identical. That is why a trader moving between the two markets must rebuild the sizing habit rather than translate a number: a two-lot forex position and a sixteen-contract futures position are the same risk and nothing else about them is similar.

Both need a buffer. Spreads widen and stops slip, and the slipped fill is what the floor sees. Adding ten to twenty percent to the planned risk changes the size by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the unit. On the trailing side that buffer matters more, because the floor has already recorded the high.

Daily stops and their bases

FundedNext applies a daily stop expressed as a percentage of balance. FTUK applies a daily stop on most plans. The expression differs and the effect is similar: a session ends at a defined figure rather than at the trader's discretion.

The basis is the question to settle. A limit measured on equity counts floating losses, so a position that is $1,500 underwater has consumed the allowance before it is closed. A limit measured on realised results counts only closed trades. The first produces better behaviour and tighter statements; the second permits a larger floating loss.

The reset time is the second question. Forex sessions roll at a fixed hour tied to New York; futures sessions roll at their own defined time. A position carried across the roll can be attributed to either day, which matters for a swing method and matters more on a product with a trailing floor.

The third question is whether the daily stop is per account or per position. A limit applied per position allows several positions to breach the intent of the rule at once, and the difference only becomes visible on the third trade of a busy session.

Consistency by model

FTUK applies a consistency condition; FundedNext's position varies by model. That makes the consistency question a filter rather than a comparison: on the FundedNext side, a trader whose method is lumpy should choose the model without a minimum, and a trader whose method is smooth is unaffected either way.

The test is the share of profit from the best day. Take sixty sessions, total the profit, and compute that share. Above a quarter, a consistency rule will bind and the plan needs a daily profit cap to flatten the distribution. Below a quarter, the rule is unlikely to change anything.

On the futures side the condition interacts with the trail in a useful way. A trail rewards banking profit; a consistency rule rewards spreading it. Both push toward small, closed, repeatable sessions, which is why a tapered unit works on that product without further adjustment.

Confirm when the condition applies: during the evaluation, during funding, or at the payout request. A test at payout allows freedom during the attempt; a test during funding shapes every session. The answer belongs in the same document as the terms.

One detail is easy to overlook on a product family: the minimum-day count. Where it is absent, the calendar is the trader's choice, and a self-imposed minimum of ten sessions before requesting a payout produces a record that looks like a process. Where it is present, the count has to be planned alongside the target, because a fast pass still has to wait.

One-off fees and retry maths

Because both products charge once per attempt, the annual cost is the fee times the number of attempts. At an illustrative $450 per attempt, one pass costs $450, two cost $900 and three cost $1,350. Duration does not enter the calculation, which makes patience affordable on both sides.

The expected cost is the fee divided by the pass probability. At a 30% probability, $450 becomes $1,500 per funded account. At 20%, it becomes $2,250. That is the number to compare with a realistic monthly profit, not the headline fee.

The lever that improves the probability is a smaller unit, and it is available on both products. A unit a third lower usually converts a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown, and because the fee is fixed, the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

The second lever is the model choice on the FundedNext side. Choosing the mechanic that matches the method is worth more than any fee discount, because it changes the probability rather than the price. On the futures side the equivalent choice is the plan with a daily stop, where one exists.

Two platform stacks

FundedNext lists MT4, MT5 and cTrader. FTUK lists Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader. There is no overlap, so the workflow decision is settled by the market decision rather than by preference.

Within the forex stack, MT4 is the familiar hedging platform with the largest library of expert advisors; MT5 adds a modern backtester but defaults to netting, which changes how multiple entries on one symbol are managed; cTrader is the level-based option with a cleaner order book.

Within the futures stack, Tradovate is the lightest front end, NinjaTrader is the programmable one, and TradingView is the analysis-first option whose execution layer is newer. A trader who depends on precise stop handling should test TradingView's behaviour before relying on it.

Data costs differ as well. Forex data is usually bundled with the platform; futures data is often billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Confirm what the plan includes before comparing the fees, because a cheap fee with an expensive feed is not cheap.

Forex hours against futures sessions

Forex trades from Sunday evening to Friday evening, with the deepest liquidity in the London and New York overlap. Futures trade nearly continuously, with the regular US session carrying most of the liquidity and the overnight session thinner.

The practical difference is the shape of the day. A forex trader in Europe can work the London session in their morning and the New York overlap in the afternoon. A futures trader outside the US works the evening or the night, and the regular session is the only block with reliable depth.

The second difference is the calendar. Forex has a continuous week with a Sunday open that can gap through a stop; futures have a daily settlement and holidays. Both require the trader to know when the account is exposed and when it is flat.

The availability test is the same on both: mark the hours you can genuinely watch for four weeks and check whether the liquid block of the instrument falls inside them. If it does not, the evaluation is being traded on hope.

Payout and review

FundedNext releases a profit share on a fixed cycle after the first payout; FTUK pays after qualification. The difference that matters is the time from a profitable month to cleared funds and the conditions that can interrupt it.

Both review conduct before releasing profit. On a trailing product the statements show a floor that moved during a session, which can look unusual to a reviewer. A journal that records the buffer at the start and end of each session answers the question before it is asked.

The paperwork is unglamorous and it is where payments stall. Names, addresses and the funding instrument have to line up, and a since-replaced card pushes the request into a queue. Finish the checks while the target is still being worked.

Two questions decide what the headline split really means: whether it applies to gross or net profit, and whether the funded stage inherits the evaluation's rules. Get both answers before the fee, not after the pass.

Releases and gaps

Both markets react to the same macro calendar, with different mechanics. Forex spreads widen sharply before a release and the Sunday open can gap through a stop. Futures react just as fast, and the overnight session carries the gap risk instead.

Restrictions vary by plan. A window of two minutes around a release is a different rule from a window of thirty, and "no new positions" differs from "no holding". The clause is short, rarely on the marketing page and enforced at review.

The conservative plan is the same on both: be flat into the scheduled release and re-enter after the first half hour. On forex, flatten before the weekend as well unless the plan permits weekend holding and the position can survive a gap.

A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the buffer can survive. On the trailing product that calculation is stricter, because the gap can lift the floor and consume the buffer in the same move.

Which trader fits which

FundedNext fits a forex trader who wants a choice of structures and can identify which model matches their method. A trader with occasional deep retracements belongs on the static two-phase version; a trader who takes profit at fixed levels can use a trailing one-step version without giving up much.

FTUK fits a futures trader who already works a Tradovate, TradingView or NinjaTrader workflow, can watch the US session, and whose method closes positions rather than holding for a large move. The trail rewards precision and punishes give-backs.

The tie-breaker is the market. A trader competent in forex will underperform on a futures evaluation regardless of the rulebook, because the sizing, session and platform assumptions do not transfer. Choosing the market you already trade is worth more than any rule comparison.

If both are accessible, simulate each on the same hundred trades with the same dollar risk and compare the maximum drawdown. Whichever survives with the smaller figure is the structure the method already fits.

What to confirm first

The first question is which model you are buying, and what floor that model uses. The second is whether the consistency condition applies to that model and at what stage.

The third is the daily stop and its basis. The fourth is the fee structure and whether a retry is charged again. The fifth is the position cap, in lots or contracts.

The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, automation and third-party trading. Each is short, each is enforced, and none is reliably on the comparison page.

Keep the replies in one dated file. Ten minutes of filing is what stands between a settled question and an argument held over a statement six weeks later.

A thirty-day plan for either

Spend the opening week at half size doing nothing but recording. Write down the buffer before each entry, what the floor did after a winner, and the spread actually paid at each fill. The week produces a small table of facts about the account, which is worth more than a target attempt.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recompute the buffer each morning on the trailing product and weekly on the static one. This is where the target is earned and where compression begins.

The final week is deliberately dull: a third of the unit, no new instrument, and the payout file checked line by line. Attempts are lost more often in that week than in the first.

One card carries the whole plan: buffer in dollars, unit in lots or contracts, and the session stop converted into a loss count. Update the first number every morning; the other two follow from it.

Common questions

Which floor does FundedNext use?

It depends on the model. The two-phase version is static, while some one-step variants trail. The model name, not the brand, is the unit that carries the rulebook, so confirm the floor for the exact plan.

Does FTUK trail against equity or balance?

The published shape describes a trail on peak equity, which includes open profit. That is the stricter definition, so a give-back on an open winner can consume buffer even when the trade closes flat.

Are both fees one-off?

Yes. Both charge once per attempt, so duration does not add cost and retries are the dominant expense. Model the annual cost as the fee multiplied by the expected number of attempts.

Can I trade forex on FTUK?

No. FTUK evaluates futures on US session hours, and its platform list is Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader. FundedNext is the forex and CFD product in this comparison.

How do I size a $100,000 account on each?

Divide the buffer by the losses you will tolerate, then convert. On forex that means pips × pip value × lots; on futures it means ticks × tick value × contracts. The dollar risk should match even though the position counts look nothing alike.

Which has the simpler consistency rule?

FTUK applies a consistency condition on the product described. FundedNext's rules vary by model, with some plans having no minimum, so the simpler answer is to check the exact plan rather than the brand.
