How Much Do Prop Firm Traders Make

The numbers that decide the cost

Evaluation economics are a multiplication problem: fee per attempt times expected attempts, plus platform costs, compared with the net value of the first few payouts. The headline fee is only one input. The consequence is cumulative. Fees, retries, data costs and payout timing add up to a total cost of reaching the first withdrawal, and that total is the number worth comparing across routes.

The return on the fee, honestly

A realistic range, not a headline

A funded trader's income depends on three variables: the account size, the profit split and the amount of profit actually withdrawn. A $100,000 account with a 90% split and a 2% monthly return produces $1,800 before fees; the same account with a 0.5% monthly return produces $450. The split is the least important of the three because it applies to a number that the trader has to produce first.

The distribution is skewed. Most funded traders make little or nothing, a smaller group makes a modest monthly income, and a very small group makes a living. Marketing pages show the top of the distribution because that is what sells evaluations. A plan built on the average of the top decile is a plan built on an outlier.

The costs that reduce the headline

Start with the gross profit, apply the split, subtract the platform and data costs, subtract the evaluation fees and resets, and subtract the payout processor fees. The result is the net income. A trader who makes $2,000 gross on a $100,000 account with a 90% split, $60 of monthly data costs and a $300 evaluation fee has a very different first-month result from the headline.

The first payout is usually the least representative because it carries the setup costs. The second and third payouts are a better measure of the ongoing income. A trader who wants to know whether the activity is viable should model six months, not one good month.

The account size illusion

A larger account does not automatically produce more income. The risk per trade is usually a small percentage of the account, so the dollar risk grows with the size, but so does the target. A trader who cannot produce a return on a $50,000 account will not produce one on a $200,000 account; the larger account simply makes the same mistakes more expensive.

The right sequence is to prove the process on the smallest viable account, then scale the size once the payout rhythm is stable. A trader who buys the largest account first is paying for a larger test, not a larger income.

Common questions

How much can a funded trader make per month?

It depends on the return, the account size and the split. A 2% monthly return on a $100,000 account at a 90% split is about $1,800 before costs. Most traders produce less, and the distribution is heavily skewed.

What costs reduce the payout?

Platform and data fees, evaluation and reset fees, and payout processor fees. The first payout carries the setup costs, so it is usually the least representative.

Should I start with a large account?

Usually not. Prove the process on the smallest viable account, then scale once the payout rhythm is stable. A larger account makes the same mistakes more expensive.
