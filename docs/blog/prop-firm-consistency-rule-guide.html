The prop firm consistency rule, explained

The consistency rule caps how much of your total profit can come from one trading day. It is the reason a passed evaluation can still refuse a payout, and it is almost entirely a matter of arithmetic — which means it can be planned for.

Key takeaways

  • A 30% consistency rule means your largest day must be no more than 30% of total profit — so total profit must reach at least 3.33× your best day.
  • Most forex firms apply it at payout only; futures firms apply it at payout almost universally, and a few apply it during the evaluation too.
  • It is a distribution rule, not a performance rule: the fix is more ordinary winning days, not better trades.
  • Planning for it during the evaluation costs days, not money — and it removes the most common first-payout refusal.

What the rule actually says

The consistency rule places a ceiling on the contribution of a single trading day to total profit. The most common formulation is 30%: no single day may account for more than 30% of the profit shown when a payout is requested. Some firms use 40% or 50%, some measure it per day rather than per session, and a few express it as a maximum best-day profit in dollars rather than a percentage.

The purpose, from the firm's perspective, is straightforward. Firms fund traders whose results look repeatable, and a single outsized day is the least repeatable way to reach a profit target. A trader who makes $9,000 in one session and $1,000 over the following month is a different risk to a firm than one who made $10,000 across six weeks, even though both hit a $10,000 target. The rule is a filter for distribution, and it is applied to the withdrawal rather than to the evaluation at most forex firms, though not all.

There is a second, less discussed consequence. Because consistency is measured against total profit, the rule interacts with the funded account's profit threshold. If a firm requires 8% of profit before a payout and enforces 30% consistency, the best day must remain under 30% of whatever is accumulated — meaning the trader cannot simply reach the threshold quickly and withdraw.

The arithmetic, with worked examples

The formula is simple: maximum permitted best day = total profit × consistency percentage. Rearranged, the requirement is that total profit ≥ best day ÷ consistency percentage.

Consistency ruleBest dayTotal profit requiredAdditional profit needed beyond the best day
30%$1,000$3,334$2,334
30%$3,000$10,000$7,000
30%$6,000$20,000$14,000
40%$6,000$15,000$9,000
50%$6,000$12,000$6,000

Two things stand out from that table. First, the multiple is what matters: a 30% rule demands 3.33× your best day in total profit, and the required additional profit scales linearly with the size of the dominant day. Second, a smaller best day is not merely better — it is dramatically better. Halving the best day from $6,000 to $3,000 halves the total profit required from $20,000 to $10,000.

Worked example: a futures payout blocked by one day

A trader finishes a futures evaluation with a $6,000 best day and $9,500 of total profit, then requests the first payout after the minimum trading days. Under a 30% rule, total profit needs to reach at least $20,000 for a $6,000 best day — so the request is refused even though the account is profitable and the evaluation was passed cleanly.

The remedy is not to trade bigger; it is to add ordinary days. Another $10,500 of profit spread across normal sessions would satisfy the rule, and the important detail is that those additional days must stay small. A second $6,000 day would push the required total to $40,000 and make the problem worse rather than better.

Worked example: the same target, three different distributions

Three traders each make $10,000 total on a funded account with a 30% consistency rule.

  • Trader A: one day of $7,000, five days of $600. Best day is 70% of total. Payout refused until total profit reaches $23,334.
  • Trader B: one day of $3,000, ten days of $700. Best day is 30% of total. Payout permitted immediately — exactly at the threshold, with no margin.
  • Trader C: best day of $2,000, remaining $8,000 spread across twelve sessions. Best day is 20% of total. Payout permitted with comfortable room.

All three have identical total profit and identical evaluation results. Only the distribution differs, and only Trader A is blocked. That asymmetry is the entire content of the consistency rule, and it is why planning the distribution during the evaluation is cheaper than repairing it afterwards.

Which firms enforce it, and when

There are three patterns in the market, and the difference between them matters more than the percentage itself.

PatternFirms that typically use itWhat it means for you
No consistency ruleMost forex and CFD evaluations, including several of the largest firmsProfit distribution is irrelevant to passing and to the first payout
Payout-stage onlyThe majority of futures firms, plus some forex firms on funded accountsAn evaluation can be passed with a dominant day; the withdrawal must wait until the distribution is fixed
Evaluation and payout stageA minority of firms, typically those with promotional evaluation pricingThe pace and size of every session is constrained from day one, and the target may need to be exceeded to satisfy the ratio

Two practical notes on reading a firm's rule. First, firms often describe consistency in the funded-account terms rather than in the evaluation terms, so a trader who only reads the evaluation page concludes the rule does not exist — until a payout request is refused. Second, the percentage is not always expressed as a percentage: some firms state a maximum best-day profit in dollars, which is simpler to plan around but changes the arithmetic when the firm also scales account sizes.

If it applies during the evaluation

An evaluation-stage consistency rule is the harder version, and it changes the plan from the first session.

The constraint is not that you must avoid big days — it is that your total profit must remain at least 3.33× your best day at the moment you reach the target (on a 30% rule). If the target is $10,000 and you have a $4,000 day, you cannot pass at $10,000; you must reach $13,334 before the ratio is satisfied. That is a 33% larger target than the firm advertised, arrived at by arithmetic rather than by choice.

Three habits prevent that trap.

  • Cap any single day at a third of the profit target. On a $10,000 target, close the day once it is $3,300 up. This is the pre-emptive version of the rule and it keeps the required multiple satisfied automatically.
  • Track the ratio, not just the balance. The number that matters is best-day ÷ total profit. Keeping a running note of it costs nothing and turns an invisible threshold into a visible one.
  • Prefer more sessions over larger ones. If the plan allows twenty sessions, spreading the target across them produces a ratio under 25% by construction, with no need to manage anything.

There is one counter-intuitive consequence worth stating: on a firm that enforces consistency during the evaluation, a slow, undramatic pass is not merely safer — it is the only way the advertised target is actually the target. A trader who reaches 10% with a 40% best day has not reached the target at all.

If it applies only at payout

The payout-stage version is more forgiving because the problem can be repaired with time rather than avoided with discipline.

The mechanism is that consistency is measured on the profit shown when a withdrawal is requested. A trader with a dominant day has two options: wait and accumulate additional small days until the ratio is satisfied, or request a partial withdrawal calculated so the ratio holds at the amount requested. Firms differ on whether partial withdrawals are permitted and how the ratio is measured in that case, so the specific answer belongs to the firm's terms rather than to a general rule.

What makes the payout stage more workable is that the dominant day does not invalidate anything. The evaluation was passed, the account is funded, and the only constraint is on the timing and size of the withdrawal. That converts a failure into a delay — typically a few weeks of ordinary trading — which is a materially better position than being unable to pass at all.

It is also the reason our own plan caps individual days at a third of the target even on firms that apply consistency only at payout. The habit costs nothing during the evaluation on a static-drawdown structure, and it means the first withdrawal is available on request rather than after a repair period.

How to fix a failed consistency check

A consistency failure is a distribution problem, and distribution problems are solved with volume rather than with performance. There are four viable routes, in the order they usually make sense.

1. Add ordinary winning days

The direct fix. If total profit must rise so that the existing best day falls to 30% of it, the required increase is mechanical: required total = best day ÷ 0.30, and the gap is the difference between that figure and current profit. On a $6,000 best day with $9,500 banked, the gap is $10,500 — spread across sessions sized at a third of a day's budget, that is several weeks of boring execution rather than one large push.

The discipline required is to keep those additional days small. A trader who fixes a consistency problem with a second large day has simply raised the bar: the required total scales with the new best day, so a $9,000 day would demand $30,000 in total.

2. Request a smaller payout

Where the firm permits partial withdrawals and measures consistency against the amount withdrawn rather than the account's total profit, a smaller request can satisfy the ratio immediately. This is firm-specific and worth confirming in writing, because the two measurement bases produce different answers and the confusion between them is common.

3. Set a hard daily cap going forward

Whatever route is used, the fix only holds if the next session cannot recreate the problem. A cap of a third of the eventual total — enforced in the plan rather than in the moment — is what prevents a repaired ratio from being destroyed by one good morning.

4. Change firms for the next account

If consistency applies during the evaluation and the target has been met but the ratio has not, the arithmetic can make the advertised target misleading. For a subsequent account, choosing a firm that applies consistency only at payout — or not at all — removes a constraint that costs days without adding anything to the funded account.

Firms with no consistency rule

Most forex and CFD evaluations apply no consistency rule during the evaluation, and several apply none at payout either. The rules matrix lists which firms enforce it and at which stage, and the 2026 comparison explains what each structure means in practice.

The trade-off is worth stating plainly. Firms without a consistency rule are easier to pass and easier to withdraw from, and their profit splits or challenge pricing are sometimes slightly less generous to compensate. Firms with a strict payout-stage rule often advertise attractive splits precisely because the rule reduces the amount that leaves the firm in the first months. When comparing two firms, a 90% split with a 30% consistency rule can pay out less, sooner, than an 80% split with none — and the difference is invisible in a headline table.

One further consideration applies to traders who intend to run several accounts. Consistency rules compound across a portfolio, because each account has to satisfy its own ratio independently. Three accounts at firms with payout-stage consistency rules require three separately distributed profit histories, whereas three accounts at firms without the rule can be traded on identical terms. For traders scaling deliberately, that is a meaningful operational difference rather than a marketing footnote.

Consistency and the daily loss limit pull in opposite directions

Two rules govern the shape of every session on an account with a payout-stage consistency requirement, and they encourage opposite behaviour.

The daily loss limit says: the day's budget is scarce, so use it efficiently when a setup appears. The consistency rule says: daily profit is expensive, because a large day raises the ratio you must satisfy. A trader obeying only the first rule tends to take the biggest available opportunity each day, which produces exactly the concentrated distribution the second rule punishes.

The resolution is to treat the two as separate budgets with different ceilings. The loss budget is a floor on caution — it caps how much can be lost. The consistency rule is a ceiling on ambition — it caps how much should be won in one day. A session that is profitable beyond roughly a third of the eventual total is not a good session; it is a liability.

SituationInstinctWhat the rules require
A strong setup appears 30 minutes into the dayTake it at plan sizeTake it at plan size — the loss budget governs the size, not the day's ambition
The day is already +0.9% on a 5%, $10,000 targetTake the next setup to build momentumConsider closing: at +$900 the day is near the third-of-target cap
The day is at +$3,300 on a $10,000 targetPress while it is workingStop. The session has reached the cap that keeps a 30% ratio satisfied at target
A losing morning, −$1,500Recover the dayReduce size, not increase it: the daily budget is still the binding constraint

The fourth row is where the two rules reinforce rather than conflict. After a loss, the consistency rule gives no permission to increase risk: larger size after a loss raises the variance of the day, and a day that turns into a large winner creates the distribution problem the rule exists to prevent. The only correct response to a losing morning is a smaller position and, usually, a shorter session.

One consequence of that discipline is worth stating because it sounds wrong: on accounts with a 30% payout-stage rule, the optimal daily result is not the largest available profit but a repeatable one. Traders who internalise this find the funded phase easier than the evaluation, because the funded account's constraints reward the same behaviour the pacing discipline already required. Traders who do not usually discover the rule at the first payout request — which is the most expensive possible time to learn it.

Four myths about the consistency rule

Myth 1: it only matters on futures accounts. Futures firms enforce it most consistently at payout, but a number of forex and CFD firms apply equivalent conditions on funded accounts. The rule is not a futures feature; it is a payout feature, and it appears wherever a firm wants to encourage repeatable results.

Myth 2: it can be solved by trading smaller everywhere. Reducing every position lowers both the best day and total profit proportionally, which leaves the ratio unchanged. What changes the ratio is the distribution: more days, each of them moderate. Size reduction helps only if it is applied to the largest days rather than uniformly.

Myth 3: a big day is always bad. A large day is not a breach; it is a debt against future ordinary days. On a $10,000 target with a $4,000 best day, the account needs $13,334 in total — an extra $3,334 of ordinary trading. The day is affordable, just expensive, and the arithmetic is fully predictable at the moment it happens.

All four myths survive because they sound like discipline. Capping daily profit sounds like timidity, and refusing to press a winning day feels like leaving money on the table — right up until the first payout request is refused and the repair costs three weeks of ordinary trading. The rule is not a judgement about how well you trade. It is a statement about how a firm reads a profit history, and it can be satisfied without trading any better than the target requires.

Each of these myths leads to the same wrong remedy: adjusting effort rather than distribution. The rule does not reward trading harder, trading less, or trading on a second account. It rewards spreading the same profit across more sessions, which is a scheduling decision rather than a trading one.

Myth 4: the rule can be worked around with a second account. Consistency is measured per account, so trading a second account does not change the first account's ratio — and firms that detect correlated positions across accounts treat them as a single exposure, which can create a separate problem. The rule is satisfied by distribution within the account, not by spreading trades across accounts.

Frequently asked about the consistency rule

What is the consistency rule in one sentence?

It caps how much of your total profit may come from a single trading day — commonly 30% — which means total profit must reach at least 3.33 times your best day before a payout is allowed.

Which is worse: a consistency rule during the evaluation or at payout?

The evaluation-stage version is harder. A payout-stage rule delays a withdrawal that can be fixed by adding ordinary winning days; an evaluation-stage rule can mean the advertised profit target is not actually the target, because the ratio must also be satisfied.

How do I calculate whether I pass a 30% rule?

Divide your best day by 0.30. On a $4,000 best day, total profit must reach $13,334. If your profit target is $10,000 and you have a $4,000 best day, you cannot finish at the target — you must exceed it by $3,334 or reduce the size of your largest day in future.

Does the rule count a day or a session?

It depends on the firm. Most measure a calendar trading day in the account's server timezone, which means a session that spans midnight is split across two days for consistency purposes. A few measure per session. Where a trade runs overnight, the firm's definition matters and is worth checking before assuming either way.

Can a single winning trade break the rule?

Yes. A single exceptional trade can produce a dominant day, and the ratio is measured on daily profit rather than on trade count. This is why capping daily profit — not just per-trade risk — is the practical defence.

Do losing days count toward consistency?

No. The rule measures the concentration of profit, so losing days reduce total profit and make the ratio worse rather than better. This is why a drawdown during a repair period extends the wait rather than shortening it.

What happens if I fail the consistency check?

At payout stage, the withdrawal is refused until the distribution satisfies the rule — the account is not closed. Where the rule applies during the evaluation, the evaluation cannot be completed until the ratio is met, which may require exceeding the stated profit target.

How do I avoid the problem entirely?

Cap every session at roughly a third of the profit target, and if you have a strong day, close early rather than extending it. A trader who never has a day above a third of the target automatically satisfies a 30% rule at the moment the target is reached.

Are there firms with no consistency rule at all?

Yes, and they are mostly forex and CFD evaluations. The rules matrix lists which firms enforce it and when, so a trader who finds the rule unmanageable can choose a firm where it does not apply rather than fighting it.

Does a passing service work around consistency rules?

It plans for them. On firms that apply the rule at payout, sessions are capped at a third of the target so the first withdrawal is available on request; on firms that apply it during the evaluation, the pace is set by the ratio rather than by the target. Either way it is arithmetic, not improvisation.

How much extra time does a repair take?

It depends on the gap. Fixing a $6,000 best day from $9,500 of profit requires $10,500 more profit, which at a third of a day's budget on a $100,000 account is several weeks of ordinary trading. Reducing the size of the dominant day in the first place is far cheaper than repairing it afterwards.

The 20%, 40% and 50% variants, and why the number is not the whole story

Firms describe consistency in several ways, and two rules with the same percentage can behave very differently depending on what the denominator is and when it is measured. Before planning around a threshold, identify which of the four common formulations applies.

FormulationDenominatorMeasuredEffect
Best day share of profitTotal profit at requestAt payoutThe most common; delays rather than blocks
Best day share of targetProfit targetDuring evaluationStricter; penalties a fast pass
Maximum best-day dollarsFixed dollar capAt payout or at closeSize-dependent, easy to plan around
Average-day multipleMean daily profitAt payoutPunishes both tails, including losing days

The first row is the one most forex firms use, and it has a forgiving property: the requirement rises with the size of the dominant day, so the remedy is always available — add ordinary profit. The second row is harder, because a single large day during the evaluation can fail the account outright, and the trader has no opportunity to repair it. The fourth is the least understood and often the most disruptive, since an unusually large losing day can also distort an average-based measure.

Why a 50% rule can be stricter than a 30% rule

It sounds impossible, and it happens regularly. A firm with a 50% threshold measured against a profit target, applied during the evaluation, is stricter than a firm with a 30% threshold measured against total profit at payout. In the first, one day at 50% of the target ends the attempt; in the second, a day at 60% of profit merely delays the withdrawal until more profit arrives.

The practical lesson is to read the sentence that follows the percentage, not the percentage itself. "No single day may account for more than 30% of total profit" and "no single day may exceed 30% of the profit target" differ by one word and are different sports.

If you are already blocked by the ratio

Most traders discover the rule at the payout request, which is the worst possible moment because the account is profitable and the instinct is to protect the gain by trading less — which is correct — while also wanting the payout to be worth having — which is the trap.

Step one: compute the gap

Divide the best day by the threshold percentage. The result is the total profit the account must show. Subtract current profit; the difference is the amount of ordinary profit still required. It is usually smaller than it feels: a $2,500 best day under a 30% rule needs $8,333 total, so an account at $6,000 needs another $2,333.

Step two: size for an ordinary month, not a heroic week

The additional profit has to arrive without creating a new dominant day, which means the per-trade risk must fall. If normal risk is 1.5%, cutting to 0.75% doubles the number of sessions the work takes and halves the chance that any single session becomes the new best day. It also halves the chance of losing the gain that is already there, which matters more than speed at this point.

Step three: do not request early

A request that is refused for distribution often triggers a second request the following week, and some firms count refusals or require a waiting period before another attempt. Asking once, when the ratio is comfortably inside the threshold, is faster in practice than asking three times and being refused twice.

Step four: if the ratio cannot be repaired, change the plan

There are accounts where the dominant day is simply too large relative to any achievable ordinary profit within the payout window. In that case the correct decision is to keep trading the account with tiny risk until the ratio normalises, or to accept that this account will pay out later than planned. Neither option involves adding size to "get it over with", which is the move that turns a delayed payout into a failed account.

Designing an evaluation that will not trip the rule

The cheapest fix is upstream. During the evaluation, cap any single session at roughly 20% of the total profit target. On a $10,000 target, that is $2,000 a day — not a small number, and achievable without concentrating the account's whole result into one Tuesday.

A trader who respects that cap arrives at the payout stage with a distribution that already satisfies most plausible thresholds, and the remaining work is administrative. A trader who ignores it arrives at the payout stage with a profitable account and a blocked withdrawal, which is a strange thing to have paid for.

The cap also has a second benefit that has nothing to do with the rule: it forces a trader to leave the table while winning, which is the single behaviour most associated with accounts that survive their first month. The consistency rule, in that sense, is a firm imposing a discipline that profitable traders adopt voluntarily.

It also explains why firms with consistency rules tend to report lower failure rates among funded traders than firms without them, despite the extra hurdle. The rule does not just filter distributions; it filters for the temperament that produces them.

For a trader, that reframes the rule from an obstacle into a free diagnostic: an account that keeps tripping a consistency check is telling you that your profit depends on rare, large days. That is worth knowing regardless of which firm you trade with, because rare large days are exactly what a bad quarter removes.

It also makes the rule easier to explain to yourself when a payout is delayed: the firm is not questioning the profit, only its shape.
