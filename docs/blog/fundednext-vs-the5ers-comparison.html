FundedNext vs The5ers: which rulebook fits your trading

Both firms evaluate forex and CFD traders, so the choice is not about the market. It is about structure: how many phases, how the target is sized, whether a consistency condition applies at payout, and how the account is meant to grow afterwards. Those four differences decide which product a given method can actually complete, and the fee is the smallest of them.

Key takeaways

  • One-step and two-step structures fail in different ways: the one-step concentrates risk into a single target, the two-step spreads it across two chance periods.
  • A one-step plan is not easier. It usually pairs the shorter path with a tighter threshold or a consistency condition.
  • A consistency rule is a payout condition, not a trading condition, unless it appears in the evaluation terms.
  • Scaling programmes reward time and consistency rather than a single good month.
  • Both firms run cycle-based payouts, so the first withdrawal is a scheduling exercise rather than an on-demand request.

FundedNext and The5ers are both forex and CFD evaluators, which means the instruments, the platform family and the general shape of the rules are familiar from one to the other. The differences that matter are structural: how many phases the evaluation has, how the target is expressed, whether a consistency threshold applies before a payout, and what the account is designed to become after funding.

As with any firm comparison, the figures move. What follows describes the published structure and the mechanics behind it. Confirm the current targets, thresholds and payout terms on each plan page before buying.

One step versus two steps

A one-step evaluation asks for a single target on a single account before funding. A two-step evaluation asks for a smaller target, then a second smaller target on a new phase, before funding.

AspectOne-stepTwo-step
Target sizeLarger single targetTwo smaller targets
Chance periodsOneTwo
Failure pointsOne account to protectTwo accounts to protect
Typical loss allowanceTighter relative to the targetWider relative to each target
Consistency conditionCommon at payoutLess common, but plan-dependent
Time to fundingShorter if successfulLonger, with a reset between phases

The fourth row is the trade-off in one line. A one-step plan compresses the same journey into a single account, so the ratio between the profit required and the loss allowed is tighter. A two-step plan splits the journey, which gives two smaller asks but also two opportunities to fail and a count that usually resets at the phase change.

The sixth row is why traders are drawn to the one-step. Fewer phases means less calendar, and for a trader with a high-conviction method the shorter path is genuinely attractive. The cost is that the single account has to survive the whole target without the halfway reset that a two-step plan provides.

The rows that differ

Because both offer more than one plan, the comparison is really between the specific products a trader is choosing from. A two-step plan at one firm and a one-step plan at the other are not the same product in different wrapping; they are different tests, and the fee comparison between them is close to meaningless until the phase count and the consistency condition are matched.

The fifth row is the one to read most carefully. A consistency condition changes the shape of a pass, not just the eligibility of a payout: it forces profit to be spread across days, which in turn forces the trader to keep trading after a strong session. Whether that is a constraint depends on the method, but it is never neutral.

Why a one-step plan is not easier

Intuition says fewer phases means an easier evaluation. The arithmetic says otherwise, because the target and the floor move together.

MeasureOne-stepTwo-step, per phase
Target10%8% then 5%
Maximum loss6%10%
Target-to-floor ratio1.67 : 10.80 : 1 then 0.50 : 1
Losses allowed at 0.5% risk1220 per phase
Total target required10%13% across both phases

The third row is the whole story. On the one-step plan, every 1% of profit requires 0.6% of loss allowance to be spent — the account has to travel further relative to the room it has. On the two-step plan, the target is smaller than the floor in both phases, so each phase is a lower-risk ask even though the total profit required is larger.

The fifth row is the counter-argument. A two-step plan asks for more total profit, and the second phase requires a new count. A trader who completes targets reliably but hates repetition may find the one-step faster in calendar terms. The point is not that one is better; it is that "one step" is not a synonym for "easier", and a trader who assumes it is will size for the wrong ratio.

The consistency condition

A consistency rule limits how much of the total profit can come from a single day, or how far the best day may exceed the average. It is usually a payout condition rather than an evaluation condition, and that distinction changes how to trade it.

Where the rule sitsWhat it constrainsHow to manage it
Evaluation termsHow the target may be reachedSpread profit from the first trade; cannot be fixed later
Payout terms onlyEligibility for a withdrawalAdd winning days until the share falls below the cap
BothThe whole cycleDesign the distribution before the first trade

The first row is the hard case. If the target must be reached with a bounded best day, a single large session creates a distribution problem that cannot be repaired without giving profit back. The second row is softer: the account is funded, and the repair is more trading rather than more risk.

A worked illustration. Total profit of $8,000 with a 30% cap means no day above $2,400. Four days of $2,000 satisfy it; one day of $8,000 does not, and neither does a sequence of $6,000 and $2,000. The number of profitable days needed is a function of the cap, so the cap should be converted into a day count before the attempt rather than discovered at the request.

Where the rule sits on each firm's plan decides whether it is a trading problem or an administrative one. Read the evaluation terms and the payout terms as separate documents; the condition often appears in only one of them.

Scaling and account growth

Both firms offer a path to a larger account, and both paths reward consistency rather than a single strong month.

Growth routeHow it is earnedTrade-off
Scaling planConsistent profit over a number of cyclesSlow; depends on the firm granting the increase
Additional accountsPurchase or programme eligibilityFaster; adds fixed cost and correlation risk
Higher-tier programmeTrack record on a lower tierRequires a clean history; resets the count

The first row is the cheapest route and the least controllable. A scaling plan usually requires a stated number of profitable cycles with no rule breaches, and it is granted at the firm's discretion. That makes it excellent for a trader who is already profitable and poor for one who needs capital now.

The second row is the route most traders actually take, and it carries the correlation problem described elsewhere: several accounts holding the same view are one position at multiple size. A trader adding accounts should add them with independent ideas or across firms, not by mirroring the same trade.

The third row is worth noting because it changes the rulebook. Moving to a higher tier usually means a new count, a new consistency condition or a tighter floor, so the track record that earned the invitation does not transfer as a head start. Budget for the new terms rather than assuming the old plan carries over, and re-read the conduct clauses at the same time, because tier changes often move more than the numbers: automation limits, weekend holding and the payout request process are all candidates for revision in a typical tier change, and each one can change the method rather than the schedule.

Payout cycles and the first withdrawal

Both firms pay on a cycle rather than on demand, which makes the first withdrawal a scheduling exercise. The relevant questions are the interval, the minimum amount, the profit split and whether a consistency condition applies at the request.

StageWhat to confirmWhy it changes the date
FundingWhether a seasoning period appliesDelays eligibility independent of profit
First cycleThe cutoff date and the processing windowA miss costs a full interval
RequestMinimum amount and splitDecides whether the whole profit is withdrawable
ReviewIdentity and method verificationFirst request only; reusable afterwards
SettlementThe rail and its feeAdds days and a fixed cost

The second row is the one traders underestimate. A cycle date that falls two days after eligibility turns a two-day wait into a month, and the arithmetic is visible before purchase if the plan states the interval. Combined with a seasoning period, the first payout can land four to six weeks after the target is met even when nothing goes wrong.

The fifth row is where small payouts lose value. A fixed fee on a first withdrawal that is deliberately small — because the trader wants to test the process — consumes a large share of it. The cheap first test is to withdraw the largest amount the plan allows, not the smallest.

Where each structure fails

StructureCharacteristic failurePrevention
One-stepOversizing to reach a large target inside a tight ratioCap risk so the floor survives a normal streak
One-stepConcentrated pass that fails consistency at payoutSpread profit across days from the start
Two-stepComplacency in the second phaseKeep the phase-one risk plan unchanged
Two-stepCount reset between phasesPlan both counts into the schedule
EitherPost-funding breach before the first payoutTreat funding as a new phase with its own plan

The first two rows are specific to the one-step and they are linked. A large target in a tight ratio encourages size, and size produces the concentrated pass that a consistency rule rejects. The structure does not force that outcome, but it makes it more likely, which is why a one-step plan demands a stricter risk policy than a two-step one.

The third and fourth rows are the two-step equivalents. The second phase looks easier because the target is smaller, and the count reset is easy to forget until the review. Both are planning problems with planning solutions.

Trading two or three setups a weekTwo-stepCounts and targets fit a slow pace

The first row is the clearest discriminator. A trader whose average win is 3% of the account cannot satisfy a 30% best-day cap without adding many days of smaller wins, which in practice means trading differently from the method that produced the edge. For that trader the two-step is not merely easier; it is the only structure that fits.

The sixth row is a reminder that automation fit is decided by conduct clauses, not by phase count. Both firms permit automation with limits, and both exclude strategies that depend on execution speed. That check belongs before the plan choice, because a plan that fits the method but prohibits it is not a fit at all.

Moving between structures

A trader moving from a two-step to a one-step, or the reverse, should change three things before the first trade.

  1. Recompute the target-to-floor ratio. A one-step ratio demands a smaller risk per trade, not the same risk on a larger target.
  2. Re-plan the distribution. Check whether a consistency cap applies and convert it into a minimum number of profitable days.
  3. Re-plan the calendar. Counts per phase, seasoning before payout, and the cycle date that sets the first withdrawal.

Those three adjustments take an afternoon and prevent the most common cross-structure error: carrying a two-step risk plan into a one-step target and discovering that the floor is closer than the plan assumed.

The daily loss and its reset

The daily loss rule is the constraint that ends most attempts, and its two hidden variables are the measurement point and the reset convention. Both firms state these in the terms; the practical work is converting them into a schedule.

VariableWhat to confirmWhy it changes the plan
MeasurementEquity including floating, or closed balanceAn open losing position may already consume the limit
ResetMidnight server time, or the broker's daily closeA position held across the reset starts the new day with its loss counted
ScopeOne limit per account, or per account and per positionCorrelated positions share one budget
ConsequenceSoft session stop or hard account failureDecides whether a bad day is survivable

The fourth row is the one traders assume rather than check. Where a daily breach is a soft stop, the day ends and the account continues. Where it is a failure, a single bad session closes the evaluation regardless of the remaining maximum loss. The difference changes the appropriate risk per trade: with a hard daily failure, the risk must be sized so that a normal losing streak cannot reach the limit in one day.

The second row interacts with holding. A position held through the reset carries its floating result into the new day, which means a trade opened late in one session can start the next with a loss already counted. For a swing method that is a scheduling constraint: entries are better placed early in a session than just before the reset.

Converting the rule into a number is simple: divide the daily limit by the risk per trade to get the number of losses the day can absorb. If that number is below five, the risk is too large for the plan.

Platforms, automation and execution

Both firms operate on the MetaTrader family, so the platform question is about account configuration rather than about which software is available.

ItemWhat to checkEffect on the method
Account modelNetting or hedgingWhether two opposite positions can exist
StopsServer-side by defaultWhether protection survives a disconnect
CommissionPer side or round turnThe real cost per trade
AutomationWhich strategies the conduct clause excludesWhether a system can run at all
SwapRates and triple-swap dayThe carrying cost of a swing position

The first row is the one that changes a strategy. On a netting account, a hedge closes or reduces the existing position rather than opening a second one, which is a different behaviour from the hedging account the method may have been tested on. An automated system written for one model can produce unintended trades on the other.

The fourth row is where most automation questions are settled. Both firms permit rule-based systems and exclude strategies that depend on execution speed, feed differences or order-flow patterns. A higher-timeframe system is inside the permission; a tick scalper is not, whatever the marketing says.

The fifth row matters more than its size suggests. A triple-swap day can consume several days of a swing position's expected profit, and on a small account that is a material change to the edge. Checking the swap schedule before holding through a Wednesday is cheaper than discovering it in the statement.

A short decision sequence

  1. Match the phase count to your distribution. If your average win is large, a one-step consistency cap will reject the pass; choose two-step.
  2. Convert the consistency cap into a day count. Divide total profit by the cap to find the minimum number of profitable days.
  3. Check the daily rule's consequence. A hard daily failure requires smaller risk than a soft session stop.
  4. Confirm the payout gate. Cycle date, minimum amount and whether the count resets at funding.
  5. Check the growth path. Scaling terms matter more than the evaluation fee once the account is funded.

Five questions, each with a numeric answer. Written down, they produce a shortlist quickly, because most plans fail on one of the first two and the rest are administrative.

The sequence also prevents the most common comparison error: choosing on fee. A plan that fits the distribution and the risk policy but costs more is cheaper in expectation than a cheaper plan whose consistency rule rejects the way the trader actually trades, because the rejected plan's cost is not the fee but the fee plus the months spent discovering the mismatch.

The cost model at three sizes

Fees are fixed and payouts scale, so the cheapest plan at one size is not necessarily the cheapest at another. A model, using illustrative figures.

Line$25,000$50,000$100,000
Evaluation fee$150$250$450
Expected attempts at 40% pass rate2.52.52.5
Expected fee cost$375$625$1,125
First gross payout at 8% and 90%$1,800$3,600$7,200
Fee as share of payout20.8%17.4%15.6%
Net after a $30 withdrawal fee$1,770$3,570$7,170

The fifth row shows the effect that matters: the same plan structure costs a larger share of the first payout on a smaller account. The difference between 20.8% and 15.6% is not enormous, but it compounds with every retake and every fixed withdrawal fee.

The second row is the assumption that most changes the answer. At a 40% pass rate the expected number of attempts is 2.5; at 60% it is 1.67, which cuts the fee cost by a third. A trader who overestimates their pass rate underestimates cost at every account size, and the error is largest on the smallest account because the fee is proportionally bigger.

The sixth row adds the withdrawal fee, which is fixed. On a $25,000 account it is 1.7% of the payout; on a $100,000 account it is 0.4%. Small accounts are not worse products, but they are less efficient at absorbing fixed costs, and that is a reason to choose them only when the risk budget genuinely fits.

The model also explains why the first payout matters more than it looks. Its fee share is the highest of any payout, because the fixed costs are spread over the smallest amount. A trader who tests the process with a deliberately small first withdrawal pays the worst effective rate of the account's life; withdrawing the maximum allowed on the first cycle is cheaper per dollar received.

The practical conclusion is not that small accounts are bad. It is that the fee model should be matched to the size: on a small account, a plan with the lowest fixed costs and the widest withdrawal allowance is worth more than a plan with better headline terms and higher fixed charges.

Common questions

Is a one-step challenge easier than a two-step?

Usually not. A one-step plan pairs a larger single target with a tighter loss allowance, so the target-to-floor ratio is harsher. It saves calendar time if you complete it, but it asks more of the same account.

Which firm has the easier consistency rule?

It depends on the plan rather than the firm. Both apply consistency conditions on some programmes and not others. Convert the cap into a number of profitable days and compare that against your method's average win before deciding.

Do both offer scaling?

Yes, through scaling programmes and additional accounts. The terms differ: scaling is usually granted on consistent cycles, while additional accounts are purchased or earned through a programme. Check what each requires and whether it resets the count.

How often can I withdraw?

Both use cycle-based payouts rather than on-demand requests. The interval and the cutoff date determine when a request can be made, and missing a cutoff usually costs a full cycle, which is why the date belongs in the plan before the first trade.

Can I hold positions over the weekend?

It depends on the account type and the plan. Some accounts are flat-only over the weekend, which changes a multi-day method into an intraday one. Confirm before buying rather than after the first Friday.

Are expert advisors allowed on both?

Automation is generally permitted with conduct limits that exclude high-frequency, latency and arbitrage strategies. The limits are the deciding factor, not the phase count, so read the conduct clause on the specific plan.

Which is better for a small account?

Neither has a structural advantage at small size, but both penalise fixed costs. Fees, minimum withdrawals and cycle timing consume a larger share of a small payout, so the cheapest plan with the lowest fixed costs is usually the better choice.

Can I run accounts at both firms at once?

Often yes, subject to each firm's per-person cap and its rule on mirrored positions. Independent ideas across the two are the intended use; copying the same trade to both is usually restricted as a concentrated position.
