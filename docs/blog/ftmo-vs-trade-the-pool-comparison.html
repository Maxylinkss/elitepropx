FTMO vs Trade The Pool: which rulebook fits your trading

This comparison is not really between two firms. It is between two ways of being given capital: a forex and CFD evaluation measured in lots against a static percentage floor, and an equities day-trading evaluation measured in shares against a hard loss limit. The sessions, the settlement, the short-selling mechanics and the billing rhythm all follow from that difference. The figures below describe each published rulebook's shape; confirm the current plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • FTMO evaluates forex and CFD accounts; Trade The Pool evaluates equity day-trading accounts. The instruments share a screen and nothing else.
  • Equity sizing is in shares with a per-share risk calculation; forex sizing is in lots with a per-pip value.
  • An equities account carries settlement, locate and halt risk that a forex account does not.
  • FTMO bills once per attempt; Trade The Pool bills monthly, so an idle account still costs money.
  • The payout path differs in cadence: a fixed seasoning period against a profit split on a recurring cycle.

Two markets wearing the same word

"Prop firm" describes a business model, not an instrument. FTMO sells access to leveraged forex and CFD positions with a notional account value and a percentage-based loss floor. Trade The Pool sells access to an equities day-trading account with a cash value, a hard loss limit and a monthly fee. Both are funded-account businesses; the products underneath them barely overlap.

The difference shows up first in what a position is. A forex position is a contract on a currency pair, quoted in lots, with a pip value that depends on the pair and the account currency. An equity position is a number of shares in a company, with a dollar price per share and a risk per share equal to the distance to the stop. One is a derivative; the other is ownership of a security, held for minutes or hours.

The second difference is what can go wrong. A forex account is exposed to spreads, swaps and weekend gaps. An equities account is exposed to spreads, commissions, halts, locate fees, settlement and the risk that a stock simply stops trading while you are in it. Those are different failure modes, and a rulebook that does not mention them is not necessarily silent on them; it may simply assume the broker handles them.

What the capital actually is

On a forex evaluation the headline number is a notional balance. A $100,000 account does not mean $100,000 of cash is available; it means the loss floor and the target are calculated from that figure, and leverage determines how much notional exposure the trader can carry. The number is a measuring stick as much as a balance.

On an equities evaluation the headline number is closer to buying power. A $100,000 account may allow positions sized against that equity, subject to day-trading margin rules and the firm's own caps. The difference matters at the moment of a drawdown: a forex account can carry a position far larger than its balance through leverage, while an equities account is constrained by margin and by the firm's maximum position size.

For the trader, the practical question is which number determines the risk unit. On the forex side it is the floor percentage and the pip value. On the equities side it is the per-share risk and the share count, with the account equity as the denominator. Both are manageable; they are not interchangeable, and a plan built for one will misfire on the other.

A static percentage floor against a hard loss limit

FTMO's floor is a percentage of the starting balance, which produces a fixed dollar failure point. On a $100,000 account with a 10% floor, the failure point is $90,000 and it does not move. A $500 risk unit buys twenty losses of room, and a winning week does not change that. The static design is why the account can be traded with a fixed size and reviewed weekly rather than daily.

Trade The Pool's loss limit is typically expressed as a hard dollar figure on the evaluation. The floor does not trail with profit in the same way, which makes the arithmetic simpler: the remaining room is the limit minus the current loss. The trade-off is that the limit is usually tighter relative to the account size than a forex floor, because equities day trading is expected to produce smaller, more frequent gains.

The two designs reward different behavior. A static percentage floor rewards patience, because the room is constant. A hard dollar limit rewards consistency, because a single oversized loss consumes a large share of a fixed budget. If your method produces occasional outsized losses, the forex floor is more forgiving. If it produces many small losses, the dollar limit is easier to plan around.

Sizing in shares: the arithmetic

Equity risk is per share, not per lot. If a stock trades at $50 and the stop is $0.50 below entry, the risk is $0.50 per share. A $500 risk budget buys 1,000 shares, which is $50,000 of position value. That is a large position relative to the account, and it is normal in day trading because the holding period is short and the stop is tight.

The share count is then constrained by three things: the firm's maximum position size, the broker's buying power, and the liquidity of the stock. A 1,000-share order in a liquid large cap is invisible; the same order in a thin small cap can move the price against you before it fills. The rulebook may permit the size and the market may not.

Compare that with forex, where the equivalent calculation uses pip value. A 25-pip stop on a major pair at $10 per pip per lot gives $250 of risk per lot, so a $500 budget buys two lots. The position is $200,000 of notional, which is ordinary on a leveraged forex account. The two calculations look similar on paper and produce completely different exposure profiles in practice.

Settlement, margin and the T+1 rhythm

Equities settle on a one-day cycle in the US market, and day-trading margin rules apply to accounts that trade frequently within the same day. A trader who holds a position overnight moves from day-trading margin to overnight margin, which can be a different requirement. On an evaluation, the firm usually restricts or discourages overnight holding, so the settlement question is mostly about the funded stage.

Forex has no settlement in the same sense: positions roll over daily with a swap charge, and the account is marked to market continuously. The operational rhythm is different. A forex trader thinks in sessions and rollover times; an equities trader thinks in opens, closes and the next day's gap.

The rulebook consequence is that an equities evaluation is usually a day-trading product. Positions are opened and closed within the session, and holding overnight may be prohibited or may require a larger margin. A forex evaluation permits overnight and weekend holding, subject to its own rules. A trader who needs to hold positions for days should not buy a day-trading evaluation, however attractive the split.

Short selling, locates and hard-to-borrow names

Short selling an equity requires a locate: a broker must confirm that shares are available to borrow. Liquid large caps are easy to borrow; small caps and recently squeezed names may not be, and the locate can fail at exactly the moment the trade is most attractive. Some brokers charge a borrow fee that accrues while the position is open.

Forex shorting is not a borrow at all. Selling a currency pair is a symmetrical position, with no locate and no borrow fee, only the swap. That is a structural advantage for a trader whose method is short-biased, and it is rarely mentioned in comparisons because it does not appear in either rulebook.

On an equities evaluation, the locate constraint interacts with the loss limit. A failed locate means the trade is not taken, which is fine. A locate that succeeds at a high borrow rate means the position costs money while it is open, which is not visible in the entry price. If your method relies on shorting momentum names, check the broker's borrow list before the evaluation, not after.

Halts, gaps and overnight equity risk

A single stock can be halted on news, and a halted position cannot be closed. If the halt resolves against the position, the loss can exceed the stop. Circuit breakers can halt the whole market for a few minutes, which is usually benign but can trap a leveraged position at the wrong moment. These are not edge cases in small caps; they are a routine feature of the market.

Forex has its own version: the weekend gap. A stop placed inside the gap fills at the first available price, which can be far from the level. Holiday liquidity can produce similar gaps intraweek. The difference is frequency and predictability. Equity halts are event-driven and often announced; forex gaps are scheduled by the calendar and easy to avoid by flattening.

The risk-management response is the same in both cases: never let a position be larger than the loss you are prepared to take beyond the stop. If a halt could double the loss, size for double. That rule is unpleasant and it is the only one that survives a gap.

The equity session is three sessions

The US equity day has a pre-market, a regular session and an after-hours session. Liquidity and spreads are dramatically different in each. The open is the most liquid and the most volatile; the last hour is liquid and directional; the middle of the day is thin and mean-reverting. A day-trading method is usually built for one of those windows, and applying it to another is a different strategy.

Forex has a similar structure across time zones: Asia, London, the London-New York overlap and the New York afternoon. The overlap is the deepest and the afternoon is the thinnest. A trader who can only watch one window is effectively running a session-specific strategy, whether or not they describe it that way.

For the comparison, the question is which session you can actually attend. An equities evaluation requires presence during US hours, usually at the open. A forex evaluation can be traded from Asia, London or New York. If your available hours are outside the US session, the equities product is the wrong purchase regardless of its terms.

Sterling and DAS against MT4 and MT5

Trade The Pool's stack is built around professional equity day-trading front ends such as Sterling Trader and DAS Trader, with TradingView for charting in some setups. FTMO's stack is MT4, MT5, cTrader and DXtrade. The two lists reflect the markets: equity platforms optimise for Level 2 data, hotkeys, order routing and fast execution across thousands of symbols; forex platforms optimise for currency pairs, expert advisors and server-side stops.

A trader moving from one to the other faces a real relearning cost. Hotkey layouts, order types, the DOM and the way a stop is attached all differ. That cost is not in the fee comparison, and it is usually larger than the fee difference. Test the platform on a demo for a week before committing to the evaluation.

Automation is a further difference. MT4 and MT5 support expert advisors, and cTrader supports cBots. Equity platforms support their own scripting and API access, but the ecosystem is different and often broker-specific. A trader with a tested forex automation cannot assume it transfers, and a trader with an equity automation should confirm the firm permits it.

One-off against monthly: the idle-account cost

FTMO charges once per attempt on the plans compared here. The fee is paid at purchase and does not recur while the account is idle or while the trader is between attempts. That structure favours a trader who needs time: a slow month costs nothing extra.

Trade The Pool charges a monthly subscription. The fee recurs for as long as the account is open, whether or not the trader is active. A three-month attempt costs three times the monthly fee, and a trader who takes a break still pays. That structure favours a trader who works intensively and finishes quickly.

The comparison is therefore a bet on your own pace. If you expect to pass in six weeks, the monthly product can be cheaper. If you expect to take three months, the one-off product usually wins. Work out your realistic timeline before comparing the headline prices, because the headline price is a monthly rate on one side and a total on the other.

How the profit is released

FTMO releases the first payout after a fixed seasoning period and subsequent withdrawals on a shorter schedule. Trade The Pool pays a profit split on a recurring cycle. In both cases the split percentage is the number traders quote and the cycle is the number that matters, because a high split paid slowly is worth less than a lower split paid quickly.

Both firms review conduct before releasing profit. For an equities account the review may include checks on pattern-day-trading compliance, prohibited strategies such as latency arbitrage, and the use of third-party signal services. For a forex account the review focuses on prohibited practices such as copying between accounts and trading restricted news windows.

The verification step is the same on both sides: identity documents, address proof and a payment method that matches the account holder. Complete verification before the first profitable month. A payout request that triggers verification for the first time is a payout request plus a delay.

Risk of ruin on each structure

The floor does not just limit losses; it sets the probability that a losing streak ends the account. Take a trader with a 50% win rate, a 1:1 payoff and a fixed risk unit. The chance of ten consecutive losses in a hundred trades is high enough to matter, and ten losses is the entire room on a ten-loss floor. That is the arithmetic behind the warning that a challenge is a constraint test rather than a skill test.

On a static forex floor with twenty losses of room, the same streak is survivable. The trader loses a fifth of the cushion and keeps trading. On a hard dollar limit with six losses of room, the streak ends the attempt. The probability of the streak does not change with the firm; only the number of attempts the account gets does.

The way to reduce the risk is not to find better trades. It is to reduce the risk unit so the floor buys more losses. Halving the unit doubles the number of losses the floor absorbs and halves the profit per winning trade. The target takes longer, and the probability of reaching it without a breach rises. That trade-off is the entire game, and it is the same on both sides of this comparison.

The second lever is the stop distance. A tighter stop reduces the risk per share or per lot, which allows a larger position for the same risk budget. It also increases the chance of being stopped out by noise. A trader who tightens stops to fit a floor usually lowers the win rate by more than the extra room is worth. The correct adjustment is size, not stop placement.

A sixty-day attempt on each side

On the forex account, sixty days is two months of sessions with a fixed risk unit. A trader taking two setups a day at $500 of risk, with a 45% win rate and a 2:1 average, produces a small positive expectancy and a wide dispersion. The static floor gives the strategy room to survive a ten-loss streak, which is the test that matters. The pace is set by the target, and the target is usually reachable inside the window without forcing size.

On the equities account, sixty days is sixty sessions of day trading with a hard dollar limit. The same trader taking two setups a day at $50 of risk per trade, with a 50% win rate and a 1.5:1 average, produces a similar expectancy with a tighter dispersion. The limit is consumed faster by a bad week, and the monthly fee keeps accruing while the account is under water.

The comparison of the two attempts is not which produced more profit. It is which one the trader could execute without changing behavior. A method that needs a wide stop fits the forex floor; a method built on tight stops and many trades fits the equities limit. The rulebook should follow the method, not the other way round.

The fee comparison, done properly

Comparing a one-off fee with a monthly subscription on the sticker price is a category error. The one-off fee is a total cost for one attempt; the monthly fee is a rate. To compare them, multiply the monthly rate by the number of months the attempt is expected to take, then add the cost of any month the trader is between attempts but still subscribed.

That calculation usually narrows the gap. A monthly product that looks cheap at one month can be double the one-off price at three. A one-off product that looks expensive can be the cheaper option for a trader who needs six months of patience. The variable is not the firm; it is the trader's pace.

There is a second cost that neither page shows: the platform. Equity front ends sometimes carry a data or software fee, and forex platforms are usually included but may charge for extra tools. Add those to the monthly side, because they recur with the subscription rather than with the attempt.

Finally, multiply by the expected number of attempts. A trader who needs two attempts on a one-off product pays twice; a trader who needs two attempts on a monthly product pays for every month of both. The expected number of attempts is the single most important input, and it is the one most traders guess rather than estimate.

Which trader should choose which

FTMO fits a forex trader with an existing MT4 or MT5 workflow, a method that can tolerate overnight and weekend holding, and a preference for a fixed failure point. The static floor and the platform stack are the decisive features, and the one-off fee suits a trader who expects to take months rather than weeks.

Trade The Pool fits an equities day trader who works the US session, is comfortable with a professional front end, and can finish an attempt inside a few months. The monthly fee is a fair trade for a faster path if the trader is genuinely full-time. It is a bad trade for a part-time trader who will pay for months of inactivity.

A trader who has never traded either market should not choose on price. The correct first step is a demo month in the market you intend to trade, measured against the rulebook you intend to buy. If the demo month would have breached the floor, the purchase is premature.

What to verify before you pay

Confirm the floor type in writing: percentage or dollar, static or trailing, measured on balance or equity. Confirm the daily loss limit and its basis. Confirm the maximum position size in shares or lots, because a limit that is below your normal size changes the whole plan. Confirm whether overnight and weekend holding is permitted, and on what terms.

Confirm the billing rhythm and the cancellation conditions. A monthly product usually requires notice to stop; a one-off product does not. Confirm the payout cycle, the split and the conditions that can delay a release. Then confirm the platform: which front end you will actually use, whether it costs extra, and whether your charting setup is supported.

Save a dated copy of the terms. Firms update plan documents, and the version in force at payout may differ from the version at checkout. The copy is not paranoia; it is the only evidence you will have if the two versions disagree.

Common questions

Can I trade forex on Trade The Pool?

No. Trade The Pool evaluates equities day-trading accounts. FTMO evaluates forex and CFD accounts, which is why the platform lists and the session hours are so different.

Is the FTMO floor static?

FTMO measures its maximum loss from the starting balance, so the dollar failure point stays fixed for the attempt. A winning week adds cushion above the floor rather than moving it.

Why does the monthly fee matter so much?

Because it accrues whether or not you trade. A three-month attempt on a monthly plan costs three times the monthly fee; a one-off plan costs the same whether it takes six weeks or six months.

Can I hold equities overnight on an evaluation?

Often not, or only on restricted terms. Equity evaluations are usually day-trading products, and overnight holding may require different margin. Confirm before relying on it.

Which account is easier to pass?

Neither is easier in the abstract. A static percentage floor suits a method with deep retracements; a hard dollar limit suits a method with many small losses. The easier account is the one that matches the method you already trade.
