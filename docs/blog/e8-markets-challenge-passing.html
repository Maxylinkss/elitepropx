How to pass an E8 Markets challenge: the rulebook, the math and the plan

E8 Markets sells both one-step and two-phase forex evaluations, most of them on a static floor, with a percentage daily stop, a one-off fee and a choice of MT4, MT5, cTrader or Match-Trader. The first decision is not the target or the fee — it is which structure fits the method, because a one-step plan and a two-phase plan fail in different ways. This guide works through both with illustrative numbers. Figures are models rather than quotations; confirm the live plan schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • One-step and two-phase plans are different products: one tests a single sample, the other tests repetition.
  • Most plans use a static floor, which keeps a constant risk unit valid for the whole path.
  • The daily stop is percentage based and usually the rule that ends a bad week first.
  • Match-Trader sits alongside MT4, MT5 and cTrader and changes the workflow more than the fee does.
  • One-off billing removes the calendar penalty, so a measured attempt costs no more than a fast one.
  • Consistency is usually absent from the evaluation and may appear on the funded account — check both.

The E8 Markets rulebook in one page

The published shape is forex and CFD, one-step and two-phase evaluations, a static floor on most plans, a percentage daily stop, no consistency condition on most evaluations, an undefined minimum-day count, one-off per attempt billing, a profit split on a fixed schedule, and MT4, MT5, cTrader or Match-Trader on weekday forex hours. The word that does the most work in that list is "most": the plan name, not the brand, carries the rulebook.

That matters because the same firm can offer a one-step plan with a larger target and a two-phase plan with two smaller ones, and the two are not interchangeable. A one-step plan asks whether a method can produce a result once; a two-phase plan asks whether it can do so twice. A trader with a volatile method may pass the first and fail the second, and a trader with a slow, steady method may pass both at the same size.

The static floor is the second important row. It means the failure point is fixed in dollars from the first trade to the last, so the risk unit does not need to be recalculated as the account earns. That simplifies the plan and it removes the discipline a trailing floor imposes, which is why a self-imposed drawdown limit matters more here than it would elsewhere.

Those four answers decide more than the fee, and none of them is reliably visible on a comparison page.

One-step against two-phase: what actually differs

A one-step evaluation is one target against one floor inside one calendar. A two-phase evaluation is two targets against the same floor, usually with the second target measured from the profit the first one produced. The fee is one-off in both cases, so the difference is not cost; it is the number of samples the firm requires.

The practical difference is where a method fails. A one-step plan punishes a method that cannot produce a result in a single window; a two-phase plan punishes a method that cannot repeat it. A trend-following method with long dry spells often struggles with the first, because the window may not contain a trend. A mean-reversion method with occasional large losses often struggles with the second, because the good phase-one result is followed by a phase-two drawdown.

The second difference is the size of the target relative to the floor. A one-step target is usually larger than either individual two-phase target, because there is only one window to reach it. That larger target against the same floor means a higher risk per unit of target, which is the real cost of the shorter path. The shorter path is cheaper in calendar and more expensive in risk.

The third difference is the diagnostic value. A two-phase result tells the trader something a one-step result cannot: whether the method repeats. For a trader who intends to run a funded account for years, that information is worth the extra time. For a trader who intends to withdraw once, the extra phase is pure cost.

Static floor arithmetic

Assume a $100,000 account with a 10% static floor and an 8% one-step target. The failure point is $90,000 for the entire attempt, and the target is $108,000. Room is $10,000; target is $8,000. The ratio of target to floor is 0.8, which is a demanding structure: the account has to earn most of its own risk budget before it passes.

Compare that with a two-phase plan at 6% and 6% on the same floor. Each phase asks for $6,000 against $10,000 of room, and the second phase starts from a higher balance with the same floor. The ratio per phase is 0.6, which is easier on both counts. The two-phase plan takes longer and is more forgiving per step, and that difference is the whole argument for choosing it.

The static floor also means the room grows in relative terms as the account earns. After phase one at $106,000, the same $10,000 floor measured from $100,000 leaves $16,000 of room from the current balance. A trader who keeps the original unit is taking less relative risk, which is a rare case where doing nothing is the correct adjustment.

The counter-argument is behavioural. A floor that never moves allows a trader to keep a size that is too large for a bad week, because the account still has room. Set a self-imposed stop at one third of the floor from the equity high, and the static structure gets the discipline it does not provide on its own.

Planning a one-step attempt

An 8% target on $100,000 is $8,000. With a $400 unit and a 2:1 payoff, a winner returns $800, so the target is ten net winners. At two trades per session and a 45% hit rate, ten net winners takes roughly forty trades, or twenty sessions — four weeks. That is a reasonable window for a method with a real edge, and a tight one for a method that needs a trend to appear.

The drawdown budget for a one-step attempt should be half the floor, not all of it. A trader who uses $8,000 of a $10,000 floor to reach an $8,000 target has taken on twice the risk the target implies, and the expected number of attempts rises accordingly. Half the floor keeps the risk per dollar of target at roughly the same level as a two-phase plan.

The unit should be held constant. A one-step plan rewards a steady sample, and changing size mid-attempt destroys the only evidence the result provides. If the method cannot reach the target at a constant $400 unit in twenty sessions, the answer is a longer window or a smaller target, not a larger unit.

The close of a one-step attempt is compressed because there is no phase boundary. Reduce to two thirds of the unit at seventy percent of the target, and to a third at ninety percent. The final sessions are for protecting the result and preparing documentation, not for pressing toward a number that is already within reach.

Planning a two-phase attempt

Two phases at 6% each on $100,000 are $6,000 per phase. With the same $400 unit, each phase is about eight net winners, or fifteen sessions at two trades a session. The full path is therefore eight to ten weeks, which is longer than the one-step calendar and easier on the risk per phase.

The phase boundary is the critical point. A trader who finishes phase one at $106,000 and immediately begins phase two at full size has skipped the only calibration the second phase gets. Two weeks at half the unit confirms that the method still behaves, and it produces a comparison between the phases that is useful for the review.

The phase-two target is usually measured from the new balance, so $6,000 means $6,000 from $106,000, not $6,000 from $100,000. That is a smaller percentage and the same dollar figure, which is a small structural advantage. Do not spend it by increasing the unit; spend it by finishing the phase with more room intact.

The second phase is also where the funded account's rules start to matter. If the funded phase applies a consistency or minimum-day condition, phase two is the best place to rehearse it: a daily profit cap and a fixed number of sessions per week produce a distribution that already looks like the funded record.

Lot sizing and pip value

Forex sizing is pips × pip value × lots. On a standard lot of a USD-quoted major, one pip is about $10, so a 20-pip stop risks $200 per lot and a $400 unit buys two lots. A 40-pip stop buys one. The position count is a consequence of the stop distance, not a decision in itself, which is why the stop should be placed by the method rather than adjusted to fit a desired size.

Pip value varies by pair and by account currency. USD/JPY depends on the exchange rate, crosses run through two conversions, and metals and indices do not use pips in the same way at all. A trader who applies $10 per pip universally will mis-size the non-USD-quoted instruments, and the error is largest when volatility is highest.

The buffer is the difference between a planned unit and a realised one. Spreads widen around the London open and around releases, and stops slip. Adding ten to twenty percent to the planned risk changes the lot count by a fraction and keeps the realised loss inside the intended unit. Without the buffer, a slipped stop consumes one and a half units and the daily cap is reached sooner than planned.

Leverage is the last check and rarely the binding one. A correctly sized two-lot position on a $100,000 account uses a small fraction of available margin, so leverage never constrains it. The only way leverage binds is when the size was wrong to begin with, which is an argument for sizing from risk rather than from margin.

The daily stop as a session budget

A percentage daily stop becomes a dollar figure and then a number of losses. On $100,000 at 5%, the limit is $5,000; with a $400 unit that is twelve losses, which no session should use. Set a personal cap at three losses, $1,200, and the firm's rule becomes a backstop rather than a plan.

The basis decides how the limit behaves. Measured on equity, floating losses count immediately, so an open position $3,000 underwater has used 60% of the allowance before it is closed. Measured on realised results, only closed trades count, which permits a larger floating loss and a different behaviour at the end of the session. Confirm which applies; the difference is thousands of dollars on one trade.

When the clock resets is the last part of the definition, and it is not the same as the session hours. A trade opened late in one session and closed after the roll may land in either day's tally, which matters for a method that holds positions past the New York close. If the document does not say, flatten before the roll rather than arguing about attribution afterwards.

The daily stop should also be paired with a weekly cap. Four sessions at the personal limit is $4,800, nearly half a 10% floor. A weekly cap of one third of the floor stops the accumulation before it becomes structural, and it is the only rule in the system that nobody else enforces.

Consistency, present and absent

The evaluation usually has no consistency condition, which means the profit can arrive in any shape. That is a freedom with a delayed cost: the funded account may apply a condition the evaluation did not, and a pass built on two oversized days can convert into a payout that does not clear.

The test to run before choosing a plan is the share of profit from the best day. Take sixty trading days, total the profit, and compute that share. If it is above a quarter, the distribution is lumpy and a funded-phase condition will bind. If it is below, the shape is already suitable and the consistency question can be set aside.

The design response for a lumpy method is a daily profit cap of three units and a trade count large enough to dilute the best session. That flattens the distribution without reducing the total, and it costs nothing in expectation. The second response is to choose the two-phase plan rather than the one-step one, because two smaller windows are easier to distribute than one large one.

Confirm whether the condition applies to the evaluation, the funded phase, or only the payout request, and whether it is measured on gross or net profit. Ask in writing, keep the reply, and put it in the same document as the terms screenshot. That document is the only version of the rules you can rely on six weeks later.

MT4, MT5, cTrader and Match-Trader

Four platforms is a wider choice than most firms offer, and the choice matters more than the fee. MT4 is the familiar hedging platform with the largest library of expert advisors. MT5 adds a modern backtester and more instruments but defaults to netting, which changes how multiple entries on one symbol are managed.

cTrader is the level-based option, with a depth-of-market view and order handling built around precise entries and exits. It suits a trader who manages positions at levels rather than at tickets, and its interface is cleaner than MT4's for that style. Match-Trader is the newest of the four and the one most firms use for a modern web and mobile experience; it is fully featured, browser-first, and a reasonable default for a trader without a legacy workflow.

The migration cost is measured in mistakes rather than fees. A trader with years of MT4 templates and a tested expert advisor faces a real cost in moving, and that cost usually exceeds any difference in spreads. A discretionary trader with no automation can choose on interface preference, which is a legitimate criterion.

Automation raises a rulebook question separate from the platform. Some plans restrict high-frequency activity, latency strategies or certain order types, and an expert advisor can breach those clauses without intent. Read the activity clauses, test on a demo under the same rules, and keep the logs. A compliant strategy without logs cannot prove its compliance.

Session and pair selection

Forex liquidity is concentrated in London and New York, and the overlap between them is the deepest window of the day. A method should be built for one window rather than for the whole day, because a London breakout and a New York reversal have different stop distributions, trade frequencies and daily loss profiles.

The pair carries as much information as the hour. EUR/USD and GBP/USD are liquid in London; USD/JPY is active in Tokyo and New York; AUD and NZD pairs move in the Asian session. A method that works on EUR/USD at 09:00 does not transfer to AUD/USD at 02:00 without re-measuring the spread and the stop distribution.

The daily stop interacts with the session because volatile sessions consume it faster. A trader whose method trades the London open will reach the personal cap sooner than one who trades the quiet afternoon, with the same unit. That is a reason to size the session against the cap, not a reason to avoid volatility.

Holding overnight adds rollover and gap risk, and the weekend adds a second gap. A position held across a session boundary should carry a smaller unit than a session trade, or be flattened. Write the choice down before the week starts, because it will otherwise be re-decided at 21:55 on a Friday.

A ten-week one-step calendar

Weeks one and two are measurement: half the unit, a full record of setups, and a note of spread and slip on every entry. The output is a table of how the method behaves under the account's conditions, including which sessions and pairs are expensive. No target work happens here.

Weeks three to seven are the working block: full unit, a fixed setup list, a personal daily cap of three losses, and a weekly cap of one third of the floor. The target should be within reach by week seven. If it is not, reduce the unit or extend the calendar rather than increasing activity, because activity does not create an edge.

Weeks eight and nine are the distribution block: two thirds of the unit, a daily profit cap, and a deliberate effort to spread the remaining target across sessions. If the funded phase applies a consistency condition, this is where the record starts to look like the funded record.

Week ten is the close: a third of the unit, the target protected, and the payout documentation prepared. Ten weeks is a realistic one-step calendar on a one-off fee, and it costs no more than a four-week sprint that fails.

Risk of ruin and the longest losing run

Assume a 45% hit rate and a 2:1 payoff. Expectancy is 0.45 × 2R − 0.55 × 1R, or 0.35R per trade, which is healthy. The probability of six consecutive losses is 0.55 to the sixth, about 2.8%. Across fifty trades the chance of seeing at least one such run is high, so a plan that cannot survive six losses fails in most attempts rather than in unlucky ones.

Size so that the longest run from your own record, plus two, consumes less than half the floor. On a $10,000 floor that is an $800 unit for a six-loss tolerance, or $670 for a seven-loss tolerance. A $400 unit is more conservative and produces a longer calendar, which is a fair trade on a one-off fee.

The daily stop changes the shape of the calculation by interrupting runs. A cap at three losses converts a long streak into a sequence of short ones across sessions, and the account continues with the floor intact. That is a genuine benefit of a daily rule on a static product, and it is the reason the personal cap matters more than the firm's generous limit.

The counter-argument is that interrupting a streak also interrupts recovery. A trader stopped for the day cannot make the loss back in the same session, which is exactly the behaviour the rule exists to prevent. The correct response to a stopped session is a review, not a second session on another pair.

Releases, spreads and restricted windows

Forex news risk is concentrated in central bank decisions, inflation prints and employment data, and the reaction can be tens of pips in seconds. Spreads widen before the release, liquidity thins, and the stop that was 20 pips away at 13:55 is not the stop that fills at 14:30. A position held through a release has an unknown risk distance.

Restrictions vary by plan. Some prohibit opening positions around a release, some prohibit holding through it, and some prohibit profiting from the resulting spike. The last is the most ambiguous and the most likely to be applied at review, because it can be judged on the trade or on the account.

The conservative plan is to flatten before the release and re-enter after the first hour. That costs some of the largest moves and removes a category of dispute. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the floor can survive if it gaps, and should accept that the daily cap may be consumed by a single fill.

Headlines arrive without a calendar entry and no rule prevents them. What prevents damage is the position: a unit small enough that an unfilled stop is a bad day rather than a dead account. That is the streak argument again, applied to a single event that gave no notice.

From pass to payout

The split and the cycle are the two numbers that decide what a pass is worth. A 90% split on a monthly cycle and an 80% split on a fortnightly cycle are different products, and the right choice depends on whether the profit is income or reinvestment. Compare the total time from first payment to cleared funds rather than the headline percentage.

The funded review examines the record: size relative to the rules, distribution of profit, number of sessions and any pattern resembling prohibited practice. A complete journal answers the questions in an afternoon; a statement without one invites interpretation, and interpretation by a third party rarely favours the trader.

Identity and payment checks are the last gate and the most avoidable delay. Funds typically go back to the method used for the fee, so a replaced card or a closed wallet forces a manual review. Finish the documents while the target is still being worked, not after it is met, and store the fee receipt next to them.

Confirm whether the funded account inherits the evaluation's rulebook or replaces it, and what the split is measured on. Those two answers decide the real value of a pass, and neither is usually on the pricing page.

The cost of choosing the wrong structure

The fee is the same for both structures on most plans, so the cost of choosing wrongly is not the fee. It is the expected number of attempts. A trader whose method cannot repeat will fail a two-phase plan repeatedly and pass a one-step plan sometimes; a trader whose method needs a long window will fail a one-step plan and pass a two-phase one. The wrong structure multiplies the fee by the gap in pass probability.

Model it directly. If a one-step pass probability is 30% and a two-phase path probability is 20%, the expected cost is the fee divided by the probability: $450 divided by 0.30 is $1,500, and $450 divided by 0.20 is $2,250. That difference is larger than any fee discount and it is decided by the method, not by the price.

The lever that improves both numbers is a smaller unit. Reducing the unit by a third typically raises the pass probability because it converts a normal losing streak from a breach into a drawdown. The fee stays the same, so the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade. That is the most reliable improvement available to a trader who has already chosen the structure.

The second lever is the calendar. On a one-off fee, a longer attempt costs nothing and raises the probability of encountering a normal distribution of results, which is what both structures are testing. Patience is cheap here in a way it is not on a subscription, and it is the most underused lever in the product.

Which plan fits which trader

The one-step plan fits a trader whose method produces results in short windows and who wants the shortest path from fee to funded account. It suits a high-frequency discretionary method with a steady hit rate, and it suits a trader who has already passed a similar evaluation and knows the size works.

The two-phase plan fits a trader whose method is slower, whose drawdowns are deeper, or who wants the diagnostic value of a repeat. It also fits a trader whose first funded payout will depend on a consistency condition, because two windows are easier to distribute than one. The longer calendar is the price and a one-off fee makes it affordable.

Whichever is chosen, the same process applies: size from the floor, cap the day and the week, build the target in small blocks, and treat the phase boundary or the final week as a checkpoint rather than a celebration. The structure decides how many times that process is tested, not whether it works.

If the choice is genuinely unclear, run the simulation twice: once on your last fifty trades against the one-step target, and once against the two-phase targets. Whichever survives with the smaller drawdown is the structure your method already fits, which is a better answer than any comparison table.

Common questions

Does E8 Markets offer one-step and two-phase evaluations?

Yes. Both structures are described on the product page, and they fail in different ways. A one-step plan tests a single sample; a two-phase plan tests whether the result repeats.

Is the loss floor static or trailing?

Static on most plans, measured from the initial balance. Confirm the floor type for the exact plan name, because the brand carries several products and the plan carries the rulebook.

Which platform should I choose?

Keep the platform you already trade well. MT4 suits a ticket-based workflow with existing scripts, MT5 adds a modern backtester but defaults to netting, cTrader suits level-based execution, and Match-Trader is a solid browser-first default.

How much can I risk per trade on $100,000?

Size so that your longest losing run plus two consumes less than half the floor. On an illustrative $10,000 floor that is roughly $670 to $800 per trade, which is well above the minimum position size on most pairs.

Does the evaluation have a consistency rule?

Usually not, but the funded account may apply one. Confirm both stages in writing, and check whether the condition is measured on gross or net profit and whether it applies at payout only.

How long does a one-step attempt take?

A ten-week calendar with two measurement weeks, a five-week working block, two distribution weeks and a close is realistic at a constant unit. A shorter calendar is possible at a larger unit and a higher risk of a retry.
