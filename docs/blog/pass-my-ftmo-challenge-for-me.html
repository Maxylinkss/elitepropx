Pass My FTMO Challenge for Me

FTMO is the firm traders ask about first, and it is also the one where the arrangement deserves the most careful reading. FTMO runs a two-phase evaluation with a daily loss rule as well as an overall loss limit, and its terms take a clear position on who may trade an account and under what conditions. This page covers what the arrangement actually involves, how the two-phase structure changes the plan, how the daily loss rule is calculated, what the funded phase demands, and the checks to complete before any money changes hands.

Key takeaways

  • FTMO evaluates in two phases — a Challenge and a Verification — each with its own profit target and minimum trading-day requirement.
  • The binding constraint is usually the daily loss rule, not the overall loss limit. An account can be closed by two bad sessions with the overall floor intact.
  • The terms position on third-party or assisted trading decides whether the arrangement exists at all. Confirm the specific clause in writing before paying.
  • A service should return the funded account in your name, on your email, with your payout details and no profit split.
  • Passing is not the end. FTMO's funded phase has its own payout cycle and requirements, and they are not the same as the evaluation's.
  • Confirm the fee, the retry policy, the terms check and the timeline in writing. A service that cannot answer all four is not ready to be hired.
  • If you have a tested method and the hours available, self-trading is usually the cheaper route to the same funded account.

What the arrangement actually is

Having someone pass an FTMO challenge for you means a third party trades the evaluation on your account and returns a funded account in your name. Two things are being combined: access to your account, and responsibility for the trading decisions. Both are governed by FTMO's terms rather than by the service's marketing, which is why the terms question comes before the price question on this particular firm.

The shape of the deal is simple enough. You buy the evaluation in your own name. The service reads the current rulebook, builds a risk plan, trades the account to the target, and reports progress. If the target is reached with the loss limits intact and the minimum trading days satisfied, the account moves to the funded stage, and the service's involvement ends.

What is not being sold matters just as much. A passing service does not guarantee a pass, does not manage the funded account afterwards, and does not take a share of your profit. Any provider offering a guarantee has mispriced its own risk, because it does not control the market; any provider asking for a profit share has converted the arrangement into something closer to a managed account, with different risks and different questions about who is responsible for the trading.

FTMO's two-phase structure

FTMO evaluates in two stages, and both must be completed before a funded account is issued. The exact figures and rules change from time to time, so treat the table below as the shape of the programme rather than a substitute for the current rulebook, and confirm every number on FTMO's own site before you buy anything.

StageWhat it testsTypical published shapeWhat it means in practice
ChallengeWhether you can hit a target while respecting daily and overall loss limitsA profit target of roughly 10% of the initial balance, with loss limits below itThe longest phase, and the one where pace and risk sizing are set
VerificationWhether the first phase was repeatableA smaller target, often around 5%, with the same loss limitsA second, shorter test that punishes traders who get reckless after passing phase one
Funded accountWhether you can keep the rules under real payout conditionsProfit split to the trader, payout cycle, funded-phase requirementsA separate rulebook, and the stage that actually produces income

Two features of this structure matter more than the headline percentages. The first is the minimum trading-day requirement in each phase: the account must be traded across a minimum number of days regardless of how quickly the target is reached. That rules out completing the evaluation in a single good session, and it means the plan has to be spread deliberately rather than executed as fast as possible.

The second is the combination of a daily loss limit and an overall loss limit. Most firms enforce something similar, but the pairing is what defines FTMO's risk profile, and it is the single most common reason evaluations fail. The next section deals with it in detail.

Verify the numbers on the day you buy. Prop firm rules are revised between plan generations. The figures in this article describe the structure at the time of writing; the rulebook attached to the specific plan in your cart is the one that will govern the attempt.

Why the daily loss rule decides most attempts

A daily loss limit caps how much the account may lose in a single trading day, independently of the total floor. It sounds like a secondary rule and behaves like the primary one, because it can end an attempt while the overall loss budget is almost entirely intact.

Consider an illustrative $100,000 evaluation with an overall loss limit of 10% and a daily limit of 5%. The overall budget is $10,000. The daily cap is $5,000. A trader who loses $5,000 in one session has breached the daily rule and ended the attempt, even though $5,000 of the overall budget remains untouched. Two bad days, in the wrong order, are enough to close the account.

Risk per tradeDollar risk on $100,000Losses to breach the daily ruleShare of the overall budget used per session
1.0%$1,000510%
0.75%$7506–77.5%
0.5%$500105%
0.25%$250202.5%

The practical implication is a hard daily stop expressed in units of risk rather than in dollars. If the plan risks 1% per trade and the daily limit is 5%, then the trading day is over after five losses — and a sensible plan ends it after three, because the fourth and fifth are the ones taken under pressure. The stop belongs to the plan, not to the trader's judgement in the moment, which is precisely the part of the process a service is meant to handle.

There is a second subtlety in how the daily figure is calculated. Balance-based and equity-based definitions produce different results on the same day: a losing position that is open but not closed contributes to an equity calculation and not to a balance one. Confirm which basis applies to your plan, and whether the day resets at midnight server time or at a broker-specific rollover. An account that breaches at 23:58 server time because of an open position is a failure that had nothing to do with the trading idea behind it.

Budget the day before you trade it. Divide the daily limit by the account balance to get the session loss budget in units of risk. If the number is five and your plan allows three, you have two units of cushion for slippage, widened spreads around news, and the ordinary bad luck of a stop being hit by a wick.

How the two phases change the plan

A two-phase evaluation is not one long test with a break in the middle. The two phases have different targets, and the transition between them is where plans are most often lost.

AspectChallenge phaseVerification phase
TargetThe larger of the two targetsRoughly half the first target on a typical plan
Risk per tradeSet by the daily limit, not by the targetUnchanged — the limits usually stay the same
PaceSpread across the minimum trading daysSame discipline, shorter distance
Common mistakeRushing to the target and eating into the daily budgetRelaxing because the target is smaller, then giving back the cushion
What carries overNothing — each phase starts from its own balanceProof that the first result was repeatable

The verification phase is where discipline tends to slip. The target is smaller, the account already has a successful phase behind it, and the temptation is to treat the second stage as a formality. In practice the loss limits are unchanged, so the same daily discipline is required for half the reward — and a plan that traded the first phase at the edge of its daily budget has no margin left in the second.

The consequence for planning is that risk per trade should be set for the tighter of the two phases and held constant across both. A trader who sizes for the first target and then discovers the second phase demands the same limits for half the profit has usually already paid for the lesson with a failed account.

The terms position, and why it comes first

This is the threshold question, and it is not a formality. FTMO's terms address who may access an account and what counts as acceptable trading on it, and firms in this market are generally explicit about account access and control because it is central to how the evaluation is meant to work. The consequence of an arrangement that falls outside those terms is not a warning: it is a closed account and a refused payout, and enforcement can arrive after a funded account has been running for months.

Position in the termsWhat it means for the service routeWhat to do
ProhibitedThe route is unavailable at any priceTrade the evaluation yourself, or choose a different firm
Restricted by conditionsPermitted only under stated conditions, such as who holds the login or what counts as assistanceCheck whether the service's process satisfies every condition
PermittedThe route is available and the remaining questions are cost and scopeProceed, with the terms confirmation saved

The risk here belongs to the account holder, not to the provider. No service can transfer it, remove it or indemnify you against it, and any provider claiming otherwise is describing a guarantee it is not in a position to make. That is why the terms check has to precede the payment, and why it should be written down and dated: a note naming the clause you relied on is the only protection available if the firm's position is later interpreted differently.

There is also a practical dimension beyond the rules. Because the account is opened in your name and your identity documents are attached to it, you are the party the firm will contact and the party whose payout would be withheld. A service that treats the terms check as a formality has not understood which of you carries the consequence.

The process a legitimate service follows

The service reads the current rulebook, builds a risk plan against the overall floor and the daily stop, trades both phases to their targets, satisfies the minimum trading-day requirements, and reports progress along the way. It should not take a share of the funded profit, and the account should remain in your name throughout.

A flat fee with retries covered is easier to audit than a profit split, because the total is known before you pay. Ask what happens after a failure, and expect a specific answer: whether the retry is covered, discounted or charged in full, whether the firm's own reset fee is included, and over what period the cover applies. Ask for the refund position in writing at the same time. A service confident in its process will put the policy on paper; a service that will not is telling you what the policy is worth.

Reporting is the part that is easy to undervalue. During the evaluation the account is in your name, so any breach is yours to answer for. Updates that show progress against the target and the distance to both loss limits let you see the attempt is on track without monitoring it personally. Silence until the outcome is not reporting, and it removes the only opportunity you have to ask questions while the account is still alive.

After the pass: the funded phase

A funded FTMO account is not the evaluation account with a new label. The requirements change, and the ones that matter most are administrative rather than tactical.

Funded-phase elementWhy it mattersWhat to confirm
Payout cycleSets how soon the first withdrawal is possible and how often after thatThe first-payout waiting period for your specific account type
Profit splitDetermines what you actually receive from each payoutThe percentage applied to your account, and whether it can increase
Funded loss limitsThe daily and overall rules still apply after fundingWhether they are the same as the evaluation's or reset to the funded balance
Holding rulesWeekend and news positions may be treated differently by account typeWhether your account is a swing type and what that permits
Payout processingAffects the net amount that reaches youThe method, the fees and the currency conversion

The handover itself has five parts, and they should happen together. Access held by the service ends and credentials are rotated. The funded login reaches you and works without the provider's involvement. Payout details are configured by you rather than by the provider. The funded terms are read on the day of the handover rather than on the day of the first withdrawal. And the evidence set — firm confirmation, written scope, terms check and pass result — is filed somewhere you can find it.

Reading the funded terms on the handover day is the single most valuable habit in this list. Evaluation rules and funded rules are not the same document, and the differences surface at exactly the moment money is involved.

Cost, compared with self-trading

On a two-phase structure, the cost comparison changes because there are more places to fail. Each phase is a separate window with its own loss limits, so the probability of completing both is lower than the probability of completing either one, and that difference is what the flat fee is priced against.

FactorSelf-tradedFlat-fee service
Cash costEvaluation fee per attempt$220 per evaluation
Attempts to budget forOne divided by your completion probabilityOne, where retries are covered
TimeYour sessions across both phasesTypically around two weeks
Skill builtYes, directly, on the rulebook you will tradeNo
Daily-rule disciplineYours to holdHandled by the plan
Funded accountYoursYours, no profit split

Two rows deserve comment. The attempts row is where the arithmetic lands: if your completion probability across both phases is 30%, you should budget for roughly three attempts, and the expected cost of the evaluation fees alone approaches or exceeds a flat service fee before any reset fees are counted. The skill row is the counter-argument, and it is real: a self-traded two-phase evaluation teaches the exact rulebook you will then trade funded, and a funded account is usually lost to the daily rule rather than to a lack of ideas.

The reasonable conclusion is situational. A trader whose constraint is hours and who has already demonstrated they can trade should price the service against their own expected attempts. A trader whose constraint is skill should spend the fee on screen time instead, because the funded account is where the returns are and it is protected by the same rules the evaluation tests.

A worked two-phase timeline

Minimum trading days turn both phases into calendar events rather than sprints. The schedule below is illustrative, assumes a four-day minimum per phase, and shows why the plan has to be spread rather than executed quickly.

1ChallengeRead the rulebook again, set the risk unit, trade normallyDaily loss limit
2ChallengeSecond counted day, no size increaseMinimum trading days
3ChallengeFlat day is acceptable; the requirement is participation, not profitMinimum trading days
4ChallengeContinue to the target without exceeding the daily budgetDaily limit and overall floor
5–12ChallengeOrdinary sessions; progress tracked against target and floorAll rules
At targetTransitionStop trading, confirm the phase is recorded as passedTarget reached, days satisfied
Next sessionVerificationReset risk unit for the new balance, smaller target, same limitsDaily loss limit unchanged
Verification days 1–4+VerificationRepeat the same discipline for roughly half the rewardMinimum days, daily limit, floor
At targetHandoverAccess rotated, login returned, funded terms readAccount ownership

Two features of that schedule are worth pulling out. The third day is a flat day, and it is not a failure: a minimum trading-day rule asks for participation rather than profit, so a session that ends unchanged still counts. And the transition from challenge to verification is not a break — the risk unit is recalculated for the new balance and the same daily discipline is applied immediately, because the limits do not get easier when the target gets smaller.

A realistic completion window for both phases lands in the region of two to four weeks. Providers who quote a specific date are describing an outcome they do not control; providers who quote a range and explain what happens if it is exceeded are describing a process.

How the account type changes the plan

FTMO offers more than one account type, and the differences are not cosmetic — they change which positions a plan is allowed to hold overnight and how the loss figures are calculated across a weekend.

Account typeTypical differenceWhat it changes in the plan
StandardPositions are generally not intended to be held through the weekend closeAll exposures are closed before the weekly close, so hold times are capped by the session
SwingWeekend holding is permitted, and targets are often set differentlyPositions can run for days, which raises gap risk and makes stop placement a weekend question rather than an intraday one

The distinction matters most for a service, because it determines whether the attempt is a series of intraday sessions or a small number of multi-day positions. A plan built for one and applied to the other will either close positions that the account type would have allowed to run, or carry exposure into a weekend on an account type that does not permit it. Confirm which type you are buying, and confirm it again in the funded phase: the holding rules that apply to a funded account are not always identical to the evaluation's.

There is a second-order effect too. Multi-day positions make the daily loss calculation more complicated, because an open position that is profitable on one day and losing the next contributes to the equity figure on both. On an account type that permits overnight holding, the daily limit is best treated as a live constraint rather than a figure you check at the close.

What can still go wrong

Even a well-run arrangement has failure modes, and they are worth naming so they are not mistaken for bad luck.

  • A rule revision mid-evaluation. Plan generations change. Save a dated copy of the terms at the start and re-read them before every payout-relevant step.
  • A daily-rule breach late in a phase. The most common ending. A plan without a hard daily stop is exposed to one bad session at any point in the process.
  • A verification-phase relapse. A smaller target invites looser sizing. The limits have not changed, so the margin has.
  • A handover problem. Credentials, two-factor prompts or email ownership blocking the transfer. Confirm the email on the account is yours from day one.
  • A funded-phase surprise. A payout cycle, a split percentage or a holding rule that was never read because the focus was entirely on passing.
  • A terms interpretation. The clause was read one way at the start and enforced another way at payout. This is the risk that belongs to you, which is why it is written down.

None of those are trading failures, and that is the point. They are planning, administrative and compliance failures, which is the category a competent service genuinely removes — and the reason the fee can be worth paying even though no honest provider can promise a result.

Four claims worth disbelieving

This is a market where the marketing is louder than the mechanics, so it helps to name the claims that do not survive contact with a rulebook.

"Passing is guaranteed." Nothing about an evaluation is guaranteed, because the outcome depends on market behaviour that no provider controls. A provider confident enough to guarantee a result has either priced the retries into the fee — which is a different and reasonable promise — or is planning not to be around when the guarantee is tested.

"The funded account stays with me." It should stay with you, and that is the whole point of the arrangement. Any structure in which the provider holds the funded login, the payout details or a share of the profit has changed the product. The evaluation is the part being sold; the funded account is yours.

"The daily limit is loose enough to ignore." The daily loss rule ends more attempts than the overall floor does, precisely because it can fire while the total budget is nearly intact. Treating it as a formality is the most expensive misreading of a rulebook available on this firm.

"Passing quickly is the goal." The minimum trading-day requirement means the calendar sets a floor on completion, and a plan that rushes the target tends to use the daily budget in the process. The goal is to complete both phases with the loss limits intact, and pace is a means rather than an achievement.

Each of those four claims is a shortcut past the terms check, the retry policy or the daily rule — the three things that actually determine whether the arrangement ends with a funded account you can withdraw from.

The verification checklist

Before paying any provider to pass an FTMO evaluation, get written answers to these seven questions and keep them together.

  1. Does FTMO's current terms permit the arrangement, and which clause was relied on?
  2. What exactly does the fee cover, and is the evaluation fee included?
  3. What is the retry policy — how many attempts, whether the reset fee is absorbed, over what period, and what voids it?
  4. Who holds the login, and until when?
  5. Is any share of funded profit taken?
  6. What is the timeline, and what happens if the phase takes longer?
  7. How is progress reported while the account is running?

A provider that answers all seven in writing, with a date, is behaving like a professional. A provider that answers four of them and deflects the rest is asking you to carry the risk that its own answers would have clarified — and at that point the comparison should be against trading the evaluation yourself, with the fee spent on something that persists.

Common questions

Is it allowed to have someone pass my FTMO challenge?

That depends on FTMO's terms at the time of your attempt, and specifically on the clauses covering account access and who may trade the account. The position must be confirmed in writing before payment. A prohibited arrangement can result in a closed account and a refused payout, and the risk sits with the account holder rather than the provider.

How many phases does an FTMO evaluation have?

Two: a Challenge and then a Verification, each with its own profit target and minimum trading-day requirement, and both governed by the same loss limits. A funded account is issued only after both are completed. Confirm the current targets and day requirements on FTMO's site before buying.

Which rule causes the most failures?

The daily loss rule. It can end an attempt while most of the overall loss budget is untouched, because it is measured per day rather than in total. A hard daily stop expressed in units of risk, set below the limit rather than at it, is the single most effective protection.

Does the service take a share of the funded profit?

A flat-fee service should not. The funded account remains in your name and the profit is yours, apart from FTMO's own profit split. A provider charging a percentage of funded profit has restructured itself as something closer to a managed account, which raises different questions about responsibility and control.

What happens if the evaluation fails?

It depends entirely on the written retry policy. Ask how many further attempts are covered, whether the firm's reset fee is included, over what period, and what would void the cover. A bundled retry is only valuable if the terms say the second attempt is genuinely covered at no extra service fee.

Does passing the evaluation hand me a funded account immediately?

The funded account is issued after both phases are complete and the minimum trading days are satisfied. The funded account then has its own rules, payout cycle and requirements, which are not identical to the evaluation's. Read the funded terms at the handover rather than at the first withdrawal request.

How long does it take?

A well-run attempt typically completes in around two weeks, but the floor is set by the minimum trading-day requirement in each phase rather than by the provider's speed. If the rules require a set number of traded days per phase, no amount of efficiency compresses the calendar.

Should I self-trade instead?

If you have a tested method and the hours available, self-trading is usually cheaper, because above roughly a 70% completion probability the expected cost of one paid attempt beats a flat fee. The service is aimed at traders whose constraint is time rather than skill — and the funded account still has to be traded by someone afterwards.
