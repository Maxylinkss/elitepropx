Prop Firms without Consistency Rule · reviewed October 2026

Prop Firms without Consistency Rule

A consistency rule caps the share of total profit that may come from one trading day. It can leave a profitable evaluation unpaid, so it belongs in the sizing plan from the first trade rather than in the payout checklist. This guide explains the arithmetic, separates firms that apply the rule at payout from firms that apply it during the evaluation, and names the specific plans where the rule is absent — because almost every firm on a "no consistency" list applies one somewhere.

Key takeaways

  • Common thresholds are 30–50% of total profit from any single day.
  • The rule is usually assessed at payout, not at the moment of the trade.
  • A distribution of ordinary days passes more reliably than one oversized winner.
  • “No consistency rule” at brand level does not guarantee it is absent from the plan you buy.
  • A trader with a lumpy method should model the best-day share before choosing a firm.

What the rule actually measures

A consistency rule divides the profit from the trader’s best day by the total profit of the evaluation or the payout period. If the ratio exceeds a threshold, the profit is considered too concentrated and the payout may be reduced, delayed or refused. The threshold is usually between 30% and 50%, but the definition of “day” is where most of the confusion lives.

Some firms measure a calendar day in the platform’s server time; others measure a trading session that crosses midnight. Some count realised profit only; others include open profit at the moment of measurement. A trader who closes a position at 23:55 on a server whose day rolls at midnight has a different best-day share from one whose position is closed at 00:05, even though the trade is the same.

The denominator matters as much as the numerator. If the rule is applied at payout, the denominator is the total profit accumulated to that point, which grows over time and makes the rule easier to satisfy with each additional week. If the rule is applied during the evaluation, the denominator is the profit to date, which is small early and makes an early large day disproportionately costly.

There are also consistency rules that are not called consistency rules: a cap on the share of profit from one instrument, a requirement that no single trade exceed a percentage of the target, or a rule that the largest winning day must be below a multiple of the average. Each behaves like a consistency test, and each belongs in the same review.

The first practical step is therefore linguistic. Ask the firm, in writing, which of those definitions applies, whether the test is at evaluation or payout, and what the consequence of a breach is: a refused payout, a reduced payout, a requirement to keep trading, or a reset. A “no consistency rule” claim that turns out to be a cap on one instrument is not a contradiction; it is a different clause with a similar effect.

The arithmetic of a best-day cap

Suppose a trader makes $10,000 over an evaluation, and $4,500 of it arrives on a single Tuesday. The best-day share is 45%. Under a 50% cap the evaluation is consistent; under a 40% cap it is not. The same profit, from the same trades, produces a different outcome depending on a number the trader may not have read.

The table explains why the rule punishes exactly the traders it is designed to catch: the ones who take a single oversized position and let it run. A method that produces a smooth series of small gains rarely approaches the cap. A method built around one high-conviction trade a week can breach it while being perfectly profitable.

The cost of a breach is not always a refusal. Some firms allow the trader to continue until the best-day share falls below the threshold, which converts the rule into a requirement to keep trading. That is a hidden cost: more trades, more spread, more rule exposure, and more time before the payout. The effective fee for a lumpy method is therefore higher than the sticker price.

A trader can also satisfy the rule by reducing the size of the best day. If the Tuesday trade would have produced $4,500 at full size, taking half the position produces $2,250 and a 22.5% share at the same total profit, assuming the rest of the month is unchanged. That is a real cost in expected profit, and it should be compared with the cost of choosing a firm without the rule.

The comparison is usually favourable to the no-rule firm for lumpy methods and neutral for smooth ones. A smooth method pays nothing for the rule because it never approaches the threshold. A lumpy method pays either in reduced size or in a longer path to payout, and both are more expensive than a slightly higher fee at a firm that does not apply the test.

Where the rule bites: evaluation or payout

At the evaluation stage, the rule constrains the strategy. A trader who would normally take a large position after a run of losses has to decide whether the resulting day would breach the cap. That changes the risk plan, not just the payout. The rule becomes part of position sizing.

At the payout stage, the rule constrains the value of the pass. A trader can complete the evaluation legitimately, receive the funded account, and then discover that the first withdrawal is capped because one day produced too much of the profit. The account is real; the cash is smaller or later than expected.

Some firms apply the test only to the first payout, some to every payout, and some only when the payout exceeds a threshold. The distinction changes the arithmetic of a long-term funded account: a rule that applies once is a one-off cost, while a rule that applies every month is a permanent constraint on the method.

The practical response is to ask for the clause in writing and to read it as a formula rather than as a sentence. Which days are counted, from which account, over which period, and what happens to profit above the cap. A firm that cannot answer those four questions has not explained its own rule.

There is a second-order effect worth noting. A trader who knows the rule exists may trade more conservatively during the evaluation and then increase size on the funded account, where the rule no longer applies. That is rational, but it means the evaluation does not test the method that will actually be used, which reduces the information value of the pass.

Why firms use the rule

The rule exists because a funded account is a liability to the firm, and a trader who makes a year’s profit in one day is a different risk from one who makes it over a year. A single oversized win can indicate a position that was never sized for the account, and the firm does not want to fund the next version of that trade.

There is also a commercial motive that is rarely stated. A consistency rule gives the firm a reason to slow a payout without accusing the trader of breaching a loss limit. The trader has not lost money; the profit is simply too concentrated. That makes the rule a softer instrument than a hard breach, which is why it appears more often at the payout stage than during the evaluation.

For the trader, the practical consequence is that the rule is a term of the contract, not a moral judgement. A method that produces lumpy results is not wrong; it is simply a poor match for a plan with a best-day cap. The correct response is to choose a plan without the cap, or to accept the cost of trading smaller, rather than to argue about the rule after the fact.

It is also worth separating the rule from the firm’s other payout conditions. A minimum number of trading days, a minimum withdrawal and a verification step can each delay a payout independently of consistency. A trader who has satisfied the consistency test can still wait, and the total wait is what determines the return on the attempt.

How to test your own distribution

Before choosing a firm, calculate the best-day share of your last three months of trading. Use the same definition the firm uses: server-day profit divided by total profit over the period. If the share is consistently below 25%, the rule is unlikely to bind and you can choose on other criteria. If it is above 35%, the rule is a live constraint and the firm list should be filtered accordingly.

Run the test on a losing month as well. The rule is usually applied to profit, so a month with a small total profit and one large day produces a high share even if the dollar amount is modest. A trader who has a $2,000 month with a $900 day is at 45%, which fails a 40% cap despite the month being unremarkable.

The test should also be run on the trade log rather than on memory. Traders remember the big day and forget the distribution, and the big day is exactly what the rule measures. A spreadsheet with the daily profit column and a running total is enough; the ratio is the maximum daily profit divided by the total.

Finally, test the method under a size constraint. If the rule would force the best day to be halved, does the method still reach the target in the same time? If not, the rule is not a footnote; it is a different strategy, and the firm that applies it is the wrong choice regardless of the fee.

E8 Markets

E8 Markets sells only one-step products in 2026, and the consistency answer differs by product. E8 Pro states that it has no consistency rule at all, with an 8% target, a 2.5% daily drawdown, an 8% static floor and a 2% daily profit cap. One applies a 40% best-day rule at payout, and Signature applies 35%.

That makes Pro the product to compare if the purpose is to avoid a best-day cap. The daily profit cap is the trade-off: the account cannot make more than 2% in a day, so the target is necessarily distributed even though no consistency test applies at withdrawal.

The caution is that the brand-level answer is wrong in both directions. A trader who reads "E8 has no consistency rule" and buys One will meet a 40% test at payout; a trader who assumes every E8 product has one will miss Pro entirely.

FTMO

FTMO runs a two-phase evaluation followed by a verification stage, with a static maximum loss and an equity-based daily loss limit. It is the most widely documented rulebook in the retail market, which is an advantage for a trader who wants to know exactly what the terms mean before buying.

The static floor means progress does not move the failure point closer, so a trader can hold a position through a retracement without the account failing at a higher level. The daily loss measured on equity is the counterweight: open positions count against the daily limit, which ends a session earlier than many traders expect.

On consistency, the answer is product-specific and unusually clean. The 2-Step FTMO Challenge has no consistency rule at evaluation or at payout. The 1-Step product applies a 50% consistency rule, and the Instant product applies a limit of its own. A trader who wants no distribution test should buy the 2-Step and not the 1-Step.

Funding Pips

Funding Pips sells a two-phase evaluation with a static loss floor, priced toward the lower end of the market. The structure is simple: a target, a static floor, a one-off fee, and a payout schedule. For a trader whose main concern is the consistency rule, the simplicity is useful because there are fewer clauses to reconcile.

The static floor keeps the failure point fixed, which makes the risk plan legible. The caution is model choice: the firm offers fewer structures than multi-plan competitors, so a trader who wants a one-step path or a very large account may need to look elsewhere.

Ask specifically whether the payout carries a best-day test on the plan being bought. The answer can differ between plan generations, and the cheapest visible plan is sometimes the older one with different payout terms.

Topstep

Topstep is a futures evaluator built around the Trading Combine, with a trailing loss floor and a monthly subscription. The daily loss limit is optional at checkout rather than standard: a Combine bought without it has only the trailing maximum loss, so a bad session is bounded by the floor rather than by a fixed daily figure.

The trailing floor is the mechanic to understand before buying. It rises with the equity high, so open profit can raise the failure point even before it is banked. A trader who gives back gains is charged for the give-back, and the risk unit should shrink as the account grows.

On consistency, Topstep applies a 55% best-day expectation relative to the profit target during the Combine, and a 40% rule only on the optional consistency payout path. That is a different shape from Apex's 50% at payout: Topstep's main test shapes the evaluation itself, so a lumpy method has to be diluted before the pass, not only before the withdrawal.

FTUK

FTUK is a futures evaluator with a trailing threshold and one-off billing. The one-off fee removes the calendar pressure of a subscription: a slow attempt does not accumulate charges, which suits a trader who expects to take longer than a month.

The trailing threshold still punishes a give-back, so the method has to be compatible with a moving failure point. A trader who takes profit at a fixed target is barely affected; one who trails a stop behind price is affected on every winner.

The consistency question should be asked at the payout stage. A futures payout condition can be phrased as a best-day expectation rather than a consistency rule, and the effect is the same. Confirm the threshold and the consequence of a breach before buying.

Lucid Trading

Lucid Trading offers a simple ladder of futures accounts with a trailing threshold and a monthly subscription. The ladder makes it easy to start small and scale, and the subscription means the cost of a slow pass rises with the calendar rather than with the number of attempts.

The trailing drawdown limits give-back tolerance, which is the main constraint for a futures method that holds through retracements. The rule should be read as a limit on open profit as well as on losses, because the failure point follows the high.

On consistency, the payout terms for the exact account size decide whether a best-day test applies. Ask for the clause and the threshold rather than assuming that a subscription model implies a particular payout condition.

FXIFY

FXIFY sells six programs, and the floor and the consistency answer change with the name. Two Phase Standard uses a 10% trailing floor and has no consistency rule. Two Phase Pro uses an 8% static floor and also has none, but adds a 3-day minimum of 0.5% days on the funded account. Two Phase Classic uses a 10% static floor and adds a 25% consistency rule on the funded account. Lightning applies 30% during the evaluation and on the funded account.

That makes Two Phase Pro the cleanest answer for a lumpy method: static, no consistency rule, and a 4% then 8% target path. The trade-off is the funded-stage qualifying-day rule, which is a different constraint from a best-day cap.

A trader comparing "FXIFY" to another brand without naming the program is comparing a logo to a rulebook. The same warning applies to every row on this page.

Alpha Futures

Alpha Futures is a futures evaluator with a trailing threshold and a monthly subscription, aimed at traders who want modern platform access. The subscription suits a trader who expects to pass quickly and penalises one who does not.

The trailing threshold is the defining mechanic. A trader who builds a cushion early has more room later, while a trader who starts slowly has less. That favours methods with a strong opening week over methods that grind out gains.

On consistency, the payout conditions should be read for the exact account size and platform. A futures payout test can be expressed as a best-day share, and a trader with a lumpy method should model the ratio before buying.

The Trading Pit

The Trading Pit is a multi-asset firm that sells allocation-style programmes as well as conventional challenges. The structure is different from a checkout challenge: the relationship can involve a longer onboarding and a different payout rhythm.

Because the programmes vary, the consistency rule has to be checked per programme rather than per brand. A plan that applies a best-day test at payout behaves differently from one that applies it during the evaluation, even when the threshold is the same.

The caution is complexity. A multi-programme firm offers more choices, and each choice carries its own terms. A trader who wants a simple answer to the consistency question should compare the specific programme documents rather than the summary page.

How to choose between them

Start with the market you can trade in the hours you are available. That single filter usually removes half the list. Then choose the loss-floor mechanic that matches your holding period: static for strategies that endure retracements, trailing for strategies that take profit quickly and re-enter. Then compare billing against your expected timeline.

Only after those three filters should price enter the decision. A $50 difference in entry fee is noise compared with the difference between passing on the second attempt and the fifth. The total cost across attempts is the number to compare, and it depends on rulebook fit more than on the checkout price.

The consistency question cuts across all three filters. A firm that fits on market, floor and billing but applies a 30% best-day cap to a lumpy method is not a fit, however cheap it is. A firm that fits on the first three and has no cap at payout is worth a premium.

Finally, verify the terms for the exact plan you are buying. Every firm on this list publishes its schedule, and every schedule can change between cohorts. A dated screenshot of the terms page is the only record that will resolve a dispute about what applied on the day of purchase.

What to confirm before buying

Confirm whether the consistency rule applies during the evaluation, at payout, or both. The stage determines whether the rule changes the strategy or the value of the pass.

Confirm the threshold and the definition of a day. A 30% cap measured on server-day realised profit is a different rule from a 40% cap measured on a rolling 24-hour window.

Confirm the consequence of a breach: a refused payout, a reduced payout, a requirement to keep trading, or a reset. The consequence is the cost, and it is rarely on the pricing page.

Confirm whether the rule applies to the first payout only or to every payout. A one-off test is a cost of entry; a recurring test is a permanent constraint on the funded account.

Confirm the loss floor, the daily stop and the billing rhythm for the same plan. A consistency rule is one constraint among several, and the cheapest plan is not the cheapest attempt if the floor or the billing works against the method.

Save the terms page as a dated file and keep the provider’s written answers with it. Rules change, and the version that applied on the purchase date is the one that matters.

Common questions

Which prop firms have no consistency rule?

It varies by plan rather than by firm. Several firms on this list publish plans without a best-day cap, but the same brand can apply one to a different plan or at payout. Confirm the clause for the exact plan and stage before buying.

Is a consistency rule assessed during the evaluation or at payout?

Both patterns exist. A rule applied during the evaluation constrains position sizing; a rule applied at payout constrains the value of the withdrawal. The clause should say which one applies.

What is a typical consistency threshold?

Most rules cap the best day at 30% to 50% of total profit. The exact number matters less than the definition of a day and the consequence of a breach.

Can I avoid the rule by trading smaller?

Sometimes. Reducing the size of the best day lowers its share, but it also lowers expected profit and may lengthen the path to the target. Compare that cost with the fee at a firm without the rule.

Does the rule apply to futures and forex the same way?

No. Futures firms often express the test as a best-day expectation at payout, while forex firms may apply a percentage cap during the evaluation. Read the plan’s own wording rather than a generic explanation.
