How to pass a Blue Sky Trading challenge: the rulebook, the math and the plan

Blue Sky Trading evaluates futures accounts against a trailing threshold, charges a monthly subscription and pays out after qualification. That combination decides more about an attempt than any entry signal: the trailing floor sets the size, the subscription sets the pace, and the payout conditions set what a pass is worth. This guide works through the rulebook in the order that ends or saves an account — floor, sizing, calendar, payout — using illustrative numbers so the arithmetic is visible. Confirm the live schedule on the plan page before you buy; the mechanics are the firm's and the figures here are models.

Key takeaways

  • The loss floor trails peak equity, so a winning day reduces the dollar room available to the next losing day.
  • A daily stop applies on top of the trail, which means the worst day is bounded but the worst week is not.
  • Futures sizing is contracts and ticks, so the risk unit is a product of tick value and stop distance, not lot size.
  • Billing is a monthly subscription: a slow attempt costs more every month, which changes the optimal pace.
  • Consistency conditions apply, so a pass built on one oversized session can still fail at the payout stage.
  • The platforms are Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader, which makes this an order-flow product rather than an MT4 one.

How the Blue Sky Trading rulebook is built

An evaluation is a set of constraints applied at the same time, not a profit target with footnotes. The target says how much has to be earned. The loss floor says how much room exists to earn it. The daily stop says how quickly a bad session ends. The consistency condition says how the profit has to be distributed. The billing rhythm says what time costs. Change any one of those and the same method produces a different result, which is why comparing firms on target size and fee is close to comparing nothing.

Blue Sky Trading's published shape is a futures evaluation with a trailing threshold, a daily stop, a consistency test and monthly subscription billing. Payouts arrive after qualification, and the platform list is Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader. Each of those rows has a distinct consequence: trailing means the failure point moves, daily means a session can be lost without the account being lost, consistency means the shape of the profit matters, and subscription means the calendar is billed.

The order to read them is fixed. Floor first, because it decides position size. Daily stop second, because it decides the worst session. Consistency third, because it decides whether a fast pass converts into a payout. Everything else — platforms, data fees, session hours — is a preference or a cost, and neither can rescue a size that the floor cannot support.

The trailing floor, in dollars

Take an illustrative $50,000 futures account with a $2,000 trailing threshold. On day one the failure point is $48,000. If the account trades up to $51,500, the failure point moves to $49,500, and the room measured from the current balance is still $2,000 — but the room measured from the original balance has fallen from $2,000 to $1,500. Nothing about the trader's method changed. The account simply became less forgiving because it worked.

This is the mechanic that catches traders coming from static forex evaluations. On a static account, a $1,500 gain leaves $3,500 of room below the original start and the same risk unit remains valid. On a trailing account, the same gain leaves the same $2,000 of room from the high, so the position size that was correct at the start is now correct only if the trader recalibrates every morning. A trader who does not recalibrate is quietly increasing risk as the account improves.

The consequence for planning is that the attempt should be treated as a sequence of independent sessions, each with its own risk budget: remaining room divided by the number of losses you are willing to absorb, capped at your original unit. If the cushion is $1,400 and the plan allows seven losses, the unit is $200. If the cushion falls to $600, the same seven-loss rule gives $85, and the honest response is either to trade that size or to stop for the day.

There is also a definition to confirm in writing: whether the threshold trails closed balance or peak equity including open profit. On a peak-equity trail, an open winner lifts the failure point while the position is still running, and a retracement can close the account before the trade is ever closed. That is a materially different rule from a closed-balance trail, and it belongs in the notes you keep beside the account.

Sizing contracts against the remaining room

Futures sizing is arithmetic on tick value. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract (MNQ) is $2 per tick, so a 20-tick stop risks $40 per contract. With a $240 risk unit, six contracts fit. One E-mini S&P contract (ES) is $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100 per contract and the same $240 unit buys two contracts. The position count differs by a factor of three for the same dollar risk, which is why futures sizing has to be calculated rather than intuited.

The second calculation is the one traders skip: the maximum position the rulebook permits. Many futures evaluations cap contracts at a number that is lower than the risk budget would suggest on the smallest stop. A size that fits the risk budget but exceeds the cap is not a size you can take, and the cap usually appears in the plan table rather than the marketing page.

The third calculation is the buffer. Stops in futures do not always fill at their level, particularly around the open, around economic releases and during thin overnight hours. A ten to twenty percent buffer on the risk unit is not pessimism; it is the difference between a planned loss and a loss that consumes two planned units. If the buffer makes the position size impractical, the stop is too wide for the account, and the trade should be skipped rather than compressed.

Finally, write the size down before the session. A trader who computes size in the moment, with the position open and the P&L moving, sizes for the outcome they want rather than the room they have. A pre-session card with room, unit, contracts and maximum losses is a five-minute exercise that removes the largest single source of breach.

The daily stop and why it is not the same as the floor

The daily stop bounds a session. The trailing floor bounds the attempt. A trader can respect the daily stop every day and still lose the account over three weeks, because three bad sessions in a row can consume more than the remaining room even when each one is legal. The daily stop is a discipline instrument; the floor is a solvency instrument. They are not substitutes.

Assume a $1,000 daily stop and a $240 risk unit. The session can absorb four losses before the stop binds. That is the real limit on activity, not the number of valid setups on the screen. If the method has a normal losing run of five, the daily stop will end sessions mid-run, and the attempt needs a plan for that: either reduce the unit so seven losses fit inside the day, or accept that some sessions end early and account for it in the schedule.

The basis matters as much as the number. A daily stop measured on equity counts open losses immediately, so a position $700 underwater has consumed $700 of the allowance before it is closed. A stop measured on realised balance counts only closed trades, which allows an open position to exceed the limit until it is closed — a distinction that changes behaviour on the last trade of the day.

There is also the reset time. Futures sessions roll at a defined hour, and a position held across the roll can be counted in either session depending on the firm's definition. A trader who holds overnight should know which day the loss belongs to, because a breach attributed to the wrong session is a dispute rather than a stop.

Consistency conditions and the minimum-day gap

A consistency condition limits how much of the profit can come from one day, or from a small number of days. It exists to stop a pass built on a single oversized position, and it changes the shape of a correct attempt: the target has to be reached through a series of ordinary sessions rather than one exceptional one. A trader whose method produces occasional large days is affected; a trader who grinds out small gains is not.

The test is easy to run on your own record before buying. Take the last sixty trading days, total the profit, and calculate the share produced by the best day and the best five. If the best day is a large fraction of the total, a consistency rule will bind and the plan needs to be designed around distribution before the first trade. If it is small, the rule is unlikely to matter and the decision should be made on the floor instead.

Minimum trading days are listed as undefined on this product, which is a meaningful gap rather than a detail. With no minimum, a pass can be completed quickly, which is valuable under subscription billing. It also means there is no structural forcing function to build a sample, so the trader has to impose one: a self-imposed minimum of ten sessions before requesting a payout review produces a record that looks like a process rather than a lucky week.

Confirm in writing whether the consistency test applies during the evaluation, after funding, or only at the payout request. The three answers imply different plans: a test at payout means the profit can be earned freely and then checked, while a test during the evaluation shapes every day of the attempt.

Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader

All three platforms are built for futures. Tradovate is the lightest of the three, browser-based with a clean DOM and a short learning curve. Rithmic is a data and routing layer with front ends built on top of it, valued for depth-of-market data and execution speed. NinjaTrader is the most programmable, with a mature strategy framework and a large ecosystem of add-ons.

The choice matters more than it does on forex because the workflow is different. A trader coming from MT5 expects server-held stops, one-click position sizing in lots and a chart that behaves a particular way. On a futures platform, orders rest at the exchange, positions are counted in contracts, and the DOM is where most execution decisions are made. That transition takes weeks, not hours, and it should happen before the evaluation starts rather than during it.

Data fees are the hidden line item. Futures data is usually billed separately from the evaluation, sometimes by the platform and sometimes by the data provider. A monthly subscription plus a data fee is the real monthly cost of an attempt, and for a slow attempt that number can exceed the evaluation fee itself. Ask which feeds are included before choosing a platform, and check whether the feed you need for your instrument is the cheap one.

Automation is the fourth consideration. NinjaTrader supports coded strategies; Tradovate and Rithmic rely on third-party bridges. If the plan depends on an automated system, confirm that the platform and the firm both permit it, and confirm the rulebook's position on high-frequency activity, because automation and scalping rules interact badly when they are written separately.

The US futures session map

Futures trade nearly around the clock, but liquidity is concentrated. The regular session carries the deepest book, the tightest spreads and the largest share of the day's range. The overnight session is thinner, spreads widen, and stop fills are less reliable. A method calibrated on regular-hours data behaves differently in the overnight session, and an evaluation does not care why.

The practical map is a three-block day: the first hour after the open, the middle of the session, and the close. The first hour carries the widest range and the widest spreads; the middle carries the cleanest trends and the least noise; the close carries position squaring and settlement effects. Most discretionary futures methods fit one of the three, and trading outside the fitted block is where the unplanned losses come from.

Time zones are part of the rulebook. A trader in Europe watching the US open is trading in the evening; a trader in Asia is trading overnight. The evaluation's session hours are fixed in the firm's time zone, and the daily reset is fixed too. Put both on the calendar before the first trade, because a session that ends at the wrong local hour will eventually produce a position held through a reset.

Holding overnight adds gap risk and, on some plans, a restriction. The relevant question is not whether holding is allowed but whether it is priced: an overnight gap can jump a stop by a multiple of its distance, so the risk unit for an overnight position should be smaller than the unit for a session trade, or the position should be flat before the close.

A twenty-session plan

Sessions one to four are calibration. Trade half the normal unit, record the remaining room before every entry, and confirm how the trailing floor behaves after a winner. The goal is not profit; it is to learn the account's mechanics at a size where a mistake is survivable. Most attempts that fail in week one fail because the trader treated calibration as an opportunity to get ahead.

Sessions five to twelve are the working block. Normal unit, a fixed number of setups per session, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recalculate the room each morning and halve the unit when it falls below the figure set in advance. Under a trailing floor, this is the block where the floor usually starts to matter, because a good run raises the failure point and the same size becomes relatively larger.

Sessions thirteen to sixteen are the distribution block. If a consistency condition applies, this is where the profit shape is corrected: smaller targets, more sessions, no oversized day. A trader who is ahead of the target at this point should be reducing risk, not pressing, because the marginal value of more profit is lower than the marginal cost of losing the cushion.

Sessions seventeen to twenty are the close. Reduce the unit by half, protect the target, and prepare the payout documentation — identity verification, payment method, statements. The last week of an evaluation is an administrative exercise with a small trading component, and treating it as a trading opportunity is how a completed target becomes a breached account.

The profit target as a distribution, not a number

Assume an illustrative $3,000 target on a $50,000 account, a $240 unit and a 2:1 payoff. A winning trade returns $480, so the target needs roughly seven net winners. That is a useful reframing: the target is not a dollar figure to chase, it is a count of normal outcomes. Seven net winners over twenty sessions is a modest requirement, and it stays modest as long as the unit does not grow.

The distribution matters as much as the count. If a consistency condition caps a single day at a share of the total, the target has to be built from days that each contribute a similar amount. A plan that produces one $2,000 day and six small winners may hit the number and fail the check. A plan that produces ten $300 days passes both.

The target also interacts with the floor in a way that is easy to miss. On a trailing account, the first third of the target is cheap and the last third is expensive, because the floor has risen with the profit. The correct response is to reduce size as the target approaches, which feels wrong and is arithmetically right: the remaining room is smaller, so the same unit now represents a larger share of the cushion.

A written target plan should state the unit for each phase, the maximum losses per session, and the condition for stopping for the week. Three lines, reviewed every morning, are worth more than a strategy document, because the failure mode on this product is not a bad method — it is a good method traded at a size the floor cannot support.

Losing streaks and the probability of survival

Assume a method with a 45% hit rate and a 2:1 payoff. The expectancy is positive, and the probability of six consecutive losses is about 2.8% per attempt of six trades — which sounds small until it is applied to a hundred sessions. Over a twenty-session attempt with three trades per session, a six-loss run is a realistic event, not a tail risk, and the account must be sized to survive it.

That is the whole argument for sizing from the floor rather than from confidence. A unit that survives six losses on a $2,000 cushion is $250 with a buffer, which is close to the illustrative unit above. A unit of $500 survives four losses and fails the fifth, and the fifth arrives in most twenty-session samples. The difference between those two traders is not skill; it is division.

The daily stop changes the shape of the calculation. If the stop binds at four losses, the streak is interrupted rather than survived, and the account continues to the next session with the cushion intact. That is a genuine benefit of a daily stop on a trailing product: it converts one long streak into several short ones, each of which resets the risk budget.

The counter-argument is that interrupting a streak also interrupts a recovery. A trader who is stopped for the day cannot make the loss back in the same session, which is exactly the behaviour the rule is designed to prevent. The correct response to a stopped session is a review, not a second session on another instrument.

Execution, slippage and the cost of a stop

Futures execution is fast and, in liquid contracts, cheap. That does not make stops exact. A stop order becomes a market order when triggered, and in a fast tape the fill can be several ticks beyond the level. On a 20-tick stop, a 3-tick slip is a 15% overrun; on an 8-tick stop it is 37%. The tighter the stop, the more the slippage matters relative to the risk unit.

The practical response is to size for the fill rather than the level. If the historical slip on your instrument is three ticks at the open, budget the stop plus three ticks when computing contracts. That is conservative, and it is the difference between a risk unit that is real and one that exists only on the order ticket. Limit orders avoid the slip and risk the miss; for a method that depends on being filled, the trade-off has to be measured rather than assumed.

Commissions are the other execution cost. Futures commissions are charged per contract per side, so a six-contract round turn is twelve commission units. On a small target, commissions can consume a visible share of the profit, and on a scalping method they can consume most of it. Add them to the model before the attempt, not after the first statement.

Finally, execution discipline is a rulebook issue as much as a technical one. Some plans restrict certain order types, some restrict activity around releases, and some treat rapid entry and exit as a pattern to review. Read those clauses before building an automated method, because a compliant strategy on a non-compliant order type is still a breach.

News windows and restricted activity

Futures react to the same macro calendar as forex, with a shorter list of dominant events: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment data and, for energy and metals, inventory reports. The reaction is fast and the spread widens before the number is released, which is when stop fills are worst. A position held through a release is a position with an unknown stop distance.

The rulebook question is whether holding through a release is restricted, and how the restriction is defined. Definitions vary: a window of two minutes before and after, a window of thirty minutes, or a prohibition on opening new positions in the period. The same practice can be compliant on one plan and a breach on another, which is why the clause should be quoted in a support reply rather than inferred from a summary.

The conservative plan is to flatten before scheduled releases and re-enter after the first hour, when the spread has normalised and the initial move has been absorbed. It costs some opportunity and removes a category of dispute. A trader who insists on holding should at minimum halve the position and widen the stop to a level the account can survive if it gaps.

Unscheduled news is different. A headline can move the tape without warning, and no plan can flatten in advance. The only protection is size: a unit small enough that a gap through the stop is a bad day rather than a lost account. That is the same argument as the streak calculation, applied to a single event.

Qualification, payouts and the review

Payouts arrive after qualification, which means the pass is the beginning of a second process rather than the end of the first. The qualification usually involves a number of profitable days, a minimum balance, and a completed identity and payment verification. Each of those is a gate that can delay funds even when the trading is finished and correct.

The review covers the trading record: statements, the distribution of profit, the size relative to the rules, and any pattern that looks like prohibited practice. A trader with a journal answers the review in an afternoon. A trader without one is relying on the firm's interpretation of a statement, which is a weaker position than it sounds, because the statement shows what happened and the journal shows why.

Prepare the documentation before the request: identity documents, proof of address, the payment method used for the fee, and a dated copy of the terms. Withdrawals usually return to the original payment method, so a mismatched method triggers a manual review and a delay measured in weeks. None of this is trading work, and all of it is on the critical path to cash.

Finally, confirm the split and the cycle in dollars rather than percentages. A 90% split paid in thirty days is worth less to a trader who needs the cash to keep trading than an 80% split paid in seven. The comparison that matters is total time from first payment to cleared funds, and it is usually longer than either the fee page or the split page suggests.

What twelve months actually costs

Subscription billing makes cost a function of duration. At an illustrative $150 per month plus a $50 data fee, a one-month pass costs $200, a three-month pass costs $600, and a six-month pass costs $1,200. The evaluation fee is the same in each case; the difference is entirely the calendar. That is why a plan with no minimum-day rule rewards a fast, correctly sized attempt rather than a slow, cautious one.

The comparison with a one-off product is not as simple as it looks. A one-off fee of $500 for an unlimited attempt is cheaper than six months of subscription, but only if the attempt is likely to last that long. A trader who passes in five weeks is better off with the subscription; a trader who needs four months is not. The break-even is worth calculating before buying, because the two billing models reward opposite pacing strategies.

Retries multiply everything. A trader who needs three attempts on a subscription product pays three times the monthly cost plus the elapsed months, which can be several times the headline fee. Model the expected cost as the monthly cost times the expected number of months times the expected number of attempts, and compare that with the payout the account is likely to produce. Most traders are surprised by the result, and by how much it improves when the risk unit is reduced by a third.

There are also the costs that do not appear on the firm's page: the data feed, the platform, the time, and the opportunity cost of capital tied up in an evaluation. They are small individually and decisive in aggregate, particularly for a trader running several attempts in parallel.

The five ways this attempt fails

The first is sizing from the account size rather than the remaining room. A $50,000 account with a $2,000 trail is not a $50,000 risk budget; it is a $2,000 one, and a unit of one percent of the account is a quarter of the floor per trade. That single error accounts for more breaches than any strategy failure.

The second is treating the daily stop as advisory. A session that ends at the limit and continues on another instrument is two sessions against one limit, and the rulebook counts the account, not the instrument. The third is passing on an oversized day and then discovering the consistency condition at payout, which converts a successful evaluation into a failed withdrawal.

The fourth is holding through a scheduled release on a plan that restricts it. The restriction is usually stated, rarely read and enforced at review, which makes it a delayed failure with a fully documented trail. The fifth is reaching the target and then increasing size to "make the pass worthwhile", which is the most common way a completed target becomes a breached account in the final week.

All five are process failures rather than market failures. That is encouraging: they are preventable with a written plan, a pre-session card and a rule against changing size after a good day. None of them requires a better strategy, and none of them is fixed by one.

Who should buy this product

This product fits a futures trader who already has a platform workflow, understands tick-based sizing, and can watch the US session. The trailing floor and the daily stop suit a method with a defined stop and a modest target per trade, because both rules reward precision and punish improvisation. The subscription makes it attractive to a trader who expects to pass in one or two months and does not want to pay for a path they will not use.

It fits less well a trader who needs a static floor, who trades a method with occasional deep retracements, or who cannot be present during the regular session. It also fits poorly a trader whose edge depends on a small number of large days, because the consistency condition and the trailing floor both penalise that distribution. Those are not criticisms of the product; they are mismatches between a rulebook and a method.

If the fit is unclear, paper-trade the exact rules for two weeks: simulate the trail by recording the equity high each day, apply the daily stop, and check whether the method's normal losing streak fits inside the remaining room. A fortnight of that exercise is cheaper than a failed attempt and more informative than any comparison table.

If the fit is clear, the plan is unglamorous: size from the floor, respect the daily stop, build the profit in small blocks, and treat the last week as administration. The rulebook does the rest.

Common questions

Does Blue Sky Trading use a trailing drawdown?

The evaluation described here uses a trailing threshold, so the failure point follows the equity high. That means a winning run reduces the dollar distance available for the next retracement, and the risk unit has to be recalculated each session.

Is there a daily stop as well as a floor?

Yes. The daily stop caps a single session while the trailing floor caps the attempt. Passing one does not satisfy the other: three legal sessions can still consume more room than the account has.

How much can I risk per trade on a $50,000 account?

With an illustrative $2,000 trail, a unit of $200 to $250 survives eight to ten losses with a buffer. The exact figure is the remaining room divided by the number of losses the plan allows, capped at the original unit.

Do commissions and data fees count towards the target?

Commissions are deducted from the account and therefore affect the balance the target is measured on. Data fees are usually billed separately by the platform or provider and do not move the balance, but they do change the real cost of the attempt.

Can the evaluation be traded by a third party?

Only if the firm's terms permit it. Some rulebooks prohibit third-party trading, and a pass obtained against those terms can be reversed at payout. Confirm the clause in writing before paying anyone to trade the account.

What happens if a stop fills beyond its level?

The realised loss is larger than planned, and it counts against both the daily stop and the trailing floor. Size with a buffer of ten to twenty percent so a slipped fill consumes one risk unit rather than two.
