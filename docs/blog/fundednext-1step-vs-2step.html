FundedNext 1-step vs 2-step: choosing the structure

FundedNext sells both one-step and two-step evaluations, and they are not the same product with a shorter queue. The one-step plan compresses the path and usually compresses the loss allowance with it; the two-step plan adds a phase and a second target in exchange for more room and a different failure profile. This guide compares the two structures on the numbers that decide outcomes — drawdown, target in R, calendar, cost per attempt and the psychology of each finish — and gives you a way to choose without relying on the marketing tier names.

Key takeaways

  • The one-step plan is faster but usually offers less drawdown room relative to its target. Speed is bought with margin for error.
  • The two-step plan adds a phase and a second target, but the first phase is often more forgiving, which changes the failure profile.
  • Compare the target-to-drawdown ratio, not the headline percentage. A 10% target against a 6% floor is a harder product than a 10% target against a 10% floor.
  • Check the current FundedNext terms for the specific plan and account size before buying. Plan names persist; mechanics change.

The structural difference

A one-step evaluation asks you to reach one profit target while staying inside one set of loss limits. A two-step evaluation asks you to do that twice, usually with a smaller target in each phase and a verification stage at the end. The difference sounds administrative. In practice it changes four things: how much room you have relative to the target, how long you are exposed to the rules, how many chances you get to fail, and how the firm prices the attempt.

FundedNext's two structures are often described as "1-step" and "2-step" or "Stellar" and "Evaluation", depending on the current naming. The names are not the analysis. What matters is the schedule attached to the specific plan you are buying: the target, the maximum loss, the daily loss, the minimum days, the consistency rule and the profit split. Those six numbers are the product. A comparison that starts with the plan name and stops there will be wrong often enough to matter.

The first structural difference is the number of targets. A one-step plan has one target; a two-step plan has two, usually with a smaller target in phase one and a larger one in phase two. The total profit required across both phases can be similar to the one-step target, or higher, depending on the schedule. The second difference is the loss allowance in each phase. A two-step plan may offer a larger percentage drawdown in phase one and a tighter one in phase two, or the reverse. The third is the calendar: a one-step plan often has a time limit or a minimum-day requirement, while a two-step plan may have a limit per phase.

What each plan optimises

Every evaluation design optimises for something. Understanding the optimisation tells you which trader the plan was built for, and whether you are that trader.

The one-step plan optimises for speed

A single target means a single sequence of decisions. There is no phase boundary at which the rules reset, no second target to plan for, and no verification phase after the profit is made. For a trader with a tested method and a clear risk unit, that simplicity is valuable: the plan is one campaign, not two. The cost is usually a tighter drawdown relative to the target, because the firm is taking on the same risk over a shorter path. A faster finish means less time for the firm's exposure to accumulate, which is why the loss allowance can be smaller.

The two-step plan optimises for margin

Two phases give the trader more room in the first phase, which is where most failures happen. A trader who is still finding their rhythm can make mistakes in phase one without losing the whole attempt, provided the phase-one drawdown is wide enough. The second phase is usually tighter, because the firm now has evidence that the trader can perform. The cost is calendar risk: two phase deadlines, a verification stage, and more time for rules to change or for the trader to lose discipline.

The hidden optimisation: data

A two-step plan also gives the firm more data about the trader. Two phases produce a longer track record, which the firm can use to assess consistency and to decide whether to fund. A one-step plan produces less data, which is one reason the drawdown may be tighter: the firm is compensating for the shorter observation window. This is not a conspiracy; it is the economics of underwriting a trader you have watched for less time.

The practical question is not which plan is "better" in general. It is which plan matches the shape of your edge. A trader with a high win rate and a small average loss may prefer the one-step plan, because the target is reached in fewer trades and the tighter drawdown is manageable. A trader with a lower win rate and a larger average win may prefer the two-step plan, because the wider phase-one room absorbs the losing streaks that are normal for that style.

The first observation is that the two-step plan requires more total profit — $13,000 versus $10,000 — but offers more room in the phase where most failures occur. The second is that the one-step plan has a tighter ratio of target to drawdown: 10R of target against 6R of room, or 1.67:1. The third observation is about the calendar. The one-step plan can finish in five days if the target is reached; the two-step plan needs at least ten trading days across both phases, plus verification. If the trader's edge produces the target in about ten sessions, the one-step plan is a better fit. If the edge needs twenty sessions, the one-step plan may run out of time or force larger size, while the two-step plan gives more room to be patient.

The fourth observation is about the daily budget. In the one-step plan, the daily loss is 3% and the maximum loss is 6%, so two full daily losses end the account if the firm treats a daily breach as fatal. In the two-step phase one, the daily loss is 5% and the maximum loss is 10%, so the same two-day arithmetic applies, but the room is larger in dollars. The ratio of daily budget to total room is the number that decides how much a bad day can cost, and it is often more important than the headline drawdown.

The drawdown question

The drawdown is the rule that decides whether a plan is forgiving or brutal, and the one-step versus two-step comparison often comes down to its type rather than its percentage.

Static versus trailing

A static maximum loss is measured from the starting balance and never moves. A trailing maximum loss follows profit, and an intraday trailing version follows unrealised profit. For the same headline percentage, the static version is the most forgiving, the end-of-day trailing version is next, and the intraday trailing version is the hardest. Before comparing the one-step and two-step plans, check which type each uses. A one-step plan with a static 6% floor can be more forgiving than a two-step plan with an intraday trailing 10% floor, even though the headline percentage is smaller.

Per-phase resets

In a two-step plan, the drawdown may reset at the start of phase two. If it does, a trader who finishes phase one with a profit starts phase two with a fresh floor, which is a meaningful advantage. If it does not reset, the phase-two floor is calculated from the phase-one result, and the room may be smaller than the headline suggests. The reset rule is one of the most important lines in the plan document, and it is often buried in a footnote.

How the floor interacts with the target

On a trailing plan, the floor rises as the target is approached. The middle of the challenge — profit accumulated, floor raised, target not yet reached — is the most dangerous phase, because the room is thinner than at the start while the remaining target is smaller. A one-step plan has a longer middle phase, which means more time in the danger zone. A two-step plan has two shorter middle phases, which can be safer for a trader who manages the transition well but riskier for one who treats the phase boundary as a fresh start and increases size.

The practical rule is to compare the distance from current equity to the floor at three points: the start, the midpoint and 80% of the target. If the distance at 80% is less than one daily budget, the plan requires a size reduction in the final phase. That reduction is not optional; it is the only way to finish without giving back the profit in a normal pullback.

The phase-two problem

The second phase of a two-step plan creates a specific set of risks that a one-step plan does not have. They are worth understanding before choosing the two-step structure.

The profit give-back

Phase one ends with a profit. Phase two starts with that profit in the account, which means the drawdown floor may be higher relative to the starting balance. A trader who treats phase two as a new challenge with a fresh $100,000 may be wrong about the room. The correct approach is to recalculate the floor from the actual balance at the start of phase two, not from the original account size.

The consistency reset

Some plans apply a consistency rule across the whole evaluation, others per phase. If the rule resets, a lopsided phase one may not affect the final payout; if it does not, the distribution has to be managed across both phases. The plan document should state which applies. If it does not, ask before starting phase two.

The pressure to press

Phase two is usually the last hurdle before funding, which creates psychological pressure to finish quickly. A trader who increases size in phase two to "get it done" is taking more risk at the moment the floor is closest. The correct response is to reduce size in phase two, not increase it, because the remaining target is smaller and the cost of a breach is the same.

The verification stage

Some two-step plans include a verification phase after the profit target is reached. Verification may require a minimum number of days, a minimum number of trades, or a consistency check. A trader who reaches the target and stops trading may not satisfy the verification condition, which delays funding. The verification rules belong in the plan, not in the fine print you read after the target.

Cost per attempt and expected cost

The fee is the most visible cost and the least informative. The number that matters is the expected cost of reaching a funded account, which includes the probability of passing and the cost of a failed attempt.

The expected-cost formula

Expected cost is the fee divided by the pass probability, plus any reset fees multiplied by the expected number of resets, plus any recurring platform costs. A $200 fee with a 50% pass rate has an expected cost of $400; a $300 fee with a 70% pass rate has an expected cost of about $429. The higher fee can be the cheaper route if the pass rate is high enough. The one-step plan often has a lower pass rate because the drawdown is tighter, which can offset a lower fee.

Reset policies

A reset policy changes the expected cost more than the fee does. A plan that offers a free or discounted reset after a breach has a lower expected cost than one that charges full price, even if the initial fee is higher. Check whether the reset applies to the same plan and account size, whether it expires, and whether it can be used more than once. A "free reset" that is only available within seven days is not the same as one available throughout the evaluation.

The cost of time

A two-step plan takes longer, which means more platform fees, more exposure to rule changes, and more opportunity cost. If the trader's capital could be used for a second attempt or a different account, the extra weeks have a cost that does not appear on the invoice. For a trader with a full-time job and a slow edge, the extra time may be irrelevant; for a trader scaling quickly, it can be the deciding factor.

Which trader fits which plan

The choice is not about which plan is objectively better. It is about which plan matches the trader's edge, temperament and calendar. Four profiles cover most cases.

The fast, high-win-rate trader

This trader takes many small wins and few large losses, and can reach a 10% target in a handful of sessions. The one-step plan fits, because the target is one campaign and the tighter drawdown is manageable with small position sizes. The risk is overconfidence: a fast start can encourage larger size, which is exactly what the tighter floor punishes.

The patient, lower-win-rate trader

This trader relies on a few large winners and can endure losing streaks. The two-step plan fits, because the wider phase-one drawdown absorbs the streaks and the phase-two target is smaller. The risk is calendar fatigue: two phases and a verification stage require discipline over a longer period, and the temptation to press in phase two is strong.

The part-time trader

This trader has limited screen time and cannot guarantee a fast finish. The two-step plan is usually the better fit, because the phase targets are smaller and the calendar is more forgiving, provided the phase deadlines are long enough. The one-step plan can work if the trader can dedicate a concentrated block of time, but a slow week inside a tight drawdown is dangerous.

The trader using a passing service

This is a different decision. The service is trading the evaluation, so the relevant question is which plan the service can execute with the most margin around the rules. A two-step plan with a wider phase-one drawdown may be easier to pass, but it also takes longer and exposes the account to more rule changes. A one-step plan is faster but leaves less room for error. The service should be able to explain its choice in terms of the rule map, not the fee.

Testing the choice before you buy

The best way to choose between the two structures is to test the decision on your own data before paying. Three exercises are enough for most traders.

Exercise one: replay your last fifty trades

Take your last fifty trades with their actual risk and result in R. Simulate the one-step target and drawdown, then the two-step target and drawdown. Count how many times each structure would have passed, failed, or run out of time. The result is a rough pass probability for each plan, based on your own behaviour rather than on marketing claims.

Exercise two: stress the losing streak

Find the longest losing streak in your record, then extend it by two trades. Ask whether the one-step drawdown would survive it and whether the two-step phase-one drawdown would. A plan that fails on your normal worst streak is not a plan you can execute, regardless of its target.

Exercise three: price the time

Write down how many sessions per week you can realistically trade. Divide the target in R by your average R per session to estimate the calendar. If the one-step plan requires more sessions than you have, the two-step plan is the only realistic option, even if its total profit requirement is higher.

The exercises are not a guarantee. They are a way to make the choice with numbers rather than with the plan names. A trader who does them will usually find that one plan is clearly better for their behaviour, and the answer is often not the plan they would have chosen from the marketing page.

Common mistakes when switching plans

Traders often move between one-step and two-step plans after a failure, and the move introduces new errors. These are the most common.

  1. Carrying the same position size. The new plan has different drawdown and daily limits. Recalculate the size from the new numbers, not from the old plan.
  2. Assuming the floor resets. In a two-step plan, the phase-two floor may be calculated from the phase-one result. Check the reset rule before starting phase two.
  3. Treating phase one as a warm-up. Phase one has its own target and its own failure conditions. A trader who uses it to "find rhythm" may run out of time or breach the drawdown.
  4. Increasing size in phase two. The remaining target is smaller, not larger. The correct response is to reduce size, not press.
  5. Ignoring the consistency rule. A rule that applies across both phases can make a lopsided phase one expensive. Plan the distribution from the first trade.
  6. Forgetting the verification stage. Reaching the target is not always the end. Check the verification conditions before you stop trading.
  7. Choosing on fee alone. The expected cost includes the pass rate and the reset policy. A cheaper plan with a lower pass rate can be the more expensive route.

The common thread is that a plan change is a rule change, and a rule change requires a new rule map. A trader who moves from one structure to another without rebuilding the map is carrying assumptions that no longer apply.

How to verify the current terms

Plan names persist; mechanics change. Before buying, verify the following for the specific plan and account size you are considering.

  1. The profit target for each phase, in percentage and dollars.
  2. The maximum loss type: static, end-of-day trailing or intraday trailing.
  3. The maximum loss in each phase and whether it resets.
  4. The daily loss, its measurement point and its reset time.
  5. The minimum trading days per phase and the definition of a trading day.
  6. Any time limit per phase.
  7. The consistency rule, when it applies, and how it is calculated.
  8. The profit split and the payout conditions after funding.
  9. The reset policy, including cost and eligibility.
  10. The conduct rules: news, weekends, hedging, EAs and third-party trading.

Get the answers from the plan document, not from a review or a video. If a term is ambiguous, ask support in writing and keep the reply. The effort is small compared with the cost of discovering a rule after a failure.

Frequently asked questions

Is FundedNext's one-step plan easier than the two-step plan?

Not necessarily. The one-step plan is faster but usually has less drawdown room relative to its target. The two-step plan requires more total profit but often gives more room in phase one, where most failures happen. Compare the target-to-drawdown ratio, not the plan name.

Does the drawdown reset in phase two?

Depends on the plan. Some plans recalculate the floor from the phase-one balance; others use a fixed reference. The reset rule is usually in the phase-two section of the plan document and is worth confirming in writing.

Which plan is better for a beginner?

A beginner usually benefits from the wider phase-one drawdown of a two-step plan, provided they can commit to the longer calendar. The one-step plan's tighter floor punishes the sizing errors beginners are most likely to make.

How many trading days does each plan require?

It varies by plan and account size, and the minimum is often per phase. Check the current plan document and confirm whether a day requires a closed trade. A fast pass can be ineligible if the minimum is not met.

Can I use the same strategy on both plans?

You can, but the position size should change because the drawdown and daily limits differ. A strategy that works on a 10% phase-one drawdown may be too large for a 6% one-step floor.

What happens if I fail phase one of a two-step plan?

The attempt usually ends and a reset or new evaluation is required. Check the reset policy, because some plans offer a discounted reset within a time window while others charge full price.

Is a two-step plan cheaper overall?

The fee can be lower or higher, but the expected cost depends on the pass rate and reset policy. A plan with a lower fee and a much lower pass rate can be the more expensive route.
