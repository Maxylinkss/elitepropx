What an FTMO Challenge actually costs

The number on FTMO's pricing page is a fee for an attempt, not the price of a funded account, and the gap between those two things is where most of the real cost sits. This page prices the fee against the capital it unlocks, lists what the fee does and does not include, and works out the all-in budget for a trader who expects to need more than one attempt.

Key takeaways

  • The fee buys an attempt, not a funded account; the price of a pass includes every attempt before it.
  • Cost per $1,000 of funded capital falls as the account size rises, which makes the 100k the efficient point.
  • Platform, data and commission costs sit outside the fee and are charged whether or not the account passes.
  • A retry policy changes the effective price more than a discount does.
  • Flat-fee passing services compete with the fee on total cost, not on list price.
  • Time is a cost: each failed attempt consumes weeks that cannot be bought back.

A fee for an option

An FTMO Challenge fee does not buy a funded account. It buys the right to attempt one under a defined rulebook, and the funded account exists only if the attempt succeeds. That distinction is not wordplay: it decides what the fee should be compared against, and it is why a $540 fee is not automatically cheaper than a $220 service that ends with a passed account.

The same structure appears in every other pay-to-attempt market. What is being priced is the evaluation, and the evaluation's value depends on the probability of passing it. A fee of $540 with a 40% success rate costs $1,350 per success in expectation; the same fee with a 70% success rate costs $771. The fee is fixed and the probability is not, so the effective price is a property of the trader as much as of the firm.

That is the frame to hold while reading any pricing page. The question is never "how much is the challenge" but "how much is a funded account, given how many attempts this method needs". The second question has an answer, and it is usually two to three times the first.

The fee against the capital

FTMO prices by account size, and the fee rises more slowly than the balance does. That is deliberate: the larger accounts are the ones the firm most wants funded, and the smaller ones carry fixed administrative cost that does not scale down.

What is stable is the relationship. The $10,000 account charges $10 for every $1,000 of capital, the $100,000 charges $5.40, and the ratio flattens from there. Buying a larger account is not just buying more capital; it is buying it at a lower unit price.

The last column is the one that decides which row to pick. A trader who expects to need two attempts pays $360 for a $25,000 account and $580 for a $50,000 one — close enough that the larger account is usually the better purchase, because a second attempt on the smaller size still ends with a smaller funded account. The break-even is not about the fee at all; it is about the capital the fee eventually unlocks.

What the fee excludes

Four costs sit outside the challenge fee and are charged whether or not the attempt succeeds. Ignoring them makes a cheap account look cheaper than it is.

The first is the trading platform and data feed. FTMO runs on MetaTrader 4, MetaTrader 5 and cTrader, all of which are free to download, but a data feed for some instruments and any third-party tooling are not. The second is commission, which on forex accounts is charged per lot round turn and is the dominant running cost for an intraday method. The third is currency conversion, because the fee and the payout may be denominated in different currencies and the card processor's rate is not the interbank rate. The fourth is tax treatment, which varies by residence and is outside the firm's control.

On a $100,000 account trading two lots a day, commission can exceed the challenge fee within a single month of funded trading. That does not make the fee unimportant; it makes the fee the entry ticket rather than the running cost. A trader budgeting for a challenge should budget for the month of trading after it, because that month is where the money actually moves.

None of these costs appear in the "from $X" figure on a pricing page, and all of them are real. The FTMO challenge guide sets out how the trading costs interact with the loss limits, which is the other place they matter.

Cost per $1,000 funded

Comparing accounts by fee alone is the standard error, because the fee is buying different amounts of capital. Divide the fee by the account size in thousands and the comparison becomes meaningful.

At $10 per $1,000, the $10,000 account is the most expensive per unit of capital by a wide margin. At $5.40, the $100,000 and $200,000 accounts are the cheapest. A trader choosing between a $25,000 account at two attempts and a $50,000 account at one is comparing $7.20 per $1,000 against $5.80, which is a 19% saving on the same capital, before counting the extra weeks the second attempt consumes.

The unit price also exposes promotions. A 20% discount on a small account still leaves it more expensive per $1,000 than a full-price large account, because the underlying curve is steeper than the discount. Discount codes are worth using, but they do not change which row of the table is efficient — they only change how far down the curve the trader can afford to buy.

There is a floor to this logic. A trader whose method cannot survive the loss limits on a $200,000 account is not served by buying one; the cheapest capital per dollar is worthless if the size it implies breaches the daily limit on the first bad session. Price efficiency and rule fit are separate questions, and both have to pass. The FTMO rule numbers set out the limits those sizes are measured against.

Retries and resets

The retry policy is worth more than any discount. FTMO's terms have historically included a free or discounted retry after a failed attempt that did not breach the loss limits, and a paid reset for attempts that did. Those two paths produce very different expected costs, and the difference is a matter of rules rather than of skill.

A free retry after a clean failure halves the cost of a two-attempt path. A paid reset does not, and a reset purchased at full price makes the second attempt as expensive as the first. Before paying, read which case applies to the way the account is likely to fail: an account that breaches a daily limit is in the paid branch, and an account that simply runs out of time or finishes short is in the other.

This is the reason a slow, low-variance approach is cheaper than it looks on a pricing page. It fails fewer times, and the failures it does produce are the kind that qualify for the cheap path back in. A high-variance method that breaches a limit is not only more likely to fail; each failure is more expensive to repeat. The pass rate data is the input that turns this into a number.

Free trials are the third path and the cheapest of all. FTMO offers a trial that replicates the evaluation's conditions without a fee, and using it once before paying converts an unknown probability into a measured one. A trader who fails the trial twice has learned something worth more than the fee they have not yet spent.

A three-attempt budget

A realistic budget for a trader expecting three attempts on a $100,000 account: $540 for the first, a discounted or free second depending on how the first failed, and a full $540 for the third if it is a paid reset. That is between $1,080 and $1,620 before commission, and it does not include the platform costs of the weeks spent trading each attempt.

Set that against the payout. A 90% profit split on a $100,000 account paying out $4,000 in the first cycle returns the budget in cash terms and leaves the account in place. The economics work — but only if the third attempt passes, and only if the payouts keep coming. A trader who spends $1,600 on attempts and then breaches a consistency rule at the first payout has spent the budget for nothing, which is the scenario the consistency arithmetic is designed to prevent.

Write the budget down before buying, with the maximum number of attempts stated. A trader who has decided in advance that this is a three-attempt project will trade the third attempt differently from one who is improvising, and the difference usually shows up as smaller size and fewer breaches rather than as better calls.

The flat-fee alternative

A challenge-passing service prices the outcome rather than the attempt. ElitePropX charges a flat $220 for any firm and any account size, with retries covered while the evaluation runs, so the fee does not multiply with attempts. Compared with a $1,080 three-attempt path on a $100,000 account, the flat fee is roughly a fifth of the spend and it is known in advance.

The comparison is not purely financial, and it should not be sold as if it were. A trader who wants the funded account and the payout stream without spending weeks on evaluation arithmetic is buying time and variance reduction as much as a cheaper path. A trader who wants to trade the evaluation themselves is buying a different product, and the challenge fee is the right price for that.

What the two options share is the part nobody prices: the funded account's rules still apply afterwards. A passing service delivers a passed evaluation; it does not remove the drawdown, consistency or payout conditions that govern the account from the first day of funding. Time as the second cost

Each attempt consumes a block of calendar time that cannot be recovered, and on an account with a payout cycle the time cost compounds. An attempt that takes four weeks and fails has cost a month of funded-account eligibility as well as the fee.

Price the time in the same units as the fee. If a funded $100,000 account is expected to produce $4,000 a month, a four-week attempt that fails has an opportunity cost of roughly that figure — which makes the fee the smaller of the two numbers by an order of magnitude. That is the argument for paying more for a higher pass probability, and it is also the argument for not rushing a first attempt with size the method cannot carry.

The practical consequence is that cost reduction should target attempts rather than prices. A trader who takes one extra week to prepare and passes on the first attempt spends less than one who pays a discounted fee and takes three. The fee is visible and the attempt count is not, which is exactly why the visible number gets optimised and the expensive one does not.

A one-page budget worksheet

Costs are easier to control when they are written as line items instead of remembered as a single fee. Six lines cover a realistic attempt budget, and filling them in takes ten minutes.

The last line is the one that changes decisions. A fee of $540 looks small next to four weeks in which a funded account could have paid out $4,000, and that comparison is the whole argument for paying more for a higher pass probability or for a flat fee that removes the attempt count.

Fill the worksheet before buying, and keep it for the next attempt. Two attempts' worth of honest line items is usually enough to show which cost is dominating, and it is rarely the one the trader expected. The cost of failed challenges works the same budget from the failure side.

Cutting cost without cutting size

Four levers lower the all-in cost without touching the account size. Use the trial first, so the first paid attempt is informed. Read the retry rules before failing rather than after. Trade the historically cheaper times of year, when firms run promotions on the larger sizes. And prepare the payout conditions in advance so the first request is not refused for a consistency reason that cost nothing to plan around.

The fifth lever is size selection, and it is the one to be careful with. Moving up an account size lowers the cost per $1,000 but raises the daily dollar loss limit in absolute terms while leaving the percentage unchanged, so the size that fits the method does not change. Buying a $200,000 account does not make a $25,000-sized method safer; it makes the same method carry twice the dollars per point. Choose the size the method can trade, then pick the cheapest route to it.

A trader who has already decided the account size and the attempt budget has done the arithmetic that matters. Everything else on the pricing page is a discount on a number that was never the real cost.

Currency, tax and payment method

Three charges sit between the advertised fee and the amount that leaves the trader's account, and together they can add a noticeable percentage to a modest fee. None of them appears in the headline price.

The first is currency. A fee quoted in euros and paid from a dollar account is converted at the processor's rate, which usually includes a spread of one to three percent over the interbank rate. On a $540 fee that is up to $16, and on a three-attempt budget it is real money for nothing. Paying from an account in the fee currency removes the charge entirely, and some cards add a separate foreign transaction fee on top.

The second is value added tax. Where a firm is registered for VAT and the trader is in a jurisdiction that applies it, the tax is added to the fee at checkout. That is a legal requirement rather than a firm's choice, and it is not refundable when an attempt fails. It can turn an advertised $540 into a materially larger number, and it is worth checking before comparing two firms whose headline prices look similar.

The third is the payment rail. Card payments are instant and often carry a small processing charge; bank transfers can be cheaper in percentage terms but slower and may attract correspondent fees at both ends; crypto is sometimes cheapest and sometimes not, depending on the network at the time. The choice matters most when the fee is being paid repeatedly, which is exactly the multi-attempt case.

Keep the payment method consistent across attempts. A firm that sees the same card and the same name across a sequence of attempts has a clean payment history, and a clean history makes the payout verification simpler, because the name and the rail are already established. The withdrawal mechanics are the mirror image of the same process.

How the fee compares across firms

Firms do not price the same product, so comparing headline fees directly is misleading. Three structures dominate, and each makes the cost land in a different place.

StructureWhat is chargedWho it suitsWhere the cost hides
One-off evaluation feeA single payment per attemptTraders who expect one or two attemptsRepeat attempts; retry pricing
Monthly subscriptionA recurring fee while the account is openTraders who pass quickly and trade longSlow attempts; months spent stalled
Flat outcome feeOne payment for a passed accountTraders who want the cost known in advanceNothing beyond the stated fee

The subscription structure is the one that most often surprises traders. A monthly fee that looks small against a one-off evaluation fee compounds for as long as the account is open, and an evaluation that takes three months can cost more than a larger single payment. The structure rewards speed and punishes hesitation, which is a reasonable design for a firm and a poor fit for a trader who trades part time.

The one-off structure is the opposite: it penalises repetition. A trader who needs three attempts pays three times, and the third payment arrives at the moment confidence is lowest. That is the case the flat-fee alternative is built for, and it is why the comparison should always be made on the expected path rather than on the first payment.

Within the one-off group, fees also scale differently with account size. Some firms price almost linearly, some discount the large sizes heavily, and some charge the same for two sizes that differ by $50,000. The cost per $1,000 column is the only way to compare them, and it should be computed for the size the method can actually trade rather than for the size with the best ratio.

What the account has to earn

The fee is repaid by the profit split, and the number of payouts needed to clear it is a simple division. Take a $540 fee on a $100,000 account with a 90% split and a first payout of $2,000. The trader's share is $1,800, so the fee is repaid in a third of one payout.

That framing is useful because it turns the fee into a threshold for the funded stage rather than a cost of entry. A trader who has spent $1,620 across three attempts needs $1,800 of their own split to be whole, which is one moderately good payout. The fee is not the risk; the risk is never reaching the funded stage at all, and that risk is a function of attempts rather than of price.

The division also exposes a trap in the small account sizes. A $10,000 account at a 90% split needs $1,200 of trading profit to repay a $1,080 three-attempt bill, and a $10,000 account producing $1,200 of profit is a 12% return on the account before any payout conditions are applied. The same bill on a $100,000 account is 1.2%. Small accounts are expensive not because the fee is large but because the capital that has to repay it is small.

That is the argument for concentrating on one larger account rather than spreading the same budget across several small ones. The fee per $1,000 funded falls, the profit needed to repay it falls as a percentage, and the contract limits leave room to trade the method rather than to scrape under them. Common questions

How much is the FTMO Challenge fee?

It scales with account size and currently sits around $100 for a $10,000 account and roughly $540 for a $100,000 one. Confirm the live figure on FTMO's pricing page, because fees and promotions change.

Is the challenge fee refunded if I pass?

The fee buys the attempt and is not returned on a pass. What the funded account provides is a profit split on trading profits, which is a separate stream from the evaluation fee.

Which account size is cheapest per dollar funded?

The larger sizes. Cost per $1,000 of capital falls from about $10 on a $10,000 account to about $5.40 on a $100,000 account and flattens after that.

What happens if I fail without breaching a limit?

FTMO's terms have historically offered a free or discounted retry in that case, which is materially cheaper than a paid reset after a breach. Read the current policy before paying for a second attempt.

What costs are not included in the fee?

Commission, platform and data subscriptions, currency conversion on the payment, and tax on any payout. Commission is the largest of these for an intraday method.

Is a passing service cheaper than the challenge fee?

Against a single attempt on a large account the two are comparable. Against a realistic multi-attempt path the flat $220 fee is far lower, because it does not multiply with attempts.

Do discounts change which account to buy?

No. A 20% discount on a small account still leaves it more expensive per $1,000 funded than a full-price large account. Discounts change what is affordable, not which row is efficient.
