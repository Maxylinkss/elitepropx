How to pass a Tradify challenge: the rulebook, the math and the plan

Tradify pairs a trailing threshold with a session stop and bills monthly, which makes it a rulebook with two independent failure points and a cost that grows with time. Payouts follow a count of qualifying days, so the calendar is part of the evaluation rather than an afterthought. This guide works through the constraints in the order they decide an attempt, with illustrative numbers for a $100,000 futures account. Figures are models, not quotations; confirm the live schedule before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Two rules end attempts: the trailing floor over weeks and the session stop within a day. They must be planned separately.
  • A trailing floor means the risk unit is a fraction of the buffer, recalculated at the start of every session.
  • The session stop caps the worst day, which makes the day — not the trade — the unit of discipline.
  • Payouts depend on qualifying days, so a fast target does not mean a fast payout.
  • Monthly billing prices patience, which changes the optimal pace compared with a one-off evaluation.
  • Consistency applies, so the shape of the profit matters as much as its total.

The two failure points in the Tradify rulebook

Most evaluations have one loss rule and several administrative ones. Tradify's published shape has two loss rules that operate on different clocks: a trailing threshold that follows the equity high across the attempt, and a session stop that ends a single trading day. The first is slow and structural. The second is fast and behavioural. A plan that addresses one and not the other is a plan with a blind spot.

The rest of the shape is a futures evaluation with a consistency test, an undefined minimum-day count, monthly billing and payouts after qualifying days, on Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader during US futures hours. Read together, those rows describe a product for a trader who can be at the screen during the liquid session, who can accept a moving floor, and who can pace an attempt across several weeks rather than sprinting one.

The order of planning follows from the clocks. The session stop sets the budget for today, so it is decided first, before the market opens. The trailing floor sets the budget for the attempt, so it is checked at the start of each day and may override the session budget when the buffer is thin. A trader who knows both numbers before the first trade has already avoided the two most common breaches.

One consequence of the two-loss-rule structure deserves stating plainly: the session stop makes the daily loss predictable, and the trailing floor makes the weekly loss cumulative. A trader can lose four days at the session limit and still be within the floor, or lose three days at the limit and breach it, depending on where the equity high sits. That is why the weekly total, not the daily budget, is the number to monitor.

Trailing floor arithmetic

Assume a $100,000 account with a $2,500 trailing threshold, a $5,000 target and a $1,500 session stop. On day one the failure point is $97,500. If the account reaches $102,000, the failure point moves to $99,500, and the buffer measured from the peak is still $2,500 while the buffer measured from the starting balance is $2,000. The account has earned $2,000 and become $500 less forgiving in absolute terms, which is the whole behaviour of a trail in one line.

The practical consequence is that the risk unit cannot be fixed at the start and forgotten. With $2,500 of room and a ten-loss tolerance the unit is $250. After the account reaches $102,000 the room from the peak is still $2,500, so the same $250 unit remains valid in relative terms — but the room from the starting balance is now $2,000, and a trader who thinks in starting-balance terms will overestimate the cushion by twenty percent. The calculation has to use the peak, not the start.

There is a second-order effect that catches experienced traders. On a trailing floor the account can be profitable and close to failure at the same time, because the floor chases the high. A trader who is up $2,000 and sees a $500 cushion may feel safe, since the balance is above the starting point. The rulebook does not care about feeling: the distance that matters is from the peak, and it is $2,500 minus the retracement.

The remedy is a written buffer with a floor of its own. Set the attempt to stop if the buffer falls below one third of the starting threshold, regardless of the target progress. That rule converts a slow bleed into a restart, which on a monthly product costs one month rather than the whole fee. Stopping early is a cost decision, not a defeat.

The session stop as a daily budget

The session stop is the more useful of the two rules because it acts every day. With a $1,500 limit and a $250 unit, the day absorbs six losses. That is the trade budget for the session, and it exists whether the setup list is full or empty. A trader who plans for three trades and sees nine valid setups has to choose three, because the budget governs activity rather than opportunity.

The basis of the limit is worth confirming. A session stop measured on equity counts floating losses as they occur, so a position $900 underwater has used 60% of the allowance before it is closed. A limit measured on realised results counts only closed trades, which permits a larger floating loss and a different behaviour on the last trade of the day. Neither is unusual; only one is your rulebook.

The reset time is the other half of the definition. Futures sessions roll at a fixed hour, and the session stop resets with the session. A trade opened before the roll and closed after it can be attributed to either day, and the attribution decides whether a loss is a warning or a breach. Put the roll time on the same card as the session hours, because they are different clocks that happen to be close together.

The most useful version of the rule is stricter than the firm's: stop after two losing trades or the session limit, whichever comes first. Two losses at the unit is $500, well inside the $1,500 limit, and it leaves room for a later session in the same day if the plan allows one. A trader who stops at the firm's limit is stopping at the point where the day is already expensive.

Sizing contracts for both limits at once

Sizing on this product is a three-way constraint: the buffer, the session stop, and the plan's position cap. The correct unit is the smallest of the three answers. Start with the buffer: $2,500 of room divided by ten losses gives $250. Then check the session: $1,500 divided by six losses gives $250, which agrees. Then convert to contracts using tick value and stop distance, and compare with the cap.

Conversion is mechanical. With a 16-tick stop on the Micro E-mini Nasdaq at $2 per tick, one contract risks $32, so a $250 unit buys seven contracts after rounding down. With a 10-tick stop on the Micro E-mini S&P at $1.25 per tick, one contract risks $12.50, so the same unit buys twenty contracts. The position count differs by nearly three times for the same dollar risk, which is why a trader who thinks in contracts rather than dollars mis-sizes the moment the instrument changes.

Round down and check the cap. A position of twenty micro contracts is often above a plan's stated maximum, and a size that breaches the cap is a rule violation regardless of the risk it represents. The cap is usually expressed in contracts per position or in total exposure, and it appears in the plan table rather than the marketing page. Read it before the first trade, not after the first warning.

The last input is the slippage buffer. Stops do not always fill at their level, particularly at the open and around releases. Budgeting the stop plus two ticks changes the contract count by ten to twenty percent, and that difference is the difference between a planned unit and a realised one. A trader who ignores slip is not risking the planned amount; they are risking something larger and calling it planned.

Qualifying days and the payout calendar

Payouts follow qualifying days, which means the funded phase has a minimum pace that the evaluation does not. A trader who reaches the target in three sessions and requests a payout immediately may discover that the payout requires a number of profitable days that has not yet accumulated. The calendar, not the target, becomes the binding constraint.

The planning consequence is that the funded phase should be paced, not sprinted. A daily target of a fraction of the unit, repeated across the required number of days, satisfies both the qualifying count and the consistency test at the same time. A single large day satisfies neither if the qualifying days are still missing, and it can also fail the consistency check even when the count is met.

Qualifying days are usually defined as days with a positive closed result above a threshold. That definition has two traps. A day that is positive by a few dollars may not qualify, and a day that is positive in the morning and flat by the close may qualify or not depending on whether the firm uses realised or peak profit. Ask which definition applies, and keep a daily record that matches it.

The payout cycle itself has a separate clock. Qualification days, review time and payment processing are sequential, so the total time from a profitable month to cleared funds is longer than any single step suggests. If the profit is needed for living expenses, that total is the number to plan around; if it is reinvested into more attempts, the cycle matters less than the split.

Consistency on a futures account

A consistency condition on a futures account is a distribution test, and futures distributions are lumpy. Index moves cluster, so a method that catches two good trends in a month can produce most of its profit in two sessions without any unusual risk-taking. That is exactly the shape a consistency rule is designed to flag, even when the trader did nothing wrong.

The test to run before buying is the share of profit from the best day. Take sixty sessions of your own results, total the profit and compute that share. If the best day contributes more than a third, the rule will bind and the plan needs to be built for distribution: fixed targets per session, a rule against holding for a larger move, and enough sessions to dilute the best result.

There is a tension here with the trailing floor. A trailing floor rewards locking profit and taking smaller wins, because giving back a gain also gives back room. The consistency rule rewards spreading profit across days. Both push in the same direction: a plan of small, closed, repeatable sessions is better than a plan of occasional large ones. That alignment is useful, and it is the reason a tapered unit works on this product.

Confirm when the test applies. During the evaluation, during funding, or only at the payout request are three different plans. A test at payout means the profit can be earned freely and checked afterwards, while a test during funding shapes every session. The answer belongs in the same document as the terms screenshot.

Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader

Tradovate is the lightest option and the fastest to learn. Its order entry is built around the DOM, positions are counted in contracts, and the browser interface means no installation. For a trader coming from MT5, the adjustment is mostly conceptual: stops rest at the exchange rather than on a broker server, and the position is a net exposure rather than a set of tickets.

TradingView is the analysis-first option. Charts, alerts and layouts carry over from research, and execution is available without leaving the workspace. The trade-off is that order handling is newer than the dedicated platforms, and a trader who depends on precise stop management in fast markets should test the behaviour before relying on it during an evaluation.

NinjaTrader is the programmable option. Strategies, indicators and add-ons are mature, and the platform records orders in enough detail to answer a review question months later. The risk is complexity: an automated method can generate activity that the rulebook's conduct clauses did not anticipate, and the consistency test will see the distribution even if the activity itself is permitted.

Whichever platform is chosen, the data feed is a separate decision and a separate cost. Depth-of-market data is not the same product as chart data, and the feed that supports a DOM is more expensive than the one that supports candles. Confirm what the plan includes, what the platform charges and what the method actually needs, because the three answers are rarely the same.

Which session to trade, and why it matters twice

The session matters twice on this product because both loss rules are session-based. The trailing floor accumulates across sessions, and the session stop resets with them. A method that trades the opening drive and a method that trades the closing hour produce different daily ranges, different numbers of trades and therefore different exposure to the session limit.

The opening block carries the widest range and the widest spread. A stop placed inside the first fifteen minutes is more likely to be hit by noise than by information, which increases the number of small losses and the speed at which the session budget is consumed. The midday block is quieter and cleaner, with fewer valid setups and a lower cost per mistake. The closing block carries rebalancing flow and settlement effects that are difficult to model.

The overnight session is thinner and should be treated as a different product. Spreads widen, stop fills degrade, and the loss is still attributed to a session. A trader who cannot monitor the overnight should not hold through it, and a trader who does should reduce the unit to reflect the wider spread and the greater gap risk.

The availability test is simple: mark the hours you can genuinely watch for four weeks and check whether the liquid block of your instrument falls inside them. If it does not, the attempt is being traded on hope. If it does, choose one block and build the method for that block rather than trading all three and calling it diversification.

A twenty-four-session attempt

Sessions one to four are measurement: half the unit, a full record of the buffer before every entry, and a note of the realised slip on each stop. The output is a table of how the account behaves, not a profit target. A trader who skips this block learns the mechanics with a full unit, which is the expensive way to learn them.

Sessions five to fourteen are the working block: full unit, a fixed setup list, a hard stop at two losses, and a recalculated buffer each morning. Halve the unit when the buffer falls below two thirds of the starting threshold. This is where most of the target is earned and where the trail begins to compress the room, so the recalculation is not optional.

Sessions fifteen to twenty are the distribution block. If a consistency test applies or qualifying days are required, this is where the shape is built: smaller targets, more sessions, no oversized day. A trader who is ahead of the target should be reducing size, because the marginal value of more profit is lower than the cost of losing the buffer.

Sessions twenty-one to twenty-four are the close. A third of the unit, the target protected, and the payout documentation prepared. Identity, payment method and statements take days, and starting them in the final week is the difference between a payout next cycle and one the cycle after.

Target maths and a tapered unit

With a $5,000 target, a $250 unit and a 2:1 payoff, the target is ten net winners of $500. Across twenty-four sessions that is less than half a winner per session, which is a modest requirement for a method with a real edge. Framed as a trade count rather than a dollar figure, the target stops inviting pressure and starts inviting repetition.

The taper is what keeps the account alive at the end. Use the full unit until forty percent of the target, two thirds until seventy percent, and one third afterwards. The reason is arithmetic: as the account earns, the trail lifts the failure point, so each remaining dollar is bought with less room. A constant unit means increasing relative risk, which is the definition of a slow breach.

The taper also serves the consistency test. Smaller positions in the final block produce smaller daily contributions, which flatten the distribution and reduce the share taken by the best day. A trader who tapers for the floor and for the consistency rule is doing one thing that satisfies two constraints, which is the mark of a plan that was designed rather than assembled.

Write the taper points in dollars alongside the buffer and the session limit. Three numbers and one rule fit on an index card, and the card is reviewed before the session rather than during it. That is the entire risk-management system this rulebook needs.

Streaks, stops and the effect on the trail

Assume a 40% hit rate and a 2:1 payoff. Expectancy is 0.40 × 2R − 0.60 × 1R, or 0.20R per trade. The probability of six consecutive losses is 0.60 to the sixth, about 4.7%. Across sixty trades the chance of seeing at least one such run is high enough that a plan which cannot survive six losses is a plan that fails in most attempts rather than in unlucky ones.

The session stop changes the arithmetic by interrupting runs. If the stop binds at six losses, a long run becomes a sequence of shorter ones across sessions, and the buffer is recomputed between them. That is a genuine benefit of a daily rule on a trailing product: it prevents a single bad day from becoming the entire drawdown, and it forces a review at a point where the trader can still think clearly.

The cost is that the stop also interrupts recovery. A trader stopped for the day cannot make the loss back in the same session, which is exactly the behaviour the rule is designed to prevent. The correct response is to accept the flat afternoon and review the setups, not to move to another instrument or a second account.

The streak calculation should be run on the trader's own record rather than on an assumption. Take the last hundred trades, compute the longest losing run and the average run, and size so that the longest run plus two consumes less than half the buffer. If that size is below the minimum position the instrument allows, the account is too small for the method, and no rulebook will fix that.

Fills, commissions and the true risk unit

Futures execution is fast and, in liquid contracts, tight. It is not exact. A stop order becomes a market order on trigger, and the fill depends on the book at that moment. At the open, two or three ticks of slip on a 12-tick stop is a 20% overrun; on a 6-tick stop it is 50%. The tighter the stop, the larger the share of the risk unit that execution consumes.

Commissions are per contract per side, so a round turn on eight micro contracts is sixteen commission units. A trader using micros to express a large dollar risk pays a commission bill proportional to the contract count rather than the exposure, which makes micros more expensive per unit of risk than minis at the same dollar size. Compute cost per unit of risk, not per trade.

The true unit is the planned stop in dollars, plus expected slip, plus commission. A $250 planned unit with four dollars of slip and three dollars of commission is a $257 unit, and six of those is the real daily burn. Budgeting from the planned number understates the drawdown by a few percent, which across sixty trades is a visible fraction of the buffer.

Order handling also has a rulebook dimension. Some plans restrict certain order types or activity around releases, and an automated method can breach those restrictions without intent. Keep the order log, read the activity clauses, and choose a platform that timestamps precisely. A review question about 09:31 is answered in seconds by a complete log and in days by a reconstruction.

Releases and restricted windows

Index futures react to a short list of dominant events: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment reports and, for some instruments, inventory data. The reaction is fast, the spread widens before the release, and the stop that looked safe at 09:25 is not the stop that fills at 09:30. A position held through a release has an unknown stop distance.

Restrictions vary by plan and by definition. A window of two minutes before and after is a different rule from a window of thirty minutes, and "no new positions" is different from "no holding". The clause is usually short and rarely on the marketing page, and it is enforced at review rather than in real time, which makes it a delayed failure with a full audit trail.

The conservative plan is to be flat into the release and re-enter after the first half hour. That costs some of the largest moves and removes an entire category of dispute. A trader who insists on holding should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the buffer can survive if it gaps, and should accept that the session stop may be consumed in one fill.

Unscheduled news cannot be planned for. The only protection is size: a unit small enough that a gap through the stop is a bad day rather than a lost attempt. That is the same argument as the streak calculation, applied to a single event with no warning.

The review and the withdrawal

Qualification is a second process: a number of qualifying days, a minimum balance, identity verification and a payment method that matches the fee. Each gate is administrative, each sits on the critical path, and each is easier to clear before the target than after. Start the paperwork in the distribution block, not in the final week.

The review reads the record. Statements show what happened; a journal shows why. The distribution of profit, the size relative to the rules, the number of sessions and any pattern resembling prohibited practice are visible in the statements alone. A pass built on one oversized day surfaces here even when nothing in the evaluation flagged it.

The payment method is the most common avoidable delay. Withdrawals usually return to the method used for the fee, so an expired card or a changed wallet triggers a manual review. Confirm the method at purchase, keep the receipt, and update it before the payout request rather than after a rejection.

Finally, compare cycles and splits together. A 90% split on a thirty-day cycle and an 80% split on a seven-day cycle are different products for a trader who needs the cash, and the same product for a trader who reinvests. That is a personal calculation, and it belongs before the fee rather than after the first payout.

The subscription as a deadline

At an illustrative $140 per month plus a $40 data fee, a two-month pass costs $360, a four-month pass $720 and a six-month pass $1,080. The evaluation fee does not change with duration; the subscription does. On a monthly product the calendar is a cost centre, and a cautious attempt spread across five months is more expensive than a disciplined one finished in two.

That does not argue for speed at any price. A trader who presses to finish early breaches the floor and pays for a retry, which costs more than the months saved. The correct pace is the fastest one that respects the buffer rule and the session limit: roughly six to eight weeks for a method with a normal hit rate and a sensible unit.

Retries compound the arithmetic. Two attempts of three months each cost six months of subscription plus two fees, which can be three times the headline price of a single attempt. Model the expected cost as monthly cost × expected months × expected attempts, and compare it with the expected payout over the same period. The number is usually higher than the fee page and lower than the trader fears.

The invisible costs complete the picture: platform fees, data fees, the time spent and the capital tied up in an account that pays nothing until qualification. An annual budget with four lines is the clearest way to see whether the exercise is a business or a hobby, and it takes ten minutes to write.

Six process failures to rule out

The first is planning for the session stop and forgetting the trail. A trader who respects the daily limit every day can still exhaust the buffer across a week, because the two rules measure different things. The second is planning for the trail and ignoring the session stop, which produces a single day that ends the attempt before the trail ever binds.

The third is a fixed unit on a moving floor, which quietly converts a ten-loss tolerance into a six-loss one as the account earns. The fourth is reaching the target and increasing size in the final week, the most common way a completed evaluation becomes a breach. The fifth is an oversized day that satisfies the target and fails the consistency check at payout. The sixth is starting the payout documentation after the target instead of before it.

All six are process errors, and all six are visible in a journal before they become expensive. A weekly review that compares the planned unit with the realised unit, the planned session count with the actual, and the buffer with the starting threshold catches each of them while the account is still open.

The remedy is a one-page plan with four numbers and one rule: buffer, unit, session limit, taper points, and stop for the week at a stated buffer. The page is reviewed before the session rather than after a loss, because a rule written after a loss is a justification rather than a constraint.

When this rulebook fits a method

This product suits a futures trader with a defined stop, a repeatable setup and availability during the liquid US session. The combination of a session stop and a trailing floor rewards precision and small, closed results, and it penalises improvisation and holding for a larger move. The monthly billing rewards a trader who can finish inside two months at a sensible size.

It suits less well a trader who needs a static floor, one whose method produces occasional large days, or one who cannot be present during the session. It also suits poorly a trader who needs a long sample before building confidence, because the monthly cost prices that caution. Those are mismatches rather than flaws: the same rulebook is a good fit for a different method.

The test is a simulation of the exact rules on your last hundred trades: apply the trail, the session stop and the buffer rule mechanically, and record the maximum drawdown and the number of sessions survived. If the simulation breaches, the real attempt will too, and the strategy is not the variable to change.

If it survives, the process is the edge: recalculate the buffer each morning, respect the session budget, build the target in small blocks, and treat the final week as paperwork. None of that is exciting, and all of it is repeatable, which is what a two-loss-rule product is built to reward.

Common questions

Does Tradify have a trailing or static floor?

The evaluation described here uses a trailing floor with a session stop. The failure point follows the equity high across the attempt, while the session stop caps the loss inside a single trading day.

How many trades can I take in one session?

The plan does not fix a trade count. The session stop and the per-trade risk decide how many losses the day can absorb — with an illustrative $1,500 limit and a $250 unit, six losses end the session.

When can I request a payout?

After the qualifying days are complete and the account is verified. Qualifying days are usually counted as days with a positive closed result above a threshold, so confirm the definition before planning the funded phase.

Which platforms does Tradify support?

Tradovate, TradingView and NinjaTrader are listed for the product compared here. Confirm the platform list for the exact plan, and check whether the data feed is included or billed separately.

Does the daily reset affect a position held overnight?

It can. A position opened before the session roll and closed after it may be counted in either session depending on the firm's definition, which changes whether a loss is a warning or a breach. Confirm the reset time and avoid holding across it if the rule is unclear.

Is monthly billing cheaper than a one-off evaluation?

It depends on duration. A one-month subscription is cheaper than most one-off evaluations; a four-month attempt is not. Since a correctly sized futures attempt usually finishes inside two months, the subscription is often the better product for a disciplined trader.
