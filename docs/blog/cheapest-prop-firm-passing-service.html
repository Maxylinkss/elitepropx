Cheapest Prop Firm Passing Service

Searching for the cheapest passing service is a fair question asked in the wrong unit. The number on the sales page is a sticker, not a price: what you actually pay is the service fee plus whatever the quote leaves out, multiplied by however many attempts it takes. This page sets out how to price a quote properly, the traps that make cheap services expensive, the costs that are genuinely safe to cut, and the cheapest honest route for four different kinds of trader.

Key takeaways

  • Compare total cost across expected attempts, never the headline fee.
  • The cheapest route is the one that survives the payout review. A service that skips the terms check is cheap only until the account is closed.
  • A per-attempt fee at $150 can cost more than a flat $220 with retries covered by the second attempt.
  • A profit split is a payment that arrives later and rarely appears in the quote. Treat it as a cost.
  • Cheap evaluation fees set by the firm and expensive reset fees are the most common trap, and the trap belongs to the firm rather than the service.
  • Safe to economise on: account size, plan phase count, and which market you trade. Unsafe: the terms check, written policies, account ownership and a traceable payment.
  • The cheapest option for a high-probability trader is usually self-trading, not any service.

Cheap is a total, not a sticker

A quote has four numbers that decide what you will actually pay, and only one of them is normally visible.

  1. The service fee itself. Flat per evaluation, per attempt, or a subscription.
  2. Whether the firm's evaluation fee is included. Many quotes leave it to you, which is fine as long as the comparison is done on the same basis.
  3. The retry policy. Covered, discounted, or charged in full. This is the number that multiplies everything else.
  4. Any share of funded profit. A profit split is not a fee at the point of sale, and it is collected from every payout afterwards.

Price two services on those four numbers and the ranking frequently inverts. A $150 per-attempt service with retries charged in full costs $300 by the second attempt; a $220 flat service with retries covered is still $220. The cheaper sticker is the more expensive product, and it only looks cheaper because the second attempt has not happened yet.

Basis of comparisonSticker-only viewTotal-cost view
Cost of a first-time pass$150 against $220 — cheaper wins$150 against $220 — cheaper still wins
Cost if a retry is needed$300 against $220 — the ranking reverses$300 plus reset against $220 — flat wins by $80 or more
Cost if the payout is refused on a terms breachNot priced at allThe whole account, plus the fee
Cost if the provider takes 20% of funded profitNot priced at all$200 per $1,000 of funded profit

The final row deserves emphasis because it is the cheapest-looking product in the market and often the most expensive. A service that charges nothing upfront and takes a share of funded profit is not free; it is a deferred fee that scales with your success. If you collect $5,000 in funded profit over a few months, a 20% share costs $1,000, which is more than four flat-fee evaluations. The structure is not automatically wrong, but it has to be priced honestly rather than ignored because it is not charged today.

The quote archetypes, priced

Most quotes in the market fall into a small number of shapes. Naming them makes the comparison concrete.

ArchetypeTypical structureTrue cost of a 2-attempt passWhere it goes wrong
Bare per-attemptLow fee, charged again on every attempt$150 × 2 = $300 plus resetsThe second attempt doubles the cost
Bundled single attemptMid fee, includes the firm's evaluation fee$250 if passed first timeRetry terms are usually unstated
Flat with retries coveredOne price, further attempts at no extra service fee$220 plus reset policyPoor value only for a first-time pass
Zero upfront, profit splitNo fee now, a share of funded profit later$0 now, $200 per $1,000 of profit laterCheapest-looking and often the most expensive
SubscriptionMonthly fee while the service works the account$90 × 2 months = $180Slow attempts accumulate months

Read the table as a function of your own pass probability rather than as a ranking. If you pass on the first attempt every time, the bare per-attempt archetype is genuinely the cheapest. If your historic pass rate is one in three, the bare per-attempt archetype is the most expensive product on the list, and it is not close. The subscription archetype sits in between and rewards speed, which makes it a reasonable choice for a trader whose method has a well-documented expectancy but a slow pace.

Ask for the retry policy in the same message as the price. Any provider that quotes a price without a retry policy is quoting half a product. The reply that matters is specific: how many further attempts, whether the firm's reset fee is included, over what period, and what would void the cover.

The cheap-evaluation trap

The most expensive mistake in this market is not an expensive service. It is a cheap evaluation at a firm whose reset fees are almost as large as the entry fee, bought by a trader who is likely to need resets.

Firm pricing shapeEntry feeReset feeCost of passing on attempt 3
Cheap entry, expensive reset$99$99$297
Higher entry, discounted reset$155$69$293
Higher entry, generous reset policy$155Included in some programmes$155–$310 depending on the plan

The three routes look similar in the third column, which is the point. The cheap entry fee buys nothing if the reset fee resets the clock at nearly the same price. Where the trap really bites is when a monthly data fee runs alongside the attempts: four months of subscription on top of three evaluation fees can add more than the fee difference the trader was shopping for.

A second version of the trap is a firm that charges nothing for the evaluation and takes a large share of the funded payout instead, sometimes with a minimum trading period before any withdrawal is possible. On paper the entry is free. In practice the cost is a share of everything you earn, collected after you have already spent the weeks. Read the funded terms before choosing the firm, because on this structure the funded terms are the price.

A third version is the cheapest-looking offer of all: a service, or a firm, that asks for payment through a channel that leaves no record. That is not a discount, it is the removal of your only remedy. Whatever the number quoted, the absence of a traceable payment converts the purchase into an unsecured one.

What you can safely economise on

There are real savings available, and they are all structural rather than promotional.

Smaller account sizeLower evaluation fee, and a lower per-dollar funded cost is lostLarger relative fee: $220 is 0.88% of a $25,000 account against 0.11% of $200,000
Single-phase planOne less phase to pay for and one less window to fail inInfrequent availability, and usually a higher target
Forex rather than futuresAvoids recurring data feesRules and dynamics differ from the futures market
Monthly billing when you are fastA quick pass can cost a fraction of a one-off feeIt punishes slowness, so it is a bet on your own pace
Trading a market whose session hours suit youFewer wasted sessions, fewer rule-adjacent errorsNarrower opportunity set
Waiting for the firm's own promotionsGenuine discounts on evaluation fees, offered periodicallyTiming is not in your control

Two of these deserve a caveat. Choosing a smaller account reduces the cash outlay but raises the fee as a share of capital, which means the flat fee is relatively more expensive there; if the goal is cost efficiency per dollar of funded capital, the larger account is better and the fee is not the constraint. And monthly billing is only cheaper if you are honest about your pace. Estimate the plan on your slowest realistic attempt, not your fastest remembered one.

What you must never economise on

Four things are outside the savings conversation, because the cost of them failing dwarfs any discount.

The terms check. One written confirmation that your firm permits the arrangement. If it is skipped and the payout is refused, the entire route was wasted and the loss is the account, not the fee.

Written policies. Fee, inclusions, retry terms, timeline and ownership, in writing, before payment. A verbal answer is not a policy, and the moment it matters there will be no record of it.

Account ownership. The evaluation and the funded account in your name, on your email, with payout details configured by you. Any provider that expects to keep the funded login has taken control of your money, whatever the discount on offer.

A traceable payment. A documented method with a merchant reference. This is the one lever that decides whether a dispute is possible at all.

A quote that is competitive on price and complete on all four is genuinely cheaper than a cheaper quote missing any of them. That is the entire argument of this page compressed into one line.

The cheapest route for four trader profiles

The cheapest route is not a property of the market; it is a property of your circumstances. Four common profiles produce four different answers.

The third profile is the one that most often overpays. A trader who has passed before and knows their own expectancy does not need a service for the mechanics, and the arithmetic of expected attempts rewards them for doing it directly. The second profile is the mirror image: a trader with limited hours and a documented inability to give the evaluation the attention it needs is buying something real, and the fee is usually cheaper than the alternative of failing twice.

Two different budgets: the firm and the service

Traders shopping for the cheapest route usually merge two purchases into one decision, which is where the confusion starts. The firm sets the evaluation fee, the reset fee, the data fee and the target. The service sets one price for its work. You are buying from both, and the cheaper option in one is not necessarily cheaper overall.

DecisionWho controls itWhere the savings areWhere the trap is
Which firm and planYouPromotions, plan choice, market type, account sizeA cheap entry fee with an expensive reset or a trailing floor
Whether to use a serviceYouSelf-trading, if your pass rate is highPaying a flat fee when one attempt would have passed
Service fee shapeThe providerA flat fee with retries coveredA per-attempt fee that doubles on failure
Terms complianceThe providerNothing — this is not a savings leverSkipping it, which risks the whole account

Separating the two budgets changes the shopping order. Choose the firm first, on its total cost and rulebook, because the service you can use depends on what that firm permits. Then decide whether the service is worth its fee against your own pass probability. Doing it the other way around — picking a service and then shopping for a firm it accepts — tends to produce an expensive firm choice driven by convenience.

A worked shopping exercise

Three quotes, a $50,000 forex evaluation, a 4% reset fee at the firm, and a trader whose honest pass probability is 40% — which means an expected 2.5 attempts.

Cost if you later collect $4,000 of funded profit$679$375$304 + $800 = $1,104

Three conclusions follow. Quote A, the cheapest sticker, is the most expensive route to funded status at a 40% pass rate. Quote C looks cheapest until the funded account earns anything, at which point the share overtakes both alternatives. Quote B is the most expensive sticker and the least expensive outcome, and it is the only one of the three whose total is known in advance.

Now change one input and watch the ranking move. Set the pass probability to 80% — one expected attempt plus a quarter. Quote A falls to $181 plus evaluation, making it the cheapest; Quote B stays at $220 and becomes the more expensive choice; Quote C still carries its profit share. The correct answer to "which is cheapest" is therefore a function of your record, which is why the question has no market-wide answer and why any provider claiming otherwise is selling a slogan.

How firms price the same evaluation differently

Identical nominal account sizes carry different fees at different firms, and the reasons are mostly structural. Knowing them stops you from mistaking a pricing model for a bargain.

ReasonEffect on the displayed feeWhat it means for you
Reset and retry pricingEntry fee set low to advertise, recovered on resetsPrice the firm on the entry plus one reset, not the entry alone
Plan generationOlder plans keep older fees while new plans launch higherCompare the plan you can actually buy today
Rule tightnessStricter rulebooks support lower feesA cheaper fee with a trailing floor shortens expected funded life
Promotions and affiliate codesTemporary reductions, sometimes scoped to a planReal money, but re-read the rules attached to the discounted plan
Market typeFutures plans often carry recurring data costs that forex does notAdd twelve months of data fees before comparing across markets
Payout cadenceLonger cycles reduce the firm's cash exposureLonger waits mean longer breach exposure for you

The tight-rules row is the one that gets missed. A firm can offer a genuinely lower fee and still be the more expensive choice, because a trailing drawdown or a payout-stage consistency rule shortens the funded account's expected life. Cost and rule quality are a single decision, which is why the fee shopping in this article always ends in the firm's terms rather than in its checkout page.

Two smaller effects are worth knowing. Promotions tied to a plan generation can carry different rules from the plan you scouted a month ago, so re-read the rulebook for the specific product in the cart. And codes that appear to come from a third party usually pay that party, which is not a problem in itself but does mean the discount is often smaller than the headline suggests.

If the fee is genuinely too much right now

For some traders the honest answer to this whole page is that the money is not available, and no arithmetic fixes that. The useful alternatives in that case are not cheaper services; they are different plans.

  • Trade a smaller evaluation from the same firm. The process is identical and the rules are usually similar, so the habits transfer when you move up in size later.
  • Take the demo route deliberately. A month of trading a simulated rulebook, with the same daily loss limit written down and enforced, builds the exact skill the evaluation tests. It is close to free and it is the only option that also improves your pass probability.
  • Save to a defined target rather than buying on impulse. Two and a half times the evaluation fee plus the service fee is the standard reserve; buying before you have it means funding a failure with money you cannot replace.
  • Use the firm's free or low-cost retry windows. Several firms periodically offer free or discounted resets. If you are mid-budget, waiting for one is a legitimate strategy rather than a compromise.

What none of those options justify is paying through a channel that leaves no record, or accepting a provider whose policy is unclear because the price is lower. The discount is never large enough to cover the removal of your only remedy.

The cost of a failed attempt beyond the fee

Shopping conversations focus on the fee because it is the only visible number. The rest of the cost of a failure is not on any price list, and it is frequently larger.

Cost of a failureShapeWhy it changes the shopping decision
The evaluation fee, unrecoveredUsually non-refundableIt is paid again, which is why the retry policy dominates
Reset feeOften close to the entry feeTurns one decision into two purchases
Data or platform subscriptionMonthly, continues between attemptsPunishes slow attempts regardless of pass or fail
Weeks of elapsed timeThree to twelve weeks per attemptDelays the funded account and any income from it
Sessions and preparation hoursFive to ten hours a weekThe largest invisible line, priced at whatever your hour is worth
Decision fatigueGrows with each attemptThe fourth attempt is usually bought on worse judgement than the first

The last row is the one that turns a cheap quote into an expensive cycle. By the third or fourth attempt, the purchase is no longer being evaluated on price; it is being made to stop the discomfort of having failed. That is exactly the moment when an unclear provider looks most appealing, and exactly when the retry policy that was never written down stops existing. Any cost comparison that ignores this pattern will underestimate the true cost of the cheapest-looking option.

Fee creep over twelve months

Evaluation prices are not static, and the trend in this market has generally been upward as targets and rulebooks have been tightened. That matters if you are planning more than one attempt across a year.

Three patterns are worth planning around. First, new plan generations often launch at a higher price than the plan they replace, while the older plan remains available for a period — so buying the outgoing plan can be genuinely cheaper if its rules suit you. Second, reset pricing tends to be adjusted more often than entry pricing, which means the fee you compare today may not be the reset you pay in three months. Third, promotions cluster around marketing moments rather than around your readiness, which makes waiting for a discount a timing bet rather than a saving you can rely on.

The practical response is not to hoard fees or to buy ahead of need. It is to keep the total cost of the current attempt written down somewhere, to re-check the firm's pricing page on the day you actually buy rather than on the day you scouted it, and to treat the retry policy as more important than the entry price precisely because the entry price is the number most likely to change before you use it.

When the cheapest option is doing it yourself

Self-trading is not merely the free version of a paid service; for some traders it is genuinely the lowest total cost, once time is priced honestly at zero only if your time actually is free.

It wins when your pass probability is high, when the hours are available, and when the evaluation teaches you something you need for the funded account. It loses when the hours are not available, when the firm's rules are unfamiliar, or when the same mistake keeps repeating across attempts — a pattern that costs a full evaluation fee each cycle.

The honest test is simple. Add up the evaluation fees you have paid in the last twelve months. If that number is above a few hundred dollars, the flat-fee comparison is not hypothetical: it is a description of what you have already spent. If it is below the price of a single service engagement, keep self-trading and spend the difference on data, tools or a smaller account. Either way, the answer comes from your own record rather than from the price list.

The decision, in order

If you take one sequence away from this page, take this one. It resolves the question in about ten minutes and it does not depend on any provider's marketing.

  1. Write down your pass probability from your own record. Attempts taken against attempts passed. Not the number you feel; the number you can count.
  2. Pull the firm's real prices. Entry fee, reset fee, any monthly data fee, and the payout fee. Add twelve months of any recurring charge, because that is the horizon over which you will either pass or stop.
  3. Compute your expected attempts as one divided by your pass probability, and price the firm on that basis rather than on a single attempt.
  4. Ask each service for its retry policy in writing, in the same message as the price. The answer is the product; the price is a sticker on it.
  5. Compute two totals per option — one pass and three attempts — then compare against the same two figures for self-trading.
  6. Check the terms clause before paying, and keep the confirmation with the checkout record and the written scope.

Every expensive mistake in this market comes from skipping step four or step six. The fee shopping is the easy part and it is nearly pointless without them, because the cheapest quote that skips a written retry policy or a terms check is not a cheaper product — it is an incomplete one whose remainder is collected later, usually at the worst possible moment.

Common questions

What is the cheapest passing service?

The one with the lowest total cost across the number of attempts you realistically expect, not the lowest quoted fee. Price the service fee, whether the firm's evaluation fee is included, the retry policy and any profit split, then compare both a one-attempt and a three-attempt total.

Is a cheap service automatically risky?

No, but cheap usually correlates with a narrower scope. The specific risk to look for is a service that does not verify whether the firm permits third-party trading, because that omission turns a small saving into a closed account and a refused payout.

Are retries included in the price?

Only if the written policy says so. Ask how many further attempts are covered, whether the firm's reset fee is absorbed, over what period, and what would void the cover. A price quoted without a retry policy is half a quote.

Is a no-upfront-fee, profit-share service cheaper?

Usually not, and it depends entirely on how much you earn. A 20% share of $5,000 of funded profit costs $1,000, which exceeds several flat-fee evaluations. It is a deferred fee rather than a saving, and it only looks free because it is not charged at the start.

Why is a cheap evaluation sometimes more expensive?

Because entry fee and reset fee are separate prices. A $99 entry with a $99 reset costs $297 across three attempts, which is close to a $155 entry with a discounted reset. Add any monthly data fee and the cheap entry can end up the most expensive route.

Should I choose the smallest account to save money?

It lowers the cash outlay but raises the fee as a share of capital: the same flat fee is 0.88% of a $25,000 account and 0.11% of a $200,000 one. Choose the smallest account you can trade well, not the smallest one available, and treat the relative cost as a separate question.

Can I save money by waiting for a promotion?

Yes — firms run periodic discounts on evaluation fees, and that is real money. The caveat is that promotions sometimes coincide with new plan generations, so re-read the rules for the plan you actually buy rather than assuming the terms you scouted last month still apply.

When is self-trading cheaper than any service?

When your pass probability is high and your hours are available. Above roughly a 70% chance of passing first time, the expected cost of a single self-traded evaluation beats any flat fee. Below that, the expected cost of repeated attempts usually exceeds it.
