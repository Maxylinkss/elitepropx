The EOD trailing minimum balance

BluSky states that there is one way to fail an evaluation: fall below the minimum balance. That balance is not a fixed line drawn under the starting amount on trailing plans. It starts at the account size minus the trail and then rises with your highest end-of-day balance.

On a $50,000 Propel account the minimum starts at $48,000, a $2,000 trail. If the account closes a day at $50,750, the new minimum becomes $48,750. If the next session gives some of it back and closes at $50,300, the minimum stays at $48,750 — it does not fall back with losses. The calculation is run at 6PM from the starting balance, which is the detail that makes the rule survivable and also the detail that makes it dangerous.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is that the rule is forgiving inside the day. Because the threshold is calculated end-of-day, an intraday excursion does not move it. A position that runs into profit and gives it back before the close has cost you money but not protection. On an intraday-trailing rulebook that same sequence would have raised the floor and compressed your buffer permanently mid-session. If your method tends to give back open profit, BluSky's evaluation is structurally gentler than a firm that trails tick by tick.

The second is that the rule is punishing at the close. A session that finishes at a new high hands you a higher floor for the rest of the attempt, and the compression is permanent. The account that could absorb a $2,000 drawdown on day one can absorb the same $2,000 on day twenty while sitting only a few hundred dollars above the level it started at. Nothing about your trading changed; the account changed underneath it.

The practical remedy is not clever, it is mechanical: risk is set from the distance between the current balance and the current floor at the start of each session, not from the original trail. On day one that distance and the trail are the same number. By the final week they are different numbers, sometimes by a factor of three, and a plan that uses the day-one unit in the final week is risking three times its own tolerance.

The static plans invert all of this. A 150K Propel Static holds a fixed $1,000 floor at $149,000 and it never moves, so a profitable attempt does not eat its own buffer. That sounds strictly better until you do the ratio: $1,000 of room against a $3,000 target is a 3:1 target-to-risk ratio, where the 100K trailing account offers $2,500 of room against $6,000. The static floor is easier to understand and harder to trade, and the plan that fits one is not the plan that fits the other.

The mistake this rule punishes most: banking a large week and then trading the next week at the size that produced it. On a trailing floor a good week raises the floor, so the same unit is now a larger share of the room. Size down after a strong close, not up.

The consistency cap, measured against the target

Most firms measure consistency as a share of total profit at the payout. BluSky does something different and stricter: your highest profit day becomes a percentage of your new target. The rule is published as a consistency rule with a stated cap, and the practical effect is that a single outsized day can push the target further away instead of finishing it.

The caps are 34% on Propel Premium and Static, 21% on the 300K plan, 50% on Launch evaluations, and none at all on Orbit. BluSky publishes the dollar figure beside each one, which removes the guesswork: $510 on the 25K, $1,020 on the 50K, $2,040 on the 100K, $4,200 on the 300K. Those are floors, not averages — an attempt that produces a $2,040 day on a 100K Propel still needs two more profitable days before the target is met.

This changes the shape of a good plan in a way that is easy to get backwards. Traders normally try to make the target in as few sessions as possible and then worry about the payout rules. Here the payout-style rule is active during the evaluation, so the plan's job is to spread profit across at least three sessions rather than to maximise any single one. A method whose edge arrives in one large day per week is being taxed twice: once by the cap, and once by the extra days the cap forces.

The monitoring is straightforward because BluSky's dashboard calculates the metric and shows the profit of your best day. Check it after every session rather than at the end. If the best day is approaching the cap, the correct response is to reduce the per-session target and add sessions — not to press for a larger win and not to stop trading, because stopping does not lower the best day, it only delays the target.

One consequence is worth stating for anyone comparing plans on price: the 300K floor plan at 21% is not simply a bigger 100K. It needs five profitable days rather than three, with a $4,200 daily ceiling, and the distribution work that implies is the real cost of the larger target complete with its larger payout ceiling.

No daily loss limit — and why that is not easier

BluSky's evaluation accounts carry no daily loss limit. The firm says there is only one way to fail, and it is the minimum balance. News trading, holding a larger position than the terms allow, and trading outside the recommended hours are not fail conditions either — the firm states it will liquidate or restart rather than fail the account. Hedging is the exception and is prohibited, with reward denial or closure as the consequence.

On paper that removes the single most common cause of failure at other firms. In practice it moves the risk somewhere less visible, and a plan has to put it back deliberately.

Without a daily stop, a session's damage is bounded only by the trailing floor. That means the natural behaviour — hold a losing position and wait for it to come back — is not punished until it is catastrophic. At a firm with a 3% daily limit the punishment arrives early and cheaply. Here it arrives once, at the floor, and it ends the attempt. The absence of a rule makes the account more dependent on a self-imposed one.

So the plan imposes a session budget that BluSky does not: a fixed dollar loss per day, set as a fraction of the room to the floor, and a hard stop when it is spent. On a 50K Propel with $2,000 of room, a $250 unit allows eight losses; on the 150K Static with $1,000 of room and a $3,000 target, the same logic produces a much smaller unit because the room is smaller. The number is not chosen from the target. It is chosen from the floor.

There is a second effect, and it is the one that catches disciplined traders. Because there is no daily limit, there is no automatic brake on a bad day, and bad days cluster. Three consecutive losses inside one session is not unusual for any method; at a firm with a daily stop it ends the day, and here it does not. The session budget exists to convert that cluster into a stop rather than into a fourth, larger position.

The static plans are the exception worth remembering. A 300K Static Growth carries a $2,500 daily loss limit alongside its fixed $5,000 floor and 21% consistency rule, and the smaller Static Growth accounts carry no daily limit but a 30% consistency cap. If you are choosing between BluSky products, the presence of the daily limit on the largest static plan is a real difference in the product rather than a rounding detail.

Sizing contracts from tick value

BluSky is a futures firm, and futures sizing is arithmetic, not judgement. A stop is a distance in ticks, a tick has a dollar value per contract, and the contract count is the quotient. Getting that order right is most of the risk control on this rulebook, because there is no daily limit to catch a mistake.

The last row is the useful one. On a $3,000 target with a $2,000 trail, a single ES contract with a 15-tick stop already risks 9% of the room. There is no sizing that makes a full-size contract conservative at this account size, which is why every worked example on this page uses micros. The contract sizing guide covers the arithmetic in more detail, including how to allow for slippage and commission before the contract count is fixed.

Two adjustments belong in the model before the first trade. Slippage: a stop becomes a market order when triggered, and in a fast tape the fill lands beyond the level, so a 15-tick stop on MNQ can cost closer to 18 ticks. Commission: charged per contract per side, so ten contracts is twenty units on a round turn, and on a small account that is a visible fraction of the target. Neither is large enough to change the plan, and together they are large enough to make the difference between eight losses of tolerance and six.

BluSky also states that it can automatically limit your maximum size if you are trading a single product. That is a safety net, not a strategy: relying on a cap you did not set means your size is decided by the firm's setting rather than by the room to your floor, and the two are not the same number.

One more sizing consequence comes from the trail itself. Because the floor rises with each new end-of-day high, the room shrinks in the profitable phase of the attempt — exactly when most traders feel most confident. Recalculate the unit each morning from the distance between balance and floor. On a Propel account that has climbed $1,500 above its start, the effective room is $500 plus whatever the floor has not yet absorbed, and a unit calibrated on day one is by then risking three or four times the tolerance the plan intended.

The plan a BluSky pass runs on

Six constraints write the plan, and none of them is a market view.

The trailing floor sets the dollar unit. Risk per trade is a fixed fraction of the room between balance and floor, recalculated at the start of every session. On a $2,000 trail that is $250 for eight losses. The unit never increases during an attempt.

The consistency cap sets the daily profit ceiling. With a 34% cap, no session is allowed to produce more than the published dollar figure: $1,020 on a 50K, $2,040 on a 100K. The cap is treated as a hard ceiling from the first session, not as a ratio checked at the end, so the rule is satisfied by construction and can never be the reason an attempt fails.

The minimum profitable days set the calendar. Three on Propel, two on Launch, five on the 300K. The target is divided across at least that many sessions, which means the pace per session is lower than the target implies and the schedule is longer than a one-phase evaluation suggests.

The absence of a daily limit sets a budget anyway. A session closes at a fixed dollar loss or at the daily profit ceiling, whichever arrives first. This is the constraint the firm does not impose and the plan must.

The conduct rules set the clock and the instruments. Permitted products, hedging prohibition and any holding restriction are treated as fixed walls. A hedging breach can lead to reward denial or closure, so the plan never opens opposing positions in correlated products, even to "lock" a gain.

The terms check gates everything. Nothing is traded until the third-party clause is confirmed in writing, and the check is free.

The order matters. A plan written from the target produces a unit that is too large for the floor; a plan written from the floor produces a calendar that respects both the cap and the missing daily limit. The second is slower and it is the only one that survives the final week, when the trail has eaten the room.

Failure handling is part of the plan rather than an afterthought. If an attempt is lost, the client is told the same day, the failure is classified as a floor breach or a conduct breach, and the retry runs at no additional service fee while the evaluation is open. The classification matters because the two failures have different causes: a floor breach is usually a sizing error in the profitable phase, while a conduct breach — a hedge, a restricted product — repeats on the next attempt unless the process changes.

The terms check, before anything is bought

Before payment we read BluSky's current terms for the specific product and confirm in writing whether the evaluation may be traded by a third party. This is the step that decides whether a pass produces a funded account or a voided one, and it costs nothing.

  1. Third-party trading on the evaluation. If the terms prohibit it, the job does not start. Accepting quietly would put the client's account at risk to protect a fee.
  2. Third-party trading after the pass. Some firms permit the evaluation to be traded externally and restrict the funded account. Since the funded account is the asset, the distinction changes the answer.
  3. Product and plan identity. Propel, Launch, Orbit, Static Growth and the two Instant Funding lines have different rules and different fees. The check is run on the exact plan and size, because a $1,000 static floor and a $2,000 trailing floor are not the same product.
  4. Conduct rules. Hedging, permitted instruments, maximum contracts and any holding restriction determine which sessions are usable and therefore how many weeks the attempt needs.
  5. Ownership. The evaluation is bought in the client's name with the client's payment details and identity verification. A provider holding the account is running a managed account, which most firms prohibit.

The check returns permitted, prohibited or silent, and silent is treated as prohibited unless the firm confirms in writing — because a firm that has not addressed the question can address it at the payout. Send the plan name, the account size and any support reply you already have, and the answer comes back before you spend anything.

How long a BluSky pass takes

BluSky does not impose a long minimum day count, so the calendar is set by the consistency cap and the target rather than by the firm. That makes a BluSky attempt faster than a staged two-phase evaluation and less forgiving of a week that produces nothing.

The consistency cap is the binding constraint. A Propel target cannot be completed in fewer than three profitable days whatever the pace, and the 300K needs five. Add flat sessions, small losses and the days the market offers no setup, and a realistic Propel attempt runs three to six weeks depending on the size.

The unit limits the pace. With risk at one-eighth of the room and a daily profit ceiling, a good week is a fraction of the target. Sizing up to shorten the calendar raises variance at exactly the point where the trail has reduced the room, which is why the plan extends the schedule instead.

Monthly billing makes the calendar a cost. Propel and Orbit bill monthly and the subscription ends when the evaluation is passed, so every extra week is a week of fees. Launch inverts that: nothing is charged until you pass, then a one-time Launch fee becomes due within 14 days, and missing the deadline loses the passed evaluation with no reinstatement. The fee timing changes which pace is rational, not just what the attempt costs.

Reporting is weekly at minimum. Progress is reported at agreed checkpoints and any failed attempt the day it happens. Silence during an evaluation is indistinguishable from a failure.

Week one — the floor is wide. Five sessions at $250 risk on MNQ with a 15-tick stop, a self-imposed session budget of $500 and a profit ceiling of $1,020. Two more green sessions at $500 and $400 take the balance to about $52,200 and the floor to $50,200. Six ways a BluSky attempt ends

Holding the opening size into the final week. The commonest failure and the most avoidable. The unit is right on day one and wrong by week three, because the trail has taken the room the unit was calibrated against.

A daytime that turns into a day. With no daily loss limit there is no brake on a losing session. Three losses inside one morning is ordinary; a fourth, larger position to recover them is how an account built over three weeks ends in an hour.

One oversized day breaching consistency. A $1,500 day on a 50K Propel is inside the target and outside the $1,020 cap. The target is then recalculated against the best day, and the attempt extends by at least a session it did not need.

Hedging across correlated products. Locking one position with another is prohibited at BluSky and can lead to reward denial or account closure, which is a worse outcome than a failed attempt because it puts the funded account in question too.

Missing the Launch fee deadline. On a Launch plan the pass is not the end of the process: the fee falls due within 14 days and a missed payment expires the account with no reinstatement.

Nothing — the pace is slow. A target not reached inside the subscription period costs the calendar and the monthly fee, and no breach. It is the cheapest way to not pass, and the plan prefers it to a breach.

After the pass: Buffer Zone and payouts

BluSky's pass does not hand you a live account with no target. It hands you a Buffer Zone, which the firm describes as deliberately gentler than the evaluation: a smaller target, the same or a similar drawdown, no daily loss limit, and the 34% consistency cap continuing. On Propel the Buffer targets are $1,500 on the 25K, $3,000 on the 50K, $3,500 on the 100K, $2,000 on the 150K, $3,000 on the 200K and $3,500 on the 300K. On Launch the 50K Buffer is $3,000 against a $2,000 trailing floor with a $1,020 best-day cap, the 100K is $3,500 against $2,500, and the 200K is $3,000 against a static $2,000.

Two things follow. First, the evaluation is not the last target — the buffer is a second, smaller one, and a plan that arrives at the buffer with the trail already tight enters it with the least room it will ever have. Second, the consistency cap survives the handover, so the discipline that got you through the evaluation is the discipline the payout depends on.

Once funded, BluSky advertises a 90/10 split with payouts Monday to Friday, subject to an eligible balance, completed identity and payment verification, and the account's consistency rules. Confirm the cycle in dollars rather than percentages: a 90% split is only better than an 80% split if the payout actually clears on the advertised rhythm, and the time from request to cleared funds is usually longer than either the split page or the fee page implies.

Four things to do in the first week after handover, in order. Change the password regardless of how access was provided. Confirm the account is in your name with your payment details and verification complete. Read the Buffer Zone and funded terms yourself rather than a summary. And write the funded plan — unit, session budget, daily profit ceiling, payout target — before the first trade rather than after the first loss. What this service will not do

It will not pass an account BluSky's terms prohibit someone else from trading. If the clause says no, the answer is no, and it is given before payment rather than after a funded account is questioned.

It will not remove the consistency cap, the trailing floor, or the minimum profitable days. Those are the firm's rules and they bind a service pass exactly as they bind anyone else. What the fee buys is the work of producing a completed evaluation inside them, not an exemption from them.

It will not promise a payout. The Buffer Zone has its own target and the funded stage has its own conditions; a pass is the end of one test and the start of another. Anyone selling a funded outcome is selling something the firm controls.

It will not trade an Instant Funding line as though it were an evaluation. Direct to Funded and Instant Sim Funded have daily loss limits, negative-day rules and no resets, and they are not passable products in the sense this page describes.
