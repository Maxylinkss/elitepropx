Choosing the model before the size

The most consequential decision on a FundedNext purchase is not the account size — it is the model, because the model determines how many targets have to be cleared, how long the account is exposed to the rules, and how much of the evaluation can be lost to a single bad session.

A staged evaluation splits the work into two phases, each with its own target and its own minimum day count. The advantage is a checkpoint: clearing the first phase is a real milestone, and the second phase starts with a reset drawdown and a smaller target. The disadvantage is duration — twice the calendar means roughly twice the number of sessions in which a conduct rule can be broken or a daily limit reached.

A one-phase evaluation asks for the whole target in a single arc. It is shorter, and it is advertised as easier. It is not easier in the way that matters: the target is larger relative to the starting balance, there is no checkpoint, and a single bad session consumes a larger share of the evaluation. Its real advantage is the shortened exposure, which matters for traders whose discipline degrades over a long calendar.

For a service pass, the choice has a second layer that a self-trading client does not face: the number of sessions is the number of opportunities to breach a conduct rule, and conduct breaches are the failures that cannot be traded around. That is the reason we generally recommend the staged model even where its total target is higher — more sessions at smaller risk each is easier to keep inside a rulebook than fewer sessions at larger risk each.

Account size comes second, and the reasoning is the opposite of what most clients expect. The percentage rules are broadly constant across tiers, so a larger account buys a larger payout ceiling and a larger fee, not an easier evaluation. The smallest size that produces a meaningful payout is almost always the right purchase, and the flat fee does not change with size — which removes the incentive a per-attempt provider has to steer clients toward the most expensive tier.

The terms check, per model

Before any payment, we read the current FundedNext terms for the specific program and confirm in writing whether the evaluation can be traded by a third party. This is not a formality; it is the step that decides whether a pass produces a payout or a voided account.

Four things are checked, and they are checked for the evaluation and for the funded account separately, because the two are governed by different clauses.

  1. Third-party trading on the evaluation. If it is prohibited, the job does not start. There is no version of this where accepting quietly is acceptable, because the loss lands on the funded account rather than on the service fee.
  2. Third-party trading on the funded account. Some firms permit the evaluation to be traded externally and prohibit it after a pass. That distinction matters because the funded account is the asset.
  3. Conduct rules. News windows, maximum position size, prohibited strategies, and whether positions may be held overnight or across a weekend. These decide which session windows are usable and therefore how long the pass takes.
  4. Account ownership requirements. The evaluation must be purchased in the client's name with the client's payment details, and identity verification must be completed by the client. Any arrangement where the provider holds the account is a managed account in disguise and is prohibited by most firms.

The check produces one of three answers: permitted, prohibited, or silent. Permitted means the job proceeds. Prohibited means we decline and say so before payment. Silent is treated as prohibited unless the firm confirms otherwise in writing, because a firm that has not addressed the question can address it at the payout.

What to send us: the program name, the account size and, if you already have it, the firm's support response on third-party trading. The check is free, and it happens before you buy anything.

What the trading plan has to respect

The evaluation is traded to the firm's rules, which means the plan is written from the rulebook rather than from a view on the market. Six constraints shape it.

The daily limit sets risk per trade. Risk is sized at a third of the daily limit at most, so that three consecutive losses leave the session survivable. The session stops at 80% of the budget spent, with the remaining fifth reserved for slippage on the closing trade.

The maximum limit sets the loss count. Total room divided by risk per trade gives the number of losing trades the account can absorb. If that number is under six, either the account is too small for the risk or the risk is too large for the account, and the plan is revised before trading starts.

The target sets the pace. The target is converted to a percentage of the account, divided by the sessions available, and checked against the daily limit. A plan whose required pace consumes more than a fifth of the daily budget is depending on the market rather than on execution, and the schedule is extended instead.

The consistency rule sets the daily profit cap. Where a best-day share applies, the cap is set at a fraction of the total that satisfies it by construction — so the ratio is never computed during the run because it cannot fail.

The minimum day count sets the calendar. Reaching the target early does not finish the evaluation if the day count is short, so the pace is planned backwards from the count. A small closed trade satisfies a qualifying day without adding meaningful risk.

The conduct rules set the clock and the instrument list. Restricted news windows and maximum position sizes are treated as fixed, not as guidelines. A plan that ignores them is a plan that will breach them eventually, and a conduct breach voids the evaluation regardless of profit.

The plan is not exotic and it is not optimised for speed. It is the same shape on every firm: risk sized to the daily limit, pace sized to the calendar, profit capped to satisfy the payout gate, and conduct rules treated as walls. What changes between firms is only the numbers, which is why the service can be priced flat.

Failure handling is part of the plan. If an attempt is lost, the client is told immediately rather than at the end of a reporting period, the failure is classified — drawdown or conduct — and the retry starts under the same rules at no additional service fee. A provider that cannot classify its own failures has not read the rulebook closely enough to trade it.

The timeline, honestly

A FundedNext evaluation is not completed in a week, and any provider promising that has not read the minimum day count. The realistic window is two to four weeks, and the drivers are worth stating precisely so the expectation is set correctly.

The minimum day count is usually the binding constraint, not the target. A plan that trades four sessions a week reaches most targets before it satisfies most day counts. The efficient approach lands both on the same date by pacing deliberately, which is why the plan trades smaller and more often rather than larger and less often.

The daily limit constrains the pace you can take. With risk at a third of the daily budget and a session stop at 80%, a good week produces a modest percentage rather than a large one. That is deliberate: the cost of a slow pass is calendar, and the cost of a fast one is variance.

Conduct windows remove sessions. A restricted release window can eliminate an hour of a session, and on a week with several scheduled releases the usable calendar is meaningfully shorter. The plan works around the windows rather than through them.

Reporting is weekly at minimum. Progress is reported at agreed checkpoints, and a failed attempt is reported the day it happens. Silence during an evaluation is indistinguishable from a failure, which is why the cadence is part of the product rather than a courtesy.

One practical note on the staged model: the handover between phases is a natural checkpoint for the client to see a real confirmation from the firm, and the second phase is traded with the same plan rather than with the first phase's aggression. Traders who carry a fast first phase into the second phase are carrying the wrong lesson: the first phase proved the plan works at that size, not that the size should now increase.

At the funded stage

The pass produces a funded account and a change of responsibility. The service covers the evaluation; the funded account belongs to the client and is governed by the firm's payout conditions, which are usually stricter than the evaluation.

Three conditions carry over and three are new. Carried over: the daily limit, the conduct rules, and the requirement to trade within the firm's permitted hours. New: a profit threshold that must be reached before any withdrawal, a minimum number of qualifying days, and a consistency share that caps how much of the total profit any single day may represent.

The consistency share is the one that catches clients who assume the hard part is over. Required total profit equals the largest day divided by the permitted share, so a large single session can multiply the requirement. The remedy is the same as during the evaluation: cap daily profit at a fraction of the threshold and the gate is satisfied by construction.

Four things to do in the first week after handover, in this order. Change the account password regardless of how access was provided. Confirm the account is in your name with your payment details and your identity verification complete. Read the funded terms yourself rather than relying on a summary. And write the funded plan — risk per trade, daily stop, daily profit cap, minimum day count — before the first trade, not after the first loss.

The payout process guide covers the withdrawal sequence and the delays that block first requests. The short version: complete verification early, request the first payout at the earliest permitted date rather than the largest amount available, and treat that first payment as a test of the process rather than as income.

What we will not do

Four refusals define the service as much as the fee does, and each one exists because the alternative transfers risk to the client without saying so.

We will not trade a firm whose terms prohibit third-party trading. Not quietly, not for a higher fee, and not with a disclaimer buried in a chat message. A funded account passed in breach of the terms can be voided at the payout, which turns a paid evaluation into a loss of the firm's fee and the service fee together.

We will not hold the funded account. The account is in the client's name, and access is returned or removed at handover. Continuing to trade someone else's funded account is a managed account in disguise and is prohibited by most firms.

We will not take a profit share. A provider paid a percentage of earnings has a reason to keep trading an account after the job is done, which extends the exposure and can lose the account in pursuit of a larger fee. A flat fee removes that incentive entirely.

Two further practices are worth stating because they are the industry's usual shortcuts. We do not buy evaluations on the client's behalf — the purchase is made in the client's name with the client's payment method, because ownership is the thing that keeps a funded account valid. And we do not ask for identity documents; verification is the account holder's step and cannot be delegated.

Everything above applies to any firm, and the FundedNext-specific part is only the terms check and the rulebook. That is the honest description of the product: a fixed-fee process applied to a ruleset that is read before the fee is taken.

If you are running more than one evaluation

Clients occasionally want several evaluations traded at once, and the answer is usually no — not because it is difficult, but because it changes the risk the client is carrying without changing what they receive.

The first problem is correlation. Two evaluations on the same firm, same instrument, same direction and same session are one position with two sets of fees. Firms that treat correlated positions across accounts as a single exposure may treat that as a breach, and firms that permit multiple accounts usually require them to be traded independently.

The second problem is conduct exposure. Every additional account is another set of news windows, size caps and holding restrictions to stay inside, and a breach on any one of them is a lost evaluation. Two accounts do not double the fee; they multiply the number of ways to fail.

The third problem is sequencing. A funded account produces income and a record. Two failed evaluations produce neither, and a client who funds three accounts in month one has three times the exposure and no payout history to show for it.

The recommendation is sequential, and it is the same advice we give self-trading clients: complete one pass, trade the funded account through two or three payouts, and only then consider a second account. The record from the first is worth more than the head start from the second.

Sizing the purchase to the payout you actually want

Account size is the last decision and the one most often made backwards. Clients usually choose the largest account they can afford, on the assumption that a bigger account is a better account. Under percentage-based rules that is only half true, and the other half costs money.

Because targets and drawdown limits scale with the balance, difficulty per dollar is roughly constant across tiers: a $200,000 account needs a larger dollar target than a $50,000 account and has proportionally more room. What does not scale is the cost of trading. Commissions, spreads and slippage are identical in dollars at every tier, so on a smaller account they consume a larger share of the target. The practical effect is that small accounts are marginally harder to pass at identical discipline — which is worth knowing before buying the cheapest tier in the belief that it is the easiest.

The other factor is the payout ceiling. A funded account's profit threshold and consistency share are expressed relative to the balance, so a larger account does not pay a larger percentage — it pays a larger amount for the same percentage. That is a real benefit, and it is the only one a larger tier genuinely buys.

Put those two facts together and the correct method is a target-payout calculation rather than a budget calculation. Decide the amount you want a payout cycle to produce, divide by the firm's payout percentage to get the profit the account must generate, then check whether that figure is reachable inside the firm's daily limit at a pace that consumes under a fifth of it per session. If the answer is yes at a small tier, buy the small tier. The extra fee for a larger account buys a bigger ceiling, not a bigger chance.

There is one exception, and it is honest to state: futures and Forex tiers are not always proportional in their fees, and firms sometimes price mid-tier accounts more keenly than the smallest ones. Where the fee curve is non-linear, the cheapest tier is not automatically the best value, and the comparison should be fee per unit of payout ceiling rather than fee alone. The flat service fee does not change with any of this, which removes one variable from a calculation that already has too many.

One further note on sequencing: whichever size is chosen, the first payout is not the place to maximise. A first withdrawal sized modestly is less likely to run into a consistency query and leaves a healthier account behind it, and once the payout process is understood the second cycle is easier to plan than the first. The client. The evaluation is purchased in the client's name with the client's payment method, identity verification is completed personally, and access is returned or removed at handover. Change the password after handover regardless of how access was provided.

A worked FundedNext pass, week by week

An illustration of the pace a compliant pass actually runs at, using a staged model with two phases, a smaller first target and a minimum day count that has to be respected across both phases.

Week one — phase one starts. Four sessions at 1.5% risk per trade against a 5% daily limit, with the session closed at 80% of the budget spent. Two small gains, one flat session, one small loss. Phase one progress around a third of the way to its target, with no day anywhere near the daily limit.

Week two — phase one completes. Three ordinary sessions reach the first target, and the remaining sessions of the week are used to satisfy the balance of the first phase's day count with single small closed trades. Phase one is confirmed by the firm, which is the client's first hard evidence of progress.

Week three — phase two begins. The drawdown resets against the new balance, so the account starts the second phase with a full buffer. The same plan is applied at the same risk, which is the decision most traders get wrong: the first phase proved the plan works at that size, not that the size should rise.

Week four — phase two completes. The second target is reached across four sessions, with the day count satisfied on the same date because the pace was planned backwards from the requirement. The best day across the whole evaluation is a fraction of the total profit, so the consistency share would clear comfortably if it were applied now.

The features worth noticing are the absence of a dramatic week, the deliberate use of small closed trades to satisfy day counts, and the refusal to increase size at the phase boundary. A pass built this way is slower than a pass built on one large session and it is considerably more likely to convert into a payout, because the consistency share is satisfied before it is ever tested.

The contrast is instructive. A trader who reaches the same target in five sessions with one dominant day has completed the evaluation and created a payout problem simultaneously.
