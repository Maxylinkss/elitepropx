Prop firm passing services: how the flat-fee model works

A passing service trades a prop firm evaluation on a client's behalf for a fixed fee. The model is simple to describe and easy to misrepresent, so this page sets out exactly what is delivered, what is excluded, how the pricing works, where the arrangement is legitimate, and the seven checks that separate a provider from a reseller with a Telegram channel. ElitePropX charges one flat $220 for any firm, any account size, with retries covered and no profit split.

Key takeaways

  • A passing service removes evaluation risk, not trading risk. The funded account is still governed by the firm's rules and still has to be traded.
  • The flat-fee model prices the evaluation, not the outcome: one fee, retries covered, no share of profit.
  • Third-party trading is prohibited by many firms. A provider that does not check the terms is putting the client's funded account at risk of being voided at payout.
  • Delivery is a sequence — terms check, fee, trading, pass, handover — and every step should be documented.
  • Percentage-of-profit and profit-split models create a different incentive: the provider is paid to keep trading your account.
  • Verify the provider's identity, the fee structure and the reset policy in writing before paying anything.

What the service actually does

A passing service takes responsibility for one thing: producing a completed evaluation that meets a firm's profit target without breaching its rules. The client buys the evaluation from the firm, supplies the account or the credentials as the provider requires, and receives a funded account that satisfies the firm's conditions.

The deliverable is narrow and worth being precise about. It is not a trading strategy, it is not a signal service, it is not a managed account, and it is not a guarantee of income. It is a completed test. Everything that happens after the pass — the funded account's profit threshold, its consistency rule, its payout schedule, its conduct restrictions — belongs to the client and to the firm.

That narrowness is the reason the model can be priced flat. A provider that trades evaluations repeatedly is running a process rather than a view: the target, the drawdown and the daily limit are known in advance, and the work is to reach a fixed objective inside fixed constraints. It is closer to project delivery than to trading advice, which is why a fixed fee is a coherent price and a percentage of future profit is not.

Three pricing models, and what each incentivises

The industry prices this work in three ways, and the differences matter more than the headline numbers because each model rewards different provider behaviour.

Flat fee per evaluation. One price for any firm, any account size, often with retries covered. The incentive is to pass efficiently, because the provider absorbs the cost of a retry. The trade-off is that the fee has to be high enough to cover the failure rate across all clients, so a flat fee is priced for the average rather than for one client's rulebook.

Percentage of the funded account or of the payout. The provider is paid a share of what the account earns. The incentive is aligned in theory and dangerous in practice: a provider paid a share of profit has a reason to keep trading the account after the pass, which extends the exposure and can lose the account in pursuit of a larger fee.

Per-attempt fee with a reset charge. The provider charges for each evaluation attempt. The incentive is the opposite of the flat model: every failure is billable, so the provider is paid more when the client needs more attempts. That is a structural conflict, and it is worth noticing even when the provider is honest.

Where the arrangement is legitimate

This is the part most providers skip, and it is the part that decides whether a funded account survives.

Proprietary trading firms are not uniform on third-party trading. Some permit it explicitly. Some prohibit it. Some permit it on the evaluation and prohibit it on the funded account, or the reverse. And some are silent, which in practice means the firm can decide at payout time that the account was not traded by the named holder.

The consequence of getting it wrong is asymmetric. A prohibited evaluation that is passed can produce a funded account that is voided at the first withdrawal, which is a worse outcome than a failed evaluation, because the client has paid the firm, paid the service, and earned nothing. A provider that does not check the terms before accepting the job is transferring that risk to the client without saying so.

Our rule: if the firm's terms forbid third-party trading, we tell the client before payment and decline the job. There is no version of this where quietly accepting is acceptable, because the loss lands on the funded account rather than on the fee.

Two related points belong here. First, the funded account must be in the client's name with payouts to the client — anything else turns a service into an unauthorised managed account, which most firms prohibit outright. Second, the client must complete identity verification personally; a provider cannot and should not do that on someone else's behalf.

Where the terms are ambiguous, the honest answer is to ask the firm in writing before buying the evaluation. A support response that permits third-party trading on the evaluation is worth having, and it is worth saving. The scam checklist covers the firms and providers where that question is never answered.

The delivery process, step by step

A provider should be able to describe the sequence in advance, in order, without hedging. Here is ours.

  1. Terms check. The client names the firm and account size. We read the current terms for third-party trading, on the evaluation and on the funded account, and confirm in writing that the job can proceed. If it cannot, the process stops here at no cost.
  2. Purchase. The client buys the evaluation directly from the firm, in their own name, with their own payment method. The fee for the evaluation is never routed through us — that keeps ownership unambiguous and keeps the client's identity where the firm expects it.
  3. Handover. The client supplies what the firm's model requires for the account to be traded. The scope of that access is agreed in writing before it is granted, and it is limited to the evaluation account.
  4. Trading. The evaluation is traded to the firm's target and within its rules. Progress is reported at agreed checkpoints, and the client is told immediately if an attempt fails rather than at the end of a reporting period.
  5. Pass and return. The evaluation is completed, credentials are returned or removed, and the client takes control of the funded account. The pass is documented with the firm's own confirmation, not a screenshot of a dashboard.

Two details in that sequence are worth emphasising because they are the usual points of failure in the industry. The first is step one: a provider that accepts any firm without checking is a provider that has not read the terms, which means the client is relying on luck at payout. The second is step five: credentials should be returned or removed at handover, and the client should change the password afterwards regardless. A service that keeps access after the pass is keeping access to a funded account.

The reporting cadence matters too. An evaluation can take one to three weeks depending on the firm's rules and the minimum day count. A provider that goes quiet for that period leaves the client unable to distinguish a slow pass from a failed attempt. Checkpoints — even a weekly line — are part of the product.

Seven checks before paying

These apply to any provider, including this one. A provider that cannot answer all seven in writing is not ready to be paid.

  1. Who is the provider? A named business, a verifiable contact address and a payment method that can be traced. An anonymous Telegram handle with a wallet address fails this check.
  2. Is the fee structure unambiguous? Flat, per attempt, or profit share — and what happens on a retry. "Contact for pricing" usually means the price depends on how much the provider thinks you will pay.
  3. Does the provider check the firm's terms? Ask directly: "Will you confirm in writing that my firm permits third-party trading before I pay?" A hesitation is the answer.
  4. Who owns the funded account? It must be in the client's name, with the client's identity and payment details on it. Anything else is a managed account in disguise.
  5. What is the reset policy? Whether retries are covered, how many, and whether the firm's reset charge is separate.
  6. What is the reporting cadence? Weekly updates at minimum, and immediate notice of a failure.
  7. What happens if the provider cannot complete the job? A refund policy for service failure — distinct from the firm's refund policy — stated before payment.

Service versus doing it yourself

The comparison is not "easy versus hard". It is a comparison of three costs: the fee, the calendar, and the probability of needing a second attempt.

Doing it yourself costs the firm's fee per attempt and whatever calendar the evaluation takes. It also costs attention, which is the input most traders under-price: an evaluation traded badly consumes the same weeks as one traded well, and a trader running three failed attempts has spent six weeks that could have produced a funded account.

A flat-fee service converts that into a known number. The client pays the firm's fee once, pays the service's fee once, and does not pay for retries while the evaluation runs. What the client gives up is control of the evaluation and the learning that comes from trading it — which is a real cost for a trader who wants to build the skill and a negligible one for a trader who wants the account.

There is a third option that is often overlooked: buy the evaluation yourself, trade it yourself, and use a passing service only after a failure. That hybrid keeps the learning on the first attempt and caps the downside after it. It is a reasonable strategy, and it is worth saying that the flat fee does not change based on how many attempts have already failed.

The risks, stated plainly

A page that lists only advantages is marketing. Five risks are real, and a client should weigh all of them.

Terms risk. The firm may prohibit third-party trading, or may change its terms between the pass and the payout. The first is checkable in advance; the second is not fully, which is why the funded account should be treated as a separate risk from the evaluation.

Provider risk. The fee is paid before the work is done. A provider that disappears, or that quietly stops reporting, leaves the client with a failed attempt and no recourse unless the payment method is traceable.

Concentration risk. The provider is trading several evaluations, possibly the same firm and instrument and direction. Firms that treat correlated positions across accounts as one exposure may treat that as a breach, which is another reason the terms check comes first.

Skill risk. A trader who passes by service has not practised the evaluation. The funded account still requires a plan, and the first month of a funded account is not the place to learn one. The complete passing guide covers the plan; it is worth reading even if someone else trades the test.

Expectation risk. The service produces a funded account, not income. A client who expects the fee to convert directly into withdrawals has misunderstood the product, and that misunderstanding is the industry's most common complaint.

Every one of those is manageable with the checks above, and none of them is eliminated by a cheap price. The correct question is not "which provider is cheapest" but "which provider will tell me, in writing, when the answer is no".

The arithmetic of using a service

Whether a flat fee is worth paying is a calculation, and it can be done with four numbers: the firm's fee, the service's fee, the expected number of attempts, and the value of the calendar.

Take a firm fee of $500, a service fee of $220, and a trader whose discipline suggests two attempts on their own. Doing it themselves costs about $1,000 in fees over six weeks. Using a service costs $500 plus $220 — $720 — over the same period, with retries covered while the evaluation runs. The saving is $280, and the calendar is equal.

Now take a trader who passes first time. Doing it themselves costs $500. Using a service costs $720. The service is $220 worse, and the trader has also given up the evaluation experience. That is the honest case against the model, and it is not small: for a disciplined trader with a suitable firm, doing it yourself is cheaper.

The break-even is a single failure. If there is any meaningful chance the first attempt fails, the flat fee is priced competitively against the expected cost — and the chance is meaningful for most traders, which is why the market exists. What the table cannot price is the value of the calendar and the attention, which for a trader with a full-time job is usually the deciding factor.

Two honest caveats. The service fee does not include the firm's reset charge where one is needed, and it does not cover the funded account. And the comparison assumes the service passes — a provider with a poor record is worse than doing it yourself at any price, which is why the checks above matter more than the arithmetic.

How to prepare before handing over an evaluation

Preparation is short and it materially improves the outcome, because the provider can only trade the account it is given.

  1. Choose the firm and the size deliberately. The cheapest account is usually the strictest rulebook. The smallest size that produces a meaningful payout is almost always the right first purchase.
  2. Confirm the terms in writing. Ask the firm whether third-party trading is permitted on the evaluation and on the funded account, and save the answer.
  3. Have a plan for the funded account before it arrives. Risk per trade, daily stop, daily profit cap, minimum day count. Writing it after the pass is how funded accounts are lost in week one.
  4. Complete verification immediately. Identity checks can be started before the first trade, and doing so removes the most common cause of a delayed first payout.
  5. Decide what happens after handover. Change the password, confirm the account is in your name, and check the funded terms yourself rather than relying on a summary.

Four of those five take under an hour in total. The fifth — a plan for the funded account — is the one that decides whether the fee produces income or a screenshotted pass.

Reading a provider's track record

Every provider claims a high pass rate. Almost none of them publish something that can be checked, and the difference between a claim and a record is the whole of the decision.

Ask what a pass looks like as evidence. The useful artefact is the firm's own confirmation that an evaluation was completed — an account-stage change, a dashboard state, or a funded login that the client controls. A screenshot of a profit number is not evidence of a pass; it is evidence of a profit number. The distinction matters because a trader can be up 9% on a 10% target and be nowhere near finished.

Ask for the sample size. A provider advertising a handful of passes is describing a small sample, and small samples are dominated by luck: a single good week can produce an impressive-looking record. Ask how many evaluations were attempted in the last quarter alongside how many passed, because the ratio that matters is pass rate, not pass count.

Ask about failures honestly. A provider that claims no failures is either new or lying. The informative question is not whether attempts fail but what happens when they do: how quickly the client is told, whether the retry is covered, and whether the failure was a drawdown breach or a conduct breach. A provider that cannot classify its own failures has not read the rules closely enough to trade them.

Ask which firms the record covers. A provider that only works with one or two firms is not necessarily worse — specialisation is a reasonable strategy — but the fee advertised as "any firm" should be tested. A rulebook the provider has never traded is a rulebook the provider will learn on a client's account.

Check whether the record is verifiable independently. The strongest version of a track record is a reviews page where clients can be contacted, or a public archive of completed evaluations. The weakest is a collage of green numbers with no dates and no account identifiers. Where the evidence sits between those two, ask what a client whose evaluation failed would say, and expect a specific answer rather than a deflection.

What a provider should ask you

Due diligence runs in both directions, and the questions a provider asks are a reliable signal of how carefully it works. A provider that takes payment without asking any of the following is trading blind.

Which firm, and at which account size? The rulebook is decided by the firm and the difficulty is decided by the size. A provider that does not ask cannot price the work accurately, which means it is pricing an average and absorbing the variance somewhere — usually by being slow or selective.

Has this evaluation been attempted before? A second attempt on an account the client has already traded is a different job from a clean purchase: the firm's reset terms apply, the account may carry a reduced balance, and the minimum day count restarts. Those are all visible in the terms and all relevant to the price.

Is the client's identity verification complete? Not because a provider needs it, but because an unverified account that is passed produces a funded account that cannot pay. Raising it before the work starts is the only point at which it is easy to fix.

What does the client intend to do with the funded account? A client who intends to trade it has to satisfy the funded conditions personally, and a provider that knows this can flag the ones that matter — a tight consistency share, an unusual minimum day count, a first-payout waiting period. A client who does not intend to trade it should be told plainly that the funded account is not income.

Does the client understand the firm's conduct rules? News windows, size caps and holding restrictions are unchanged by who trades the evaluation. If the client is planning to trade the funded account immediately, those rules are about to apply to them, and the first week after handover is a poor time to read them.

What is the client's refund expectation? The service fee and the firm's fee are different things with different policies, and the most common post-purchase dispute is a client who expects the service to refund an evaluation fee it never received. Setting that expectation before payment costs one paragraph and prevents the argument entirely.

The pattern across all six questions is that a careful provider is really asking whether the client's plan survives the funded stage. That is the part of the transaction the fee does not buy, and it is the part most likely to decide whether the money was well spent.

Frequently asked about passing services

Is using a passing service allowed?

It depends entirely on the firm's terms. Some permit third-party trading, many prohibit it, and some permit it on the evaluation while prohibiting it on the funded account. A provider should check and confirm in writing before taking payment, and decline the job where it is banned.

What does $220 cover?

One evaluation at any firm and any account size, traded to the firm's target within its rules, with retries covered while the evaluation is running and no profit split on the funded account. The firm's own evaluation and reset fees are paid by the client directly to the firm.

Do you take a share of the funded account's profit?

No. The funded account and its payouts belong to the client. A profit share creates a reason to keep trading someone else's account after the job is done, which is a conflict of interest we choose not to have.

What happens if the evaluation fails?

The attempt is retried at no additional service fee while the evaluation is running. The firm's own reset charge, where it applies, remains the client's cost, and the client is told about the failure immediately rather than at the end of a reporting period.

Who owns the funded account?

The client, always. The evaluation is purchased in the client's name with the client's payment method, identity verification is completed personally, and credentials are returned or removed at handover. Anything else is a managed account in disguise and is prohibited by most firms.

Do I need to give you my trading account password?

The mechanics depend on the firm's platform, and the scope of any access is agreed in writing before it is granted and limited to the evaluation account. After handover, change the password regardless of how access was provided.

How long does it take?

Typically one to three weeks, driven mainly by the firm's minimum day count and target rather than by trading speed. A provider that promises a fixed number of days without knowing the firm's day count has not read the rulebook.

Will I get updates while it runs?

At agreed checkpoints, at minimum weekly, plus immediate notice if an attempt fails. Silence during an evaluation is indistinguishable from a failure, so a reporting cadence is part of the product.

What if my firm changes its terms mid-evaluation?

The evaluation is traded to the terms in force when it started, and any material change is raised with the client immediately. Firms that change payout conditions retroactively are a risk that no service can remove, which is why the review process matters.

Can the service also trade the funded account?

It should not. The funded account belongs to the client and is governed by the firm's rules; keeping a provider on it converts a completed job into an ongoing arrangement that most firms prohibit and that puts the payout at risk.

Is a passing service cheaper than doing it myself?

Only if you would have needed more than one attempt. At a $500 firm fee, one attempt costs $500 and a service costs $720, so the service is worse if you pass first time. From the second attempt onward the arithmetic reverses.

What should I check before paying any provider?

A named business, a clear fee structure, a written terms check, client ownership of the funded account, a reset policy, a reporting cadence and a refund policy for service failure. A provider that cannot answer all seven in writing is not ready to be paid.
