The most important column on this table is drawdown type. An intraday trailing threshold responds to unrealised profit, which means a retracement can end an account while the day is still net positive. End-of-day trailing responds only to closed profit, and static—rare in futures—does not move at all. Two firms with the same dollar target and the same threshold can therefore be very different assignments.

Topstep's daily loss limit is optional at checkout, so a Combine bought without it has only the trailing maximum loss limit. That is a different product from the one implied by a column that simply says "daily limit: yes", which is why the qualifier is written into the row.

Converting dollar rules into percentages

Futures rules are published in dollars, forex rules in percentages. To compare them, divide by the account size. A $3,000 target on a $50,000 futures account is a 6% target; a $2,500 maximum loss on the same account is a 5% threshold. Do that conversion before comparing a futures evaluation to a forex one, because a "$3,000 target" sounds small next to "10%" and is not necessarily easier.

The five rules that decide outcomes, ranked by damage

  1. Daily loss limit measured on equity. Floating losses count, so a position that is briefly deep in the red can end the day before it recovers. This is the most common cause of a failed evaluation across every firm on this page.
  2. Intraday trailing drawdown. The floor moves up with unrealised gains. Traders who bank a large early profit and then give back a normal retracement breach the threshold without ever taking a losing trade.
  3. Consistency at payout. Not fatal to the evaluation, fatal to the withdrawal. A 30% best-day rule requires total profit to be at least 3.3× your largest day.
  4. End-of-day trailing drawdown. Gentler than intraday trailing, still moving. The buffer after a strong day is smaller than the balance suggests.
  5. Minimum trading days. Rarely ends an account, frequently delays funding. An evaluation completed in three sessions against a ten-day minimum is not complete.

Practical rule: read the daily basis and trailing columns before the target column. The target tells you where the finish line is; the other two tell you whether you reach it.

Reading a rulebook without being misled

Three habits prevent almost every avoidable breach.

Translate percentages into currency before trading. A 5% daily limit on a $200,000 account is $10,000 a day; on a $25,000 account it is $1,250. The second number is small enough that two average losing trades can end the day, and traders who think in percentages rather than dollars over-size on small accounts without noticing.

Write down the failure level as a price, not a percentage. On a $100,000 account with a 10% maximum loss, the failure level is not "$90,000" in the abstract — it is a specific equity number that should be visible on screen. Trailing floors move, so that number has to be recalculated after every strong session rather than set once.

Check whether the firm enforces rules it does not publish clearly. News windows, weekend holding policies and instrument restrictions are frequently enforced by the risk desk on a definition that is stricter than the marketing page suggests. When a rule is ambiguous, assume the stricter reading; that is the assumption the service trades on.

Read the payout rules at the same time as the evaluation rules. The two are usually on separate pages, and the second set contains the constraints that decide whether a passed account actually produces money. Minimum trading days, profit thresholds, consistency windows and KYC requirements are all part of the same agreement, and a plan built only from evaluation numbers tends to arrive at the funded phase unprepared. Copy both sets of rules into one document before starting.

Check what a reset actually costs. Firms differ sharply on this, and it changes the expected cost of an evaluation substantially. Some issue a free reset within a time window, some charge a reduced reset fee, and some require a full new challenge purchase. Where a firm charges a full fee for a reset, the practical cost of an evaluation is much higher than its headline price, and that should be part of the comparison rather than a discovery after a breach.

Confirm the rules for the plan you are actually buying. Firms increasingly run several products under one brand — a standard two-phase evaluation, a single-phase version, a trailing-drawdown variant, and an instant funding account. The rules differ between them, sometimes significantly, and a comparison table that lists a firm under one structure can be quietly wrong for the plan a trader selects at checkout. Screenshot the rules shown at the point of purchase and keep them with your notes.

Rule by rule: what each column actually means

Profit target

The return required to pass, expressed as a percentage on forex evaluations and as a fixed dollar figure on futures ones. Phase one targets are usually larger than phase two. Two subtleties matter: some firms calculate the target on the starting balance while others use the current balance at the start of a phase, and target drawings must usually be closed trades — floating profit does not count toward completion until it is realised.

Worked example on a $100,000 account with a 10% target: $10,000 is required, and a 5% daily limit means $5,000 is the most that can be lost in a day. If your average winning trade is $600 and your average loss is $400, you need roughly seventeen more winners than losers to finish — assuming you never breach the daily budget, which is the constraint that decides the whole evaluation.

Daily loss limit

The maximum loss allowed in a single trading day, resetting each morning. The basis determines strictness. Measured on equity including floating positions, an open trade that is $3,000 down consumes $3,000 of the budget even if it recovers before the close. Measured on closed balance, only realised losses count. The difference roughly doubles the discipline required, and it is invisible in a headline comparison.

Worked example: a $50,000 account with a 5% daily limit measured on equity. The budget is $2,500. With a two-position maximum and $1,200 of risk each, two losses end the day. With a single position at $800 of risk, three consecutive losses cost $2,400 and the day survives. The same account, the same limit, two very different outcomes — decided entirely by sizing.

Maximum loss and trailing behaviour

The total drawdown permitted before the account fails. Static means the failure level never moves: on a $100,000 account with a 10% limit, it stays at $90,000 forever. End-of-day trailing means the level rises with closed profit at the end of each session: bank $4,000 and the floor becomes $94,000. Intraday trailing means it follows unrealised equity: a position that is $4,000 in profit and then retraces to $1,000 has already moved the floor to $94,000, leaving the account $3,000 above failure with only $1,000 of realised profit on the books.

That last case is why intraday trailing thresholds produce failures that look inexplicable to traders reading their own P&L. No losing trade was taken. The floor simply outran the balance.

Minimum trading days

The number of distinct days on which trades must be placed before an evaluation can be verified. Firms define a qualifying day differently: some require a closed position, others any order fill, and a few require a minimum holding period. Reaching the target on day three of a ten-day requirement does not complete the evaluation, and the additional seven days are not free — they are seven more chances to breach a limit with an account already at target.

Consistency rule

A cap on how much of your total profit may come from a single day, usually expressed as a maximum best-day percentage. The common shape is 30%: if your largest day is $3,000, total profit must be at least $10,000. The rule is enforced at payout by most firms and during the evaluation by a few, and it is the most common reason an otherwise successful account cannot withdraw.

The arithmetic is simple and unforgiving. A $6,000 best day under a 30% rule demands $20,000 of total profit — meaning another $14,000 of ordinary days before a payout is allowed. Traders who finish an evaluation with one exceptional session are effectively at 30% of the way through the funded phase, not at the finish line.

Time limits

An overall window in which the evaluation must be completed, or none. Futures firms are more likely to impose one — thirty days is common on intraday evaluations — while most forex firms operate without an overall deadline. A time limit changes the risk plan substantially, because it caps the number of sessions available and therefore raises the required profit per session.

Three worked evaluations

FTMO $100,000, static floor

Target $10,000 on phase one, then $5,000 on phase two. Daily limit $5,000 measured on equity. Maximum loss static at $90,000, minimum four trading days, no time limit. Risk plan: $1,600 maximum per trade, three positions maximum, hard stop for the day at $4,500 of realised loss. At an average win of $2,200 and an average loss of $1,300, the target requires roughly eleven more winners than losers — a quantity that fits comfortably inside sixteen to twenty sessions, which is why this structure passes reliably.

Apex $150,000, intraday trailing floor

Target is a fixed dollar figure, the failure threshold trails unrealised equity, and consistency is enforced at payout. The critical adaptation is that risk must decrease as profit accumulates: at 40% of target the plan runs full size, at 70% it reduces by a quarter, and beyond 85% it uses half size. A trailing floor means an untouched pass is worth more than an extra few hundred dollars of progress, and evaluations at this firm are completed at a slower pace than a static account of the same size for exactly that reason.

Topstep $100,000, trailing floor plus optional daily limit

The maximum loss limit trails end-of-day at $3,000 on a $100,000 Combine. A daily loss limit of $2,000 is optional at checkout and is not itself a rule breach if it fires. The consistency target requires the best day to stay at or below 55% of the profit target during the Combine, while 40% applies at payout. The plan therefore spreads profit across several ordinary sessions rather than one dominant day, and treats the optional daily limit as a budget that protects the trailing floor.

What all three have in common: none of them is passed by finding exceptional trades. Each is passed by sizing against the failure condition and letting the target arrive over a realistic number of sessions.

Static, end-of-day trailing or intraday trailing: how to choose

The same equity curve passes under one model and fails under another, so the drawdown type is the first decision rather than a detail. Three questions resolve it.

How much room do you need to be wrong?

A static floor preserves the maximum buffer for the whole evaluation. On a $100,000 account with a 10% limit, the failure level stays at $90,000 whether you are at $100,000 or $115,000. Every dollar of profit is genuine progress with no side effect. End-of-day trailing reduces the buffer progressively, and intraday trailing reduces it fastest of all. If your style involves meaningful drawdowns — holding through retracements, wider stops, fewer larger positions — a static floor is close to a requirement, and choosing a trailing structure means adopting a different risk profile as well as a different firm.

How quickly do you need to finish?

Trailing structures force risk reduction as profit accumulates, which lengthens the final phase of an evaluation. The effect is not marginal: the last 20% of a target on an intraday trailing account typically takes as long as the first 50% did, because size has been cut to protect the moving floor. Where a firm imposes a thirty-day window as well as a trailing threshold, those two rules compound, and the evaluation becomes a scheduling problem as much as a trading one.

What are you planning to trade afterwards?

Funded accounts usually convert the drawdown to a static level at the starting balance, so the trailing behaviour that defined the evaluation disappears once you are funded. That argues for not over-fitting your choice to the evaluation: pick the structure you can pass reliably, then trade the funded account the way you intend to trade it for years. The exception is a firm whose funded terms also trail or whose payout rules are unusually strict — in which case the funded rulebook, not the evaluation rulebook, should drive the decision.

A simple preference order

For a rules-based approach, the order of preference is: static maximum loss with no consistency rule; static maximum loss with payout-stage consistency; end-of-day trailing; intraday trailing. Two caveats keep this honest. First, a firm's payout reliability can outweigh a worse drawdown structure, because an account that pays slowly is worth less than an account with a moving floor that pays on time. Second, a firm whose terms clearly prohibit third-party trading sits outside this ordering entirely — that is a compliance question, not a risk-model question.

Rules that are enforced but rarely published clearly

Four constraints cause a disproportionate share of resets because they are enforced by a firm's risk desk rather than explained on a comparison page.

  • News windows. Many firms restrict trading around high-impact releases, and the window is defined differently from firm to firm — often two minutes either side, sometimes five, occasionally extended around central bank decisions. Positions opened legitimately before the window can still breach if the rule requires them to be closed.
  • Weekend and overnight holding. Some firms prohibit holding through the weekend, others allow it on reduced leverage, and a few charge a fee. An evaluation that is comfortably at target on Friday afternoon can fail on Monday morning if the rule was missed.
  • Instrument restrictions and lot caps. Permitted instrument lists are usually published, but maximum lot or contract sizes per trade are sometimes buried in the terms. Trading the permitted instrument at twice the permitted size is a breach, and it is one of the most common accidental ones.
  • Banned strategies. Latency exploitation, tick scalping on restricted firms, group hedging across accounts and copy-trading from an external signal provider are prohibited by most firms, and they are enforced by pattern detection rather than by manual review. Any strategy that depends on order behaviour rather than market direction should be checked against the terms before it is used at all.

The safe assumption across all four categories is the strictest available reading. When a rule's definition is unclear, the version that protects the account is the one to trade, because a firm's interpretation is the one that will be applied — and by then the account, not the argument, is what is at stake.

How this matrix is maintained

Every numeric row carries a link to the firm's own rules page and the date it was checked. Rows that could not be checked against a source are listed without numbers rather than filled in from memory, which is why this table is shorter than most. Firms revise structures without notice — targets, trailing behaviour and split percentages all change — so every row here is a dated snapshot that you verify before buying.

Two conventions are used throughout. "Equity" in the daily basis column means the limit is measured including floating profit and loss; "balance" means closed positions only. "Trailing" describes what the failure floor follows: nothing (static), closed profit (end-of-day), or unrealised equity (intraday).

Version drift is the main practical hazard in this kind of reference. Firms describe the same rule differently in their FAQ, their terms and their checkout page, and the version that governs a dispute is usually the one in the client agreement rather than the marketing site. Where the three disagree, treat the client agreement as authoritative, and where it is unclear, ask the firm's support team in writing and keep the reply. A written answer from the firm's own support channel is worth more than any page like this one, including when it contradicts it.

If a number here contradicts your firm's current documentation, send it with a screenshot of the terms and the row will be corrected. Accurate rules data is more useful to traders than any opinion about which firm is best, and the fastest way to keep it accurate is for people who hit a discrepancy to report it.

Rules matrix questions

Why is the daily loss basis so important?

Because the same percentage produces very different constraints. A 5% limit on closed balance only counts realised losses, while a 5% limit on equity including floating losses can be breached by an open position that later recovers. The second version roughly doubles the practical size discipline required.

What does end-of-day trailing mean exactly?

The failure floor moves up only when profit is closed at the end of the trading day. Intraday swings do not move it. It is gentler than intraday trailing and stricter than a static floor.

Which firms have no consistency rule at all?

It varies by plan, not by market. FTMO's 2-Step evaluation states no consistency rule; FundedNext asks traders to confirm it at checkout; Apex applies 50% at payout on current accounts (30% on legacy accounts); Topstep applies 55% of the target during the Combine and 40% at payout. The column above records the stage where the rule applies, and a plan page is the only place the answer is definitive.

How do minimum trading days affect an evaluation?

They set a floor on completion time. Reaching the target early does not end the evaluation if the firm requires ten trading days, and rushing the target to finish sooner usually creates a consistency problem at payout instead.

Can rules change during an evaluation?

Yes, and firms are generally within their rights to do so. Changes mid-evaluation are rare but they happen, which is why every attempt is logged session by session — if a firm alters a rule that ends an attempt, the log is the record that supports a reset request.

How often should I re-check these figures?

Before every purchase. Firms change terms quarterly at times, and promotional periods sometimes come with different conditions than the standard product with the same name.

How to read the matrix without being misled

A table of rules is compressed information, and compression loses things. Four columns in particular are easy to misread, and each has a specific failure mode that has cost traders a challenge.

The daily limit column

Where a firm states a daily loss limit, the table records the percentage but not always the measurement point. Almost every firm measures it on equity, including floating positions, which means an open trade that is losing counts against the limit before it is closed. A trader who reads the column as a realised-loss limit closes positions at the limit and then discovers the account was already in breach while the position was open.

The trailing column

"Trailing" without a qualifier is ambiguous. Intradaily trailing updates with every tick of unrealised profit; end-of-day trailing updates once at the close, on realised balance. The matrix distinguishes the two where the rulebook is explicit, and marks the column as plan-dependent where a firm offers both. A firm offering both is not being vague — it is selling two products, and the choice between them is one of the most consequential decisions a futures trader makes.

The consistency column

Consistency rules almost always apply at payout rather than at evaluation, and the matrix says so. The trap is reading "30% best day" as a target constraint and trading accordingly. It is a withdrawal constraint: the account can be at target and profitable with the payout still blocked, because the best day exceeds the threshold as a share of total profit.

The notable-feature column

This column is deliberately not a recommendation. It records the one thing about a firm that changes how an evaluation is planned — an end-of-day trailing model, an unusual minimum day count, a payout window that arrives monthly rather than on request. Treat it as the answer to "what should I read next in this firm's rulebook", not as a summary of quality.

Choosing a first firm from the matrix

For a first evaluation, three properties matter more than any other combination: a static maximum loss, an explicit daily limit measured in a way that is easy to track, and no consistency rule at payout. A firm with all three is the most forgiving structure available, and forgiving is the correct objective for a first attempt — not cheap, and not fast.

The next-best structure is a static maximum loss with a consistency rule, because the rule delays a payout but does not risk the account during the evaluation. Trailing drawdowns belong later, once the platform mechanics and the daily routine are established, since a trailing floor punishes precisely the behaviour a new trader has least control over: giving back an unrealised gain.

What the matrix cannot tell you

It cannot tell you how a firm behaves when a withdrawal is delayed, how quickly support answers, or whether a rule is enforced consistently. Those are the questions reviews answer, badly and usefully in equal measure. It also cannot tell you whether a rule will still exist next month — firms revise terms, and a matrix is a snapshot with a review date rather than a live feed.

The practical consequence is to check the two or three rows that matter against the firm's own rulebook before purchase. The matrix is for shortlisting: it reduces eleven firms to two or three worth reading properly. Reading it as a substitute for the rulebook is how a trader ends up surprised by a footnote.

Why some firms are excluded

Firms without a publicly readable rulebook are not included, and neither are firms whose drawdown mechanics are described only in marketing language. That is a deliberate editorial line rather than an oversight: a rule that cannot be quoted cannot be compared, and a comparison that fills the gap with an estimate is worse than one that admits the gap.

Two further exclusions are worth naming. Firms that require a subscription rather than a one-off fee are left out because the cost of an attempt becomes a function of how long it takes, which makes the comparison unreadable. And firms whose rules differ by region are left out unless the differences are published, since a matrix that describes one jurisdiction and is read in another is actively misleading.

Where a row has been excluded for one of these reasons, the article notes it rather than silently dropping the firm. Readers arrive with a firm in mind, and an unexplained absence is usually interpreted as an oversight rather than a judgement.

The same courtesy applies to figures that have changed. Where a firm has moved a limit or a target since the last review, the entry is updated rather than left with a footnote, because a matrix is read at a glance and a glance does not read footnotes. The review date at the top of the page is the only claim being made about currency, and it is worth checking before relying on any row.

Where two firms look identical in the table, the difference is almost always in a clause the columns cannot hold — an enforcement policy, a scaling criterion, or a definition of a trading day. Those are the reasons the firm-specific articles exist, and the matrix is best used as an index into them rather than as a verdict.

Read this way, the table saves hours: it decides which two rulebooks are worth an afternoon, and it does so in a single screen.
