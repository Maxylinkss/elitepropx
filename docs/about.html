An independent challenge-passing service — and nothing else

ElitePropX is not a signal group, a fund, a trading educator, a broker affiliate or a copy-trading platform. It does one thing: it takes a prop firm evaluation, trades it against that firm's published rules, and hands over the funded account when the firm confirms the pass.

Why this service exists

A prop firm evaluation is not a test of whether you understand markets. It is a test of whether you can produce a specific return inside a specific set of constraints without ever breaching them. Those are different skills, and the second one is mechanical, repeatable and largely independent of forecasting ability.

That gap is where most traders lose money. Someone who has traded profitably for years on a personal account will often fail three $100,000 evaluations in a row, because the daily loss limit punishes a style that worked fine when the only consequence of a bad day was a smaller personal balance. The frustration is real, and it compounds: after the second failure the sunk cost starts driving decisions, and the third attempt is usually worse than the first.

ElitePropX was built for that specific situation. Instead of trying to convert a discretionary trader into a rule-abiding evaluation machine, we separate the two jobs. The evaluation is a rules problem, so we treat it like one — sized positions, a fixed daily risk budget, a pace that reaches the target over days rather than in one session, and a hard stop when the day's budget is spent. The trader keeps the part that actually requires judgement: running the funded account afterwards with their own strategy.

How the operation is structured

The service runs on three documents and one rule.

The rulebook summary is written before the evaluation starts. It lists the firm's target, daily loss limit, maximum loss, drawdown type, minimum trading days, consistency requirement and any instrument or news restrictions. If a firm's rule is ambiguous — and many are — the ambiguity is resolved conservatively, in the direction that protects the account.

The risk plan converts those rules into numbers: maximum risk per trade as a percentage of the daily loss limit, maximum number of trades per session, and the conditions under which trading stops for the day. The plan is deliberately unsophisticated. Its purpose is to survive the sequences that end evaluations, not to maximise return.

The progress log records what happened: equity at the close of each session, distance to the failure floor, percentage of target reached, and days elapsed against the firm's limits. Clients see the log rather than a summary of it, because the log is the only honest way to tell someone that a week was flat.

The rule is simpler than any of that: the firm's rules always beat the market view. An opportunity that looks exceptional is skipped if taking it would put the account within reach of the daily limit. Evaluations are not passed by finding the best trade of the month; they are passed by never letting a bad sequence matter.

A worked example: pacing a $100,000 two-phase evaluation

Abstract descriptions of risk discipline are easy to write and hard to apply. A concrete example is more useful, so here is how a standard two-phase forex evaluation — 10% target on phase one, 5% on phase two, 5% daily loss limit, 10% maximum loss — is typically paced when ElitePropX trades it.

Starting conditions

Balance $100,000. Profit target phase one: $10,000. Daily loss limit: $5,000. Maximum loss: $10,000. Minimum trading days: four. No time limit, which is the single most helpful rule in the rulebook, because it means a flat week costs time rather than the account.

The plan sets maximum risk per trade at $1,600 — roughly a third of the daily limit. That number is chosen so that three consecutive losses, which happen regularly to any strategy with a normal win rate, still leave the account intact and the day unfinished by only a fraction of its budget.

Week one: build distance from the floor

The first sessions are about creating separation between equity and the $90,000 failure level. Sessions are short: one or two high-quality setups per day, no chasing, no averaging into losers. A realistic week-one outcome is $2,000 to $3,500 of profit, or a small loss with the daily budget barely touched. Both are acceptable. The purpose of week one is not progress toward the target; it is arriving at week two with a cushion and no rule breach.

Week two: convert the cushion

With a cushion in place, the same risk per trade can produce a larger share of the target, because a losing sequence no longer threatens the maximum loss level. Sessions lengthen slightly and the target is approached in increments. Traders who want to know the arithmetic can check it directly: at $1,600 risk per trade and a 1.8:1 average reward-to-risk, roughly nine winning trades at $2,880 each reach the $10,000 target — a quantity of trades that fits comfortably inside two to three weeks of disciplined execution.

Phase two: smaller target, tighter margins

The second phase usually has a smaller target and the same daily limit, which sounds easier and is not. Complacency is the failure mode here: traders who sprinted phase one with loose sizing discover that a 5% target still has a 10% maximum loss attached, and one oversized attempt can erase both phases of work. The second phase is traded more conservatively than the first, with maximum risk per trade reduced and the profit taken in smaller pieces.

What the progress log looks like

Each session is logged with four numbers: closing equity, distance from the failure floor, percentage of target completed, and trading days used against the firm's minimum. A client reading the log can see immediately whether the evaluation is ahead or behind, and whether the risk budget was respected. There is no interpretation layer between the numbers and the client, which is deliberate — a summary can hide a week, a log cannot.

The point of the example: passing is a pacing problem with a fixed risk budget, not a forecasting contest. Almost every failed evaluation breaches the daily limit or the trailing floor in a single session, and almost every one of those breaches was avoidable by sizing for the downside instead of the target.

Why the fee is flat

Most service pricing in this space is a percentage of something: a share of the funded account's profit, a subscription that continues for as long as you keep the account, or a fee that scales with the account size being passed. All three create the same problem — the cost of the service grows with the value of the thing you are trying to own.

A flat $220 fee inverts that. A trader passing a $300,000 evaluation pays the same as a trader passing a $25,000 one, which means the larger accounts are dramatically cheaper in percentage terms. That is fine, because the work is nearly identical: the same rulebook analysis, the same risk discipline, the same reporting. The number of zeros on the account does not change how many losing trades the daily limit can absorb in relative terms.

The same reasoning explains the retry policy. If a fee is charged per attempt, a failed evaluation becomes revenue rather than a problem, which is exactly the wrong incentive for a business whose entire value proposition is discipline. Covering retries removes that incentive entirely.

What you pay and what you do not: $220 to ElitePropX covers one evaluation traded to target, with retries if an attempt breaches a rule. The firm's own challenge fee is paid separately and goes to the firm. There is no monthly charge, no profit split and no fee at payout.

When you should not use a passing service

Three situations justify doing the evaluation yourself. First, your firm prohibits third-party trading and enforces the policy — in that case any passing service puts the funded account at risk, and the correct answer is to trade it yourself or choose a different firm. Second, you are deliberately using the evaluation as practice to build evaluation-specific discipline, which many traders do as a form of training. Third, your strategy is already producing consistent results within your firm's constraints; if the constraint set is not the bottleneck, removing it saves nothing.

For everyone else — traders who can trade but keep breaching one rule — the arithmetic usually favours paying once for execution rather than paying repeatedly for attempts.

Almost every prop firm pays affiliates a commission for sending traders to their evaluation checkout pages. Those commissions are frequently large — a meaningful percentage of the challenge fee — and they create an obvious conflict: the more evaluations a site sends, the more it earns, regardless of whether any of them are passed.

ElitePropX takes no affiliate commission from any firm. That is why this site does not push you toward whichever firm has the best referral programme this month, and why the firm comparison pages rank firms on drawdown structure, payout reliability and evaluation mechanics rather than on the size of an introductory offer.

It also means you should expect recommendations that do not always serve a firm's interests. When a firm's rules make a passing service a poor idea — because third-party trading is explicitly prohibited and enforced — the right answer is to say so, even though it costs a sale. That standard is easier to hold when no one is paying you per referral.

Track record: what the numbers mean

ElitePropX reports more than 500 managed evaluations passed, a completion rate around 95%, coverage of more than twenty firms, and an average time to pass of roughly fourteen days. Four caveats belong next to those figures, because a track record without them is marketing rather than information.

First, the denominator. A completion rate only means something if failures are counted. Ours includes evaluations that breached a rule and were reset under the retry policy — which is why retries are covered rather than billed.

Second, selection. Not every assignment is accepted. Evaluations with rules that make the outcome genuinely unpredictable, or firms whose terms make the arrangement a clear breach, get declined or redirected. A pass rate measured only on work you accept should always be read that way.

Third, a pass is not a payout. Funding is the end of the evaluation and the beginning of a new rulebook. Firms apply minimum trading days, profit thresholds, consistency checks and identity verification before the first withdrawal. A service that confused the two would be lying about what it controls.

Fourth, past results do not predict yours. Firm rule changes, account sizes, instrument lists and market regimes all vary. The certificates on the passed challenges page are there so you can check the claims against documents issued by the firms themselves rather than screenshots of an equity curve.

How engagements usually end

The typical relationship is short and transactional. A trader arranges an evaluation, it is passed in around two weeks, the funded credentials are handed over, and that is the last we hear from them unless they come back for a second account — which many do, because a firm's scaling plan makes multiple evaluations worthwhile.

A minority of clients want to talk through the funded phase: how to keep the consistency rule satisfied, when to request a payout, how to handle the first withdrawal thresholds. That advice is part of the handover and is not billed separately. It is also not trading advice — we will discuss rules and mechanics, not which way the market is going.

Assignments we decline, and why

Turning work away is the clearest signal of what a business actually believes. Four categories are routinely declined.

Firms that explicitly prohibit third-party trading and enforce it. If a firm's terms state that the account must be traded personally by the holder, the risk of a voided funded account falls on the client, not on us, and that is a bad trade for the client regardless of how attractive the account size is. Those enquiries are redirected to firms with clearer terms, or to passing the evaluation themselves.

Evaluation models where the outcome is genuinely random. Some promotional evaluation structures use tight limits and no time limit, which is workable; others use short windows, tight limits and aggressive trailing floors, which compresses the pass into luck. When the rules leave no room for normal variance, the honest answer is that nobody can pass it reliably, including a service that claims to.

Requests to keep trading the funded account. Clients occasionally ask whether the same arrangement can continue after funding, sometimes offering a profit share that looks generous. The answer is always no. Running funded accounts for a cut turns a fixed-fee execution business into a profit-sharing arrangement with payout-timing conflicts, and it takes the account out of the hands of the person whose name is on it.

Impatient timelines that conflict with the rules. "Can this be passed by Friday?" is a fair question and sometimes the answer is yes. When the firm requires a minimum number of trading days, or when a target that large cannot be reached inside the daily loss budget without gambling, the timeline is refused rather than attempted. A rushed evaluation fails and consumes a retry that a correctly paced attempt would not have needed.

How an assignment is run internally

Most of what decides a pass happens before the first order and after the last one, so it is worth describing the process in the order it actually happens rather than presenting the outcome as though it were the method.

Stage one: reading the rulebook properly

Every firm publishes terms pages, and most of them contradict themselves somewhere between the marketing page and the schedule of charges. The first task on an assignment is to reconcile those pages into one sheet: what the daily limit is measured on, whether the maximum loss trails or stays static, whether the target is balance or equity based, what the minimum trading days are, and what happens at payout. Where the published terms are genuinely ambiguous, the firm is asked directly and the answer is kept in writing.

This stage is unglamorous and it is where most of the value sits. A trader who misreads one line — for instance treating a trailing maximum loss as static — is not making an analysis error, they are solving a different puzzle than the one they bought.

Stage two: converting the rules into four numbers

The rulebook becomes an index card: daily loss budget in dollars, distance to the failure level, target expressed in R multiples of the per-trade risk, and any consistency constraint that applies at payout rather than during the evaluation. Those four numbers decide position size, session length and pacing. They are recalculated every morning of the assignment, because three of the four change as equity moves.

What is trackedWhy it mattersHow often it changes
Daily loss budgetSets the maximum exposure for a sessionDaily, from the firm's measurement point
Distance to failure levelDetermines whether size must shrinkEvery time equity makes a new extreme
Target in RConverts a percentage into a trade countOn each change in per-trade risk
Payout constraintsCan delay a withdrawal after a clean passOnly when the rulebook changes

Stage three: trading the plan, not the chart

The evaluation itself is deliberately boring. A fixed instrument set, a fixed session, constant risk per trade, and a hard stop at eighty percent of the daily budget. Progress is measured in trading days completed and rule violations incurred, not in distance to target — because distance to target is the number that produces the behaviour which fails accounts.

Where a firm permits leverage that would allow a target to be reached in a single session, the correct response is to ignore it. The target is not the constraint; the constraint is surviving a normal losing sequence while reaching it, and that arithmetic does not care how much margin the firm offers.

Stage four: the handover

On a pass, the firm issues the funded account in the client's name. The handover includes the certificate or dashboard evidence, the rulebook as it stood on the day of the pass, and the payout schedule. What it does not include is ongoing discretionary trading on the funded account: the account is the client's, and whether it is traded by them or by another service is a separate decision with its own terms.

What we measure, and what we do not claim

Two numbers appear on this site: challenges passed and pass rate. Both are self-reported and both should be read with that caveat. The pass count is the number of evaluations completed to target or to a funded certificate, and it excludes attempts that were abandoned by the client. The pass rate is calculated against evaluations started in the same period, not against enquiries, which is the more generous denominator and the more misleading one.

What is not claimed is equally important. There is no claim of a proprietary edge, no claim that a pass is guaranteed, and no claim that past results predict the next attempt. Prop firm evaluations are a rules puzzle with a variance component; a service that has solved the rules puzzle still loses to variance sometimes, and the retry clause exists for exactly that reason.

Corrections policy

Firm rulebooks change without notice, and this site carries a lot of numbers that can go stale. Where a figure is found to be wrong — a loss limit, a target, a fee, a payout term — it is corrected on the page and the review date in the header is updated. Where a firm has changed its terms in a way that invalidates an article's central argument, the article is rewritten rather than patched, because a page whose conclusion no longer follows from its evidence is worse than no page at all.

Complaints are handled the same way. A client who believes a fee was misquoted, a timeline misrepresented or a rule misread is asked for the correspondence and the date. Where the complaint is correct, the fee is refunded and the page that caused it is fixed. Where it is not, the reasoning is written out in full — a service that cannot explain its own pricing to an unhappy client has a pricing problem, not a communication problem.

Why the site carries long articles rather than a sales page

A single-page service site has to compress everything into persuasion, which is why so many of them are unreadable. The articles here exist for a different reason: the questions clients ask before buying — how a trailing drawdown actually updates, why a consistency rule blocks a payout after a clean pass, what happens when a firm forbids third-party trading — have long answers, and a client who has read the long answer tends to make a better decision than one who has read a bullet list.

That also sets the editorial standard. Pages are dated and reviewed, firm numbers are attributed to the rulebook rather than to memory, and where a rule varies between account types the variation is described instead of averaged away. None of that makes the content immune to being wrong; it makes being wrong visible, which is the more useful property.
