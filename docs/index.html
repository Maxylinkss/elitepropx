Why evaluations fail

The four rules that end most challenges

Across hundreds of managed evaluations, failures cluster around a small number of mechanical errors rather than bad market reads. Understanding them is useful even if you never hire a passing service, because they explain why a strategy that works on a demo account produces a blown evaluation.

Daily loss limit

The tightest constraint on most accounts. A single oversized position can consume the entire daily budget, and the day is over regardless of what the market does next.

Trailing drawdown

A floor that follows your equity upward shrinks your cushion every time you are right. Traders who bank a big early win often fail because the floor moved with it.

Consistency rules

Firms increasingly refuse payouts when one day dominates total profit. A 40% best day can disqualify an otherwise legitimate pass.

Behaviour

Revenge trading after a loss, doubling size to recover, and abandoning the plan at the first drawdown. This is the rule that is not in the rulebook, and it causes the most damage.

Read the full breakdown in our guide to the consistency rule, which includes the arithmetic firms use and how to keep any single day under the threshold.

The arithmetic

Target and drawdown are one equation

A prop firm evaluation is a probability problem dressed as a trading opportunity. The target tells you how far you must travel. The drawdown tells you how much room you have to be wrong along the way. Most traders size for the target — they calculate the position that reaches 10% fastest — and then discover that the same position can lose the account in three bad trades.

Our sizing model starts from the loss limit and works backwards. If a $100,000 account has a 5% daily limit, the daily budget is $5,000 and no single trade is allowed to risk more than a third of it. Three consecutive losses — a routine occurrence for any strategy with a normal win rate — leave the account bruised but alive. The target is then reached by repetition, not by one heroic trade.

This is the least exciting part of the service and the reason it works. There is no secret indicator. There is a defined risk per trade, a defined number of attempts per day, and a hard stop when the daily budget is spent.

Inside the process

What a managed evaluation actually looks like, day by day

Every evaluation follows the same rhythm, though the length changes with the firm. A two-phase forex challenge with no time limit is paced very differently from a futures evaluation with a thirty-day window, and the daily plan is written accordingly.

Day one: mapping the rulebook

Before a single trade is placed, the firm's rules are written into a one-page plan: the profit target in currency and percentage, the daily loss limit in currency, whether the maximum loss is static or trailing, whether the trailing stops at break-even or keeps following, the minimum number of trading days, any consistency requirement, and the restrictions on news trading, weekend holds, and instruments. This document is the only thing that decides whether the evaluation is passed. Market opinion is secondary.

Days two to four: building the cushion

The opening phase is deliberately unremarkable. Positions are sized so that a losing streak of three or four trades cannot approach the daily limit, and the target exposure is chosen from the firm's permitted instrument list rather than from a favourite pair or contract. The goal of the first days is not profit — it is distance between equity and the failure floor. A trader who is 1% up with the floor untouched has more room to work with than a trader who is 3% up with a trailing floor sitting just beneath the current price.

The middle: pacing, not sprinting

This is where most self-managed attempts die. Once an account is halfway to target, the temptation is to accelerate, which means larger size, which means the daily limit is consumed by a single position. In a managed evaluation the daily risk budget is fixed at the start and does not expand because the account is winning. Progress is reported as it happens, and if a day is flat, that is recorded as a flat day rather than an excuse to double up.

The final stretch: protecting the pass

When the target is within reach, the priority flips from making money to not breaching anything. Trading stops earlier in the session, size comes down, and the remaining ground is covered in small increments. Where a firm requires a minimum number of trading days, those days are logged with legitimate activity — a requirement that catches out traders who sprint to target in three sessions and then discover the account is not eligible for funding until day ten. The final checkpoint is the consistency test: if one day is more than roughly a third of total profit, additional ordinary winning days are added until the distribution is acceptable.

When the firm verifies the pass, the account is funded and the credentials are handed over. From that moment the plan is yours to keep or throw away — and the funded phase has its own set of checkpoints before a payout clears.

Compliance, firm terms and fair use

Passing services sit in an area that prop firms treat inconsistently, and the honest version of that story is worth reading before you pay anyone — including us.

  • Some firms prohibit third-party trading outright. Their terms may state that the account must be traded by the account holder personally, and that any other arrangement voids the evaluation or the funded account. If a firm takes that position, using a passing service carries the risk of losing the account after funding, not just during the evaluation.
  • Other firms restrict account sharing but do not police who presses the button. Enforcement is usually reactive: it happens when something else draws attention to an account, such as an unusual payout request or a pattern that breaks the consistency rule.
  • Firms change terms frequently. A policy that permitted third-party trading last quarter may be gone this quarter. Nobody, including us, can give you a permanent guarantee about a firm's future enforcement.
  • You are the account holder. KYC is matched to you, payouts are paid to you, and any dispute with the firm is yours to resolve. Our role ends when the funded login is handed over.

The practical advice is unglamorous: read your firm's terms, ask us what we know about that specific firm's current policy, and decide with your eyes open. If a firm's rules make a passing service a bad idea for you, we would rather tell you before you pay than after your payout is frozen.

After the pass

What happens once the account is funded

The moment of funding is not the finish line. Most firms relax the evaluation rules on a funded account but add new ones: a minimum number of trading days before the first payout, a profit threshold, a consistency check, and identity verification.

  1. Funded credentials issued. The account is in your name and under your control.
  2. Minimum trading days. Usually five to ten, depending on the firm.
  3. Profit target for payout. Often 5–10% on forex accounts, or a dollar figure on futures accounts.
  4. Consistency check. No single day should dominate your total profit.
  5. KYC. Identity and address documents, matched to the account holder.
  6. Withdrawal. Typically one to seven business days after approval.

Before you start

What to prepare so the handover is smooth

The technical part of a managed evaluation is ours. Three small things on your side make the difference between a pass that converts into funding on day one and one that stalls in the firm's verification queue.

1. Know which firm and which account size

Firm names are not enough, because most firms run several evaluation models. FTMO runs a two-phase challenge with a 10% target and no consistency rule on that route; Apex runs dollar-based EOD or intraday models with a 50% consistency rule at payout. If you are choosing between two firms, ask which one passes more reliably at your preferred account size — the answer is usually the one with a static drawdown and no consistency rule, because there is less that can technically disqualify a finished attempt.

2. Have your KYC ready before the funded phase

Identity verification is a firm requirement, not ours, but it is the most common reason a funded trader waits an extra week for a first payout. Government ID, a matching address document, and a payment method in the same name remove almost all verification delays.

3. Decide how you will trade the funded account

This is the part we cannot do for you. A funded account has its own daily limits and its own consistency expectations, and traders who pass an evaluation and then trade it like a personal account usually give it back within a month. If you have a written plan for the funded phase — maximum risk per trade, maximum daily risk, target, and a rule for stopping — you are in a much stronger position than someone who simply wants to be funded.

When those three things are in place, the process is short: confirm the firm and size, pay the flat fee, and wait for the first report.

The retry clause is the part worth reading twice. A retry covers an evaluation that ends through a rule breach — a daily loss limit, a maximum loss limit, a consistency check at payout. Why a flat fee rather than a profit split

Most services in this market charge a share of the funded account, typically somewhere between 10% and 30% of payouts, sometimes indefinitely. That model aligns incentives in theory — the service earns only if the trader earns — but in practice it changes what the service optimises for. A split model rewards large accounts and long retention; a flat fee rewards a clean pass and a client who comes back for a second account. The second incentive is easier to check: either the certificate exists or it does not.

There is a cost to the flat model, and it should be stated plainly. The service is paid whether or not the evaluation passes, so the only thing protecting quality is the retry clause and reputation. Anyone comparing services should treat that as the key question: what happens on a failure, in writing, before money changes hands.

Which firms we will and will not trade

Any firm with a published rulebook and an account size the client can afford is workable in principle. What is not workable is a firm whose terms are ambiguous on the two things that decide an evaluation: how the loss limit is measured and whether third-party trading is permitted. Ambiguity in either direction turns a rules problem into a dispute, and a dispute is the one outcome that costs the client more than a failed attempt.

Firms that explicitly prohibit third-party trading are refused, not worked around. The reason is not moral positioning; it is that enforcement after funding is the worst possible outcome for a client, since it forfeits the account at exactly the point where the work would have paid. A service that quietly accepts those terms is transferring its own risk to the person least able to absorb it.

Everything else is negotiable, and most of it should be discussed before purchase rather than after. Account size, platform, instrument set and even the timing of the attempt can all be adjusted to fit a client's circumstances — a trader who wants a small first account and a three-week window is a different assignment from one who wants the largest tier available this month. What cannot be adjusted is the rulebook, and that is precisely why it is the thing read first.

If you are undecided between two firms, send both names. The reply will usually be a recommendation with a reason attached, and the reason is the useful part: it tells you which of the two rulebooks is more forgiving at the account size you can afford, which is a question with an answer independent of anyone's opinion about the firms themselves.
