Pass a Prop Firm Challenge Without Breaking the Rules

Most traders who fail a challenge do not lose because they were wrong about the market. They lose because a rule they knew about was breached by a decision they made in the four minutes after a loss. Passing without breaking the rules is therefore less about prediction and more about building a process that makes the breach difficult: a written limit, a smaller size, a stop rule and a log. This guide is that process, from the pre-trade checklist to the recovery plan after a breach.

Key takeaways

  • Breaches are usually behavioural, not analytical. The rule is known; the decision to break it is made under pressure.
  • The seven rule categories cover almost every failure: loss, daily, calendar, consistency, conduct, platform and payout.
  • A pre-trade checklist and a hard session stop remove the decision from the moment of stress.
  • After a breach, the correct response is a smaller size and a written review, not a faster recovery.

What breaking a rule actually means

Prop firms distinguish between two kinds of breach, and the distinction changes the consequence. A hard breach is a rule that ends the account immediately: the maximum loss, the trailing threshold, a prohibited conduct that the firm treats as terminal. A soft breach is a rule that ends the day, delays a payout or triggers a warning: the daily loss limit, a consistency condition, a missing trading day. The same action can be hard at one firm and soft at another, which is why the first step is to classify every rule in your firm's terms.

The classification matters because traders tend to treat all rules as equally serious. They are not. A hard breach is a binary event that ends the evaluation; a soft breach is a cost that can be recovered. A trader who treats a soft breach as a catastrophe may take a reckless recovery trade, turning a recoverable day into a hard breach. A trader who treats a hard breach as a minor inconvenience may lose the account while trying to fix a soft one.

There is a third category that is often overlooked: the rule that is not a rule until a human decides it is. "Excessive risk", "abusive trading" and "inconsistent with the firm's values" are phrases that appear in terms and are enforced by review. They do not have a number, so the trader cannot know exactly where the line is. The safe approach is to behave as though the line is stricter than the written rule, especially with conduct and account-sharing clauses.

The practical definition of breaking a rule is therefore: any action that gives the firm a contractual reason to end the account, delay a payout or void a result. The definition includes actions that are not explicitly prohibited but that a reviewer could reasonably treat as abusive. The rest of this guide is about making those actions unlikely.

The seven categories of breach

Almost every breach falls into one of seven categories. Sorting them helps because each has a different prevention method.

One: the loss floor

The maximum loss or trailing threshold is the most serious rule. Breaches happen when the trader sizes too large for the remaining room, or when a trailing floor moves up after profit and the trader does not reduce size. The prevention is to calculate the distance to the floor every morning and to size from that distance, not from the account balance. If the distance divided by the normal risk is fewer than five, reduce the size; if it is fewer than three, stop for the day.

Two: the daily limit

The daily loss is the most frequently breached rule. It is usually measured on equity, which means an open losing position counts before it is closed. The prevention is to risk a fraction of the daily budget per trade, to track the aggregate floating loss, and to stop at a fixed percentage of the budget rather than at the limit. The reset time is part of the rule; a late-session recovery trade is a breach waiting to happen.

Three: the calendar

Calendar breaches are quiet. A trader reaches the target but has not met the minimum trading days, or runs out of time because the plan assumed unlimited patience. The prevention is to write the phase deadline and the minimum days on the daily card, and to plan the target across the required number of sessions. A fast pass that is not eligible is not a pass.

Four: consistency

A consistency rule can delay a payout even after a clean pass. Breaches happen when one day dominates the profit and the trader does not add enough profitable days to dilute it. The prevention is to plan the distribution from the start: a target reached over more sessions, with no single day above the cap. The rule should be checked at the start, not at the payout request.

Five: conduct

Conduct includes news trading, weekend holding, hedging, copy trading, account sharing, EAs and third-party trading. The rules vary by firm and are often enforced after funding. The prevention is to read the conduct section, ask for written confirmation of anything ambiguous, and avoid any arrangement that depends on the firm not noticing. A conduct breach can void an account that has already been paid.

Six: platform and technical

Technical breaches are caused by the platform rather than the strategy: a stop that was not server-side, an order that was rejected, a disconnect during an open position, a data feed that lagged. The prevention is to test the platform on a demo, confirm server-side stops, keep a backup connection and avoid holding positions through known maintenance windows. The trader is responsible for the platform's behaviour, even when it is not their fault.

Seven: payout conditions

Payout breaches are the ones that turn a funded account into an unfunded one: a missing KYC document, a consistency condition, a minimum payout day, a withdrawal method that is not allowed. The prevention is to read the payout terms before starting and to complete the KYC early. A trader who reaches the payout request and discovers a missing condition has lost time, not the account, but the delay can be long.

The four behaviours behind most breaches

Rules are broken by people, and the same four behaviours account for most failures. Recognising them is more useful than memorising a rule list.

The recovery trade

After a loss, the trader increases size to get back to flat. The recovery trade is usually the largest position of the day and the one with the least analysis. It turns a normal loss into a daily breach or a floor breach. The fix is a hard stop after two losses, written before the session, regardless of the budget remaining. The stop is not a punishment; it is the recognition that judgement is worst immediately after a loss.

The late-session entry

A trader opens a position shortly before the daily reset, hoping to recover the day. The position belongs to tomorrow's budget in practice, but the trader is thinking about today's. If it loses, the new day starts in drawdown before the trader has made a decision. The fix is a cut-off time, usually one hour before the reset, after which no new positions are opened.

The correlated book

Three positions in correlated instruments, each sized correctly in isolation, can consume the daily budget together. The trader believes they are diversified, but the firm measures the account, not the tickets. The fix is a maximum aggregate exposure and a rule to count correlated pairs as one position. The daily check should look at the total floating loss, not the per-trade risk.

The rule that is not read

The trader assumes the new firm's rules work like the last firm's. The drawdown trails instead of static, the consistency rule applies at payout, the news policy is stricter. The fix is a rule map for every firm, built before the first trade. A trader who changes firms without rebuilding the map is carrying assumptions that may be wrong.

The common thread is that all four behaviours happen under time pressure. The recovery trade happens after a loss; the late entry happens before a reset; the correlated book builds during a busy session; the unread rule is discovered at the first breach. The process has to be designed for the pressured moment, not the calm one.

The pre-trade compliance checklist

A checklist is not a sign of weakness; it is how professionals avoid errors that are obvious in hindsight. Run this list before the first trade of each session.

  1. What is the current equity and the distance to the failure floor? Write the dollar figure. On a trailing account, use the current floor, not the starting balance.
  2. What is today's daily budget, and how much of it is already spent? Include floating losses, not just closed trades.
  3. What is the maximum risk per trade, as a fraction of the daily budget? One third is a common starting point.
  4. What is the maximum aggregate exposure? Count correlated positions as one.
  5. When does the daily limit reset, and what is the cut-off time for new entries? Write both times.
  6. How many trading days are required, and how many have been used? Check the phase deadline.
  7. Is there a consistency rule, and what is the current distribution? If one day is near the cap, plan the next sessions.
  8. Are there news, weekend or conduct restrictions today? Check the economic calendar and the firm's policy.
  9. Is the platform's stop server-side, and is the backup connection ready? Test before the session, not during it.
  10. What is the stop rule for the session? The number of losses or the percentage of budget that ends the day.

The checklist takes five minutes and covers the rules that end accounts and days. It does not cover the payout conditions, which are a one-time check at funding, or the strategy, which is a separate process. The point is to make the compliance decisions before the market is open, when the trader is calm.

Building a rule-safe process

A process is rule-safe when it makes the breach difficult even under pressure. Five design choices do most of the work.

Size from the budget, not the balance

The most important choice is to calculate the per-trade risk from the daily budget rather than from the account balance. A trader who risks 1% of the account on a 5% daily limit is risking a fifth of the day per trade, which means five losses end the day. A trader who risks a third of the budget is risking 1.65% of the account, which means three losses end the day. The second is safer even though the percentage looks larger, because the budget is the binding constraint.

Write the stop rule before the session

The stop rule should be a number, not a feeling: two consecutive losses, or 60% of the daily budget, whichever comes first. A written rule is a decision made by a calmer version of the trader. A trader who decides in the moment will negotiate, and the negotiation usually goes in the direction of the open position.

Separate analysis from execution

A trader who analyses on one platform and executes on another needs a clear handoff. The analysis should produce a plan with entry, stop, target and size; the execution should follow the plan without renegotiation. The handoff reduces the chance of an impulsive trade on the analysis platform, where there is no order ticket to slow the decision.

Keep the rule map visible

The rule map — the six or seven numbers that matter — should be visible during the session, not buried in a document. A card next to the screen is enough. The map is updated each morning and after any equity change. A trader who has to look up the daily budget during a losing streak is a trader who will guess.

Review weekly, not daily

A daily review of every trade is exhausting and tends to focus on outcomes. A weekly review of the process — how often the budget was more than half spent, how many sessions ended at the stop, how many trades were outside the plan — is more useful. The weekly review is where the process improves; the daily checklist is where it is executed.

The daily routine

The routine is the practical form of the process. It has three parts: before the session, during the session and after the session.

Before the session

Update the five numbers, check the economic calendar, confirm the platform and the stop rule, and decide the maximum number of trades. The decision is made before the market opens, when there is no position to defend. The routine should take less than ten minutes; if it takes longer, the rule map is too complicated.

During the session

Track the aggregate floating loss, not just the closed profit. Check the distance to the floor after any significant move. Stop at the written rule, not at the limit. If a rule changes or the platform behaves unexpectedly, stop trading and investigate; the market will still be there after the issue is resolved.

After the session

Record the closing equity, the highest floating loss, the percentage of the daily budget used, the number of trades and whether the stop rule was followed. The highest floating loss is the number that explains an equity-measured breach; the closed result may look fine while the intraday drawdown was near the limit. The record is the input to the weekly review.

The routine is deliberately boring. Its value is that it removes the need to make compliance decisions in the moment. A trader who follows it will still have losing days, but the losses will be inside the rules, which means the account survives to trade another day.

Knowing when to stop

Stopping is the hardest part of rule-safe trading, because the market always offers another trade. Four signals are enough to end a session.

  1. The written stop is reached. Two losses, or 60% of the daily budget, whichever comes first. The stop is not negotiable because the trader agreed to it before the session.
  2. The aggregate floating loss is near the daily limit. Even if no trade has closed at a loss, the equity measurement may already be close to the limit. Reduce or close, then stop.
  3. A rule has changed or is unclear. If the firm announces a change, or the dashboard disagrees with the terms, stop and clarify. Trading through an ambiguity is a gamble on the interpretation.
  4. The trader is reacting rather than deciding. If the last three trades were entered within seconds of a loss, the session has become emotional. Stop, review and return the next day.

Stopping is not the same as giving up. A stopped session preserves the account and the trader's judgement; a session that continues past the stop risks both. The best traders are not the ones who never have losing days; they are the ones whose losing days end at the written limit.

What to do after a breach

A breach is not the end of the process, but the response determines whether it becomes a pattern. Five steps are enough.

Step one: stop trading immediately

Do not try to recover the day. The breach has already happened; additional trades only increase the loss and may turn a soft breach into a hard one. Close the platform and wait until the next session.

Step two: identify the category

Was it the loss floor, the daily limit, the calendar, consistency, conduct, platform or payout? The category tells you which part of the process failed. A daily breach is a sizing problem; a conduct breach is a reading problem; a platform breach is a testing problem.

Step three: find the decision point

Reconstruct the session and find the moment the breach became likely. It is usually not the last trade; it is the second or third position that used too much of the budget. The decision point is where the process needs to change.

Step four: reduce size for the next session

After a breach, reduce the per-trade risk by a third for the remainder of the evaluation. The reduction is not a punishment; it is a response to the evidence that the current size is too large for the trader's behaviour under pressure. The size can be restored after a week of compliant sessions.

Step five: write the review

Write a short review: what happened, which rule was breached, which decision caused it, and what will change. The review is the input to the next week's process. A breach that is not reviewed is likely to repeat; a breach that is reviewed becomes a data point.

If the breach ends the account, the same steps apply, plus a decision about whether to retry the same firm or switch. A retry without a process change is a donation to the firm. A retry with a smaller size and a written review is a genuine second attempt.

A compliance log is a simple record that makes the process visible. It does not need to be elaborate; a spreadsheet with six columns is enough.

The log is useful for three reasons. It shows the trader's behaviour over time, it supports a payout review, and it provides evidence if a firm applies a rule differently from its terms. A trader who can produce a clean log is in a stronger position than one relying on memory or screenshots.

The log should be reviewed weekly, not daily. The weekly view shows patterns that a single session cannot: how often the budget is more than half spent, how many sessions end at the stop, how many trades are outside the plan. The patterns are the material for process improvement, and they are more useful than a daily profit-and-loss figure.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common way traders break a prop firm rule?

The daily loss limit, usually through position size or aggregate exposure rather than a single oversized trade. The measurement point and the reset time are the details most often missed.

Is breaking a rule always a failed evaluation?

No. Some breaches end the day and the account continues; others end the account. The consequence is in the terms, and the distinction matters for how the trader responds.

What should I do immediately after a breach?

Stop trading. Do not try to recover the day. Identify the category and the decision point, reduce size for the next session, and write a short review.

How do I avoid a conduct breach?

Read the conduct section, ask for written confirmation of anything ambiguous, and avoid any arrangement that depends on the firm not noticing. Third-party trading is the clause that matters most for a passing service.

Can a platform cause a rule breach?

Yes. A local stop, a rejected order or a disconnect can cause an unplanned loss. Test server-side stops, keep a backup connection and avoid holding positions through known maintenance.

How much should I risk per trade on a challenge?

A fraction of the daily budget, usually one third, rather than a fraction of the account. That keeps three full losses inside the day and leaves room for floating drawdown.

Is it worth retrying after a breach?

Only with a process change. A retry with the same size and the same behaviour is likely to produce the same result. A retry with smaller size and a written review is a genuine second attempt.
