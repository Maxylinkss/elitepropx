What the Topstep Combine actually asks

A Combine is a futures evaluation priced and measured in dollars. The account size sets the target and both loss figures, but the shape of the rules is the same at every tier, and the shape is what has to be planned for.

Topstep is a futures prop firm, so the Combine is an evaluation rather than a live brokerage account: the target has to be reached and the rules held before a Topstep funded account exists at all.

The interaction is the part traders misread. A trailing floor and a daily stop are not two versions of the same protection; they fail on different days. Early in an account, with the floor well below the balance, the daily stop is usually the tighter of the two. After a strong run of profitable closes, the floor has climbed towards the balance and becomes the tighter constraint — at which point the daily stop offers less protection than it appears to, because the account can fail before the day's budget is spent.

That is why a pass is planned with both numbers computed each morning and the smaller of the two treated as the day's real limit. A plan that uses only the daily figure is over-trading the second half of the evaluation, which is precisely when most failed attempts are lost. Topstep's own rules and the pacing arithmetic are set out in the Combine rules guide.

The Topstep daily loss limit: the checkout decision

Because the daily loss limit is purchased rather than applied, the checkout page is a risk decision with a price attached, and it is worth treating as one instead of clicking through.

With the daily limit. The worst session is capped, and a session that reaches the cap is not a rule breach — the day simply ends. That converts a single bad afternoon from an account-ending event into a bounded loss, which is the single most useful property a futures rulebook can have for anyone trading to a target on a schedule.

Without it. The trailing floor is the only failure condition, so the account survives a losing session that the floor is not near, and dies on a session that reaches it. The saving is real at checkout and small relative to the evaluation's total cost; the exposure added is not small, because an unplanned session on a futures account can consume a large share of the floor in minutes.

For a service pass the recommendation is to take the daily limit where the size being bought offers it. It is not a claim that the pass cannot be run without one — it is that the plan's worst case is defined rather than open-ended, and a defined worst case is what makes a schedule honest. Where a client has already bought an account without it, the plan self-imposes the same figure, and the difference is that the limit is then a discipline rather than a rule.

Buy the rule, not the discount. Topstep discount codes change the price of a Combine, never the shape of its rules. A cheaper entry with no daily stop is a different risk from a full-price entry with one, and the two should not be compared on price alone.

The Topstep 55% best-day rule and the rising target

Most firms apply consistency at the payout. Topstep applies an expectation during the Combine as well, and the mechanism is unusual enough to state in full: the largest day is compared with the profit target, and if it exceeds 55% of that target, the target is raised to the best day divided by 0.55.

Worked in dollars, a $3,000 target on a $50,000 account sets the line at $1,650. A $2,000 session — a good day by any ordinary measure — pushes the target to $3,636, so the trader who had $1,000 left to earn now has $1,636, and the extra profit has to come from later sessions under a floor that has risen with the gain. The rule does not punish the day directly; it makes the finish line move away.

At payout the test tightens to 40% of total profit, which is the version most traders expect and the one that catches accounts passed quickly. A Combine cleared in six sessions with one dominant day can satisfy the evaluation and still fail the withdrawal test, which leaves the account trading on a funded basis for longer than planned.

Both tests are satisfied the same way, and it is a pacing decision rather than a trading one: cap each session at a fraction of the target, so no single day can approach the line, and spread the work across enough sessions that the total is built evenly. A pass assembled from eight modest days clears 55% and 40% without arithmetic at the end. The consistency guide compares Topstep's version with the payout-stage rules at other firms.

The plan a Topstep Combine pass runs on

The evaluation is traded to the rulebook, so the plan is written from Topstep's document rather than from a market view. Four numbers govern it, and all four are fixed before the first session.

  1. Risk per trade sized from the smaller of the daily budget and the remaining floor distance, not from the target. On a 100k Combine with a $3,000 floor, $600 of risk means five consecutive losses are survivable; $1,000 means three, and three is not a plan.
  2. A session stop at the daily figure where one was bought, and at the same figure self-imposed where it was not.
  3. A per-session profit cap at roughly a fifth of the target, which keeps the best day far below 55% by construction and keeps the finish line from moving.
  4. A risk taper across the last third of the target, because each profitable close lifts the floor permanently and the room left is smaller than the chart suggests.

Two behavioural rules sit alongside the numbers. No size increase after a winning day, since that is the exact moment the floor has risen and the cushion has thinned. And a pause after two losing sessions, because on a trailing account the cost of a pause is calendar while the cost of pressing is the account.

Failure handling is part of the plan rather than an afterthought. If an attempt is lost, the client hears on the day, the cause is classified as a floor event or a rule breach, and the retry starts under the same discipline at no additional service fee. A provider unable to say which rule ended an account has not read the rulebook closely enough to trade it.

The terms check, before payment

Topstep's terms decide whether a paid pass is worth anything, and they are read before the fee is taken. The answer is given in writing, including when it is no.

Is third-party trading permitted on the Combine? Answered from Topstep's own documentation. Where the clause is silent, the assumption is that it is not permitted, because a silent clause can be interpreted at the payout.

Does it extend to the funded account? Checked separately. A firm can permit outside trading during an evaluation and prohibit it afterwards, which turns a paid pass into a voided account at the first withdrawal.

Who must own the account? The client, with the client's payment method, and with verification completed by the client. Topstep's monthly billing makes it easy to accumulate accounts; ownership is still what keeps a funded account valid.

Two conduct questions are answered at the same time because they decide which sessions exist: whether trading is restricted around scheduled economic releases, and what the position or contract limits are at the size being bought. On a futures account those limits bind quickly, and a breach voids the evaluation regardless of how much profit was on the screen.

How long a Topstep Combine takes: ten to eighteen days

That range is the honest one for a paced Combine, and it comes from three constraints rather than from a marketing number.

The qualifying-day count. A dollar target can be reached in a handful of sessions; an evaluation that requires a set number of qualifying days cannot be finished before them. Pacing backwards from the count is what makes the target and the calendar land together.

The per-session cap. Keeping each day at a fifth of the target deliberately slows the pass. The trade is calendar for consistency, and it is worth making because the same distribution is what clears 40% at payout.

The taper. The last third of the target is traded at reduced size, which adds sessions at the point where the floor is closest. Attempts that fail at 80% of target usually fail there because the size was held constant while the cushion shrank.

Reporting is weekly at minimum and immediate on a failed attempt. At the end, the client receives Topstep's own confirmation of the pass rather than a summary from the service.

From a passed Topstep Combine to first payout

A passed Combine produces a funded account with its own rules, and the 40% payout test is the one that decides when money actually leaves.

Four steps belong to the first week after handover. Change the password. Confirm the account is in your name with your payment method and verification complete. Read the funded terms directly rather than a summary — payout cadence, qualifying days and the best-day test are the three that gate the first request. And write the funded plan before the first trade, with the session cap kept at a level that keeps the largest day under 40% of total profit for the cycle.

The practical consequence is that the first withdrawal should be planned around a modest accumulated profit rather than a maximum one. A funded cycle that produces $4,000 with a $2,500 best day fails the 40% test and needs more trading on an account with a live floor; the same account earning $4,000 across six even sessions withdraws without arithmetic. What this service will not do

Four refusals define the offer, and each exists because the alternative moves risk onto the client without saying so.

No pass where the terms forbid it. If Topstep's documentation does not permit outside trading, the job is declined in writing before payment.

No trading of the funded account. Access is returned or removed at handover, and the account is then the owner's to trade.

No income promise. The deliverable is a completed Combine. What the funded phase pays depends on the firm's terms and the owner's trading.

No cut of the payout. A fee taken from earnings gives the provider a reason to keep trading an account that should have been left alone.

Two habits follow. The Combine is bought by the client, not by the service, because ownership is what makes the funded account valid. And no identity documents are requested — verification cannot be delegated, whatever a provider advertises.

Frequently asked about the Topstep service

Should I buy the Combine with the daily loss limit?

Yes, at any size where it is offered. It caps the worst session and, because hitting it is not a rule breach, it turns a bad afternoon into a bounded loss rather than a failed account. Where a client already bought without it, the plan imposes the same figure as a discipline.

Does the $220 depend on account size or billing?

No. The fee is the same at every Combine size and whether the account is paid monthly or annually. The process does not change with the dollar figures, so the price does not either.

How long does a Topstep pass take?

Ten to eighteen trading days with disciplined pacing. The floor is the qualifying-day count rather than the target, and the per-session cap that satisfies the 55% rule deliberately slows the pass down.

What happens if the best day goes over 55%?

The account is not failed; the target is raised to the best day divided by 0.55. A $2,000 day against a $3,000 target makes the target $3,636, so the work grows and the floor has already climbed with the gain. The plan avoids this by capping each session at about a fifth of the target.

Can the pass fail at payout instead?

Yes. The payout test is 40% of total profit rather than 55% of the target, so an account passed with one dominant day can still be blocked at withdrawal. Even pacing satisfies both tests, which is why the session cap is part of the service rather than optional advice.

What if an attempt is lost?

The client is told the day it happens, with the cause identified as a floor event or a rule breach, and the retry runs at no additional service fee while the engagement is open. Topstep's own subscription and reset charges stay with the client.

Do you run several Combines at once?

Rarely, and not as a default. Correlated futures accounts are one position with several subscriptions, and a single session can breach more than one floor. Sequential accounts, each funded and withdrawn before the next is bought, are the recommendation.

How much does a Topstep Combine cost?

Topstep bills the Combine monthly, and the figure depends on account size and any discount running at the time, so the checkout is the only place to read it. This fee is charged once at $220 whatever the size and sits outside Topstep's own subscription.

Is the Topstep Combine the same as a Topstep evaluation?

Yes — Combine is Topstep's name for its evaluation. Traders also search for Topstep prop firm account and Topstep evaluation, and all three describe the same purchase: a dollar-target account that has to be passed inside the trailing floor before funding.

Which Topstep evaluation should a service pass trade?

The Combine, at a size where the optional daily loss limit can be bought at checkout. Choosing the smaller size with the daily stop is a better decision than the larger size without it, because the stop is what defines the worst session the plan has to survive.

What do you need from me to start?

The Combine size, whether the daily limit was included, and your holding pattern for the terms check. If the terms clear, you buy the account, we agree access in writing, and the plan is set before the first session.
