How it runs

From your first message to a funded login

The sequence is fixed and describable in advance, which is the point. A provider that cannot state the order of its own process is making it up as it goes, and the place that improvisation costs you is the payout.

  1. Terms check. You name the firm and the account size. We read the current terms for third-party trading, on the evaluation and on the funded account, and confirm in writing that the job can proceed. If it cannot, the process stops here at no cost to you.
  2. Purchase. You buy the evaluation directly from the firm, in your own name, with your own payment method. The evaluation fee is never routed through us — that keeps ownership unambiguous and keeps your identity where the firm expects it.
  3. Handover. You supply what the firm's platform requires for the account to be traded. The scope of that access is agreed in writing before it is granted, and it is limited to the evaluation account.
  4. Trading. The evaluation is traded to the firm's target and inside its rules. Progress is reported at agreed checkpoints, and you are told the day an attempt fails rather than at the end of a reporting period.
  5. Pass and return. The evaluation is completed, credentials are returned or removed, and you take control of the funded account. The pass is documented with the firm's own confirmation, not a screenshot of a dashboard.

Two details in that sequence decide most outcomes. The first is step one: a provider that accepts any firm without reading the terms has not read the terms, which leaves the payout to luck. The second is step five: access should end at handover, and the password changes afterwards regardless. A service that keeps access after the pass is keeping access to a funded account.

Reporting cadence belongs in the product too. An evaluation runs one to three weeks depending on the firm's target and minimum day count, and a provider that goes silent for that stretch leaves you unable to tell a slow pass from a failed one. Weekly checkpoints, plus immediate notice of a breach, is the honest minimum.

Doing it yourself

When paying the fee is the cheaper decision

The comparison is not easy versus hard. It is three costs weighed against each other: the fee, the calendar, and the probability of needing a second attempt.

Trading it yourself costs the firm's fee per attempt, plus whatever calendar the evaluation takes. It also costs attention, which is the input most traders under-price — an evaluation traded badly consumes the same weeks as one traded well, and three failed attempts is six weeks that produced nothing. A flat fee converts that into a known number: the firm's fee once, the service fee once, and no service charge for retries while the evaluation runs.

The arithmetic is short enough to do on the page. Take a firm fee of $500, the service fee of $220, and a trader whose own record suggests two attempts. Scope

What a prop firm passing service does

A prop firm passing service takes responsibility for one thing: producing a completed evaluation that meets a firm's profit target without breaching its rules. You buy the evaluation from the firm, supply what the platform requires, and receive a funded account that satisfies the firm's conditions.

The labels are interchangeable and worth settling before the rest of the page, because the market uses them loosely. A prop firm challenge passing service, a challenge pass service, a prop firm passing evaluation, an assessment and a combine all describe the same purchase: someone produces a completed evaluation in your name against your firm's rulebook. The request usually arrives in the first person — pass my challenge, pass my evaluation, have my account passed for me — and the answer is the same each time. What differs between providers is not the name but the three answers underneath it: whether the firm permits third-party trading, what a breached attempt costs, and who holds the account when the work stops.

The deliverable is narrow and worth being precise about. It is not a trading strategy, not a signal service, not a managed account, and not a guarantee of income. It is a completed test. Everything after the pass — the funded account's profit threshold, its consistency rule, its payout schedule, its conduct restrictions — belongs to you and to the firm.

That narrowness is why the model can be priced flat. A provider trading evaluations repeatedly is running a process rather than a view: the target, the drawdown and the daily limit are known in advance, and the work is to reach a fixed objective inside fixed constraints. It is closer to project delivery than to trading advice, which is why a fixed fee is a coherent price and a percentage of future profit is not.

Is it allowed by your firm?

This is the part most providers skip, and it is the part that decides whether a funded account survives.

Proprietary trading firms are not uniform on third-party trading. Some permit it explicitly. Some prohibit it. Some permit it on the evaluation and prohibit it on the funded account, or the reverse. And some are silent, which in practice means the firm can decide at payout time that the account was not traded by the named holder.

The consequence of getting it wrong is asymmetric. A prohibited evaluation that is passed can produce a funded account voided at the first withdrawal, which is worse than a failed evaluation: you have paid the firm, paid the service, and earned nothing. A provider that does not check the terms before accepting the job is transferring that risk to you without saying so.

Our rule: if your firm's terms forbid third-party trading, you are told in writing before payment what the clause says and what enforcement has looked like. There is no version of this where quietly accepting is acceptable, because the loss lands on your funded account rather than on our fee.

Two related points belong here. First, the funded account must be in your name with payouts to you — anything else turns a service into an unauthorised managed account, which most firms prohibit outright. Second, identity verification has to be completed by you, personally; a provider cannot and should not do it on your behalf.

Where the terms are ambiguous, the honest move is to ask the firm in writing before buying the evaluation. A support reply that permits third-party trading on the evaluation is worth having, and worth saving. The other side

What the fee does not remove

A page that lists only advantages is marketing. Four risks are real, and you should weigh all of them before paying anyone, including us.

Terms risk. The firm may prohibit third-party trading, or may change its terms between the pass and the payout. The first is checkable in advance; the second is not fully, which is why the funded account should be treated as a separate risk from the evaluation.

Provider risk. The fee is paid before the work is done. A provider that disappears, or quietly stops reporting, leaves you with a failed attempt and no recourse unless the payment method is traceable. This is the risk the free demo exists to reduce.

Concentration risk. The provider is trading several evaluations, possibly the same firm, instrument and direction. Firms that treat correlated positions across accounts as one exposure may treat that as a breach, which is another reason the terms check comes first.

Skill risk. A trader who passes by service has not practised the evaluation. The funded account still needs a plan, and the first month of a funded account is not the place to learn one. The complete passing guide covers the plan; it is worth reading even when someone else trades the test.

Expectation risk. The service produces a funded account, not income. A client expecting the fee to convert directly into withdrawals has misunderstood the product, and that misunderstanding is the industry's most common complaint. The honest question is not which provider is cheapest, but which one will tell you in writing when the answer is no.
