The process

What exactly happens after I pay?

The firm's rulebook is written into a one-page plan covering target, daily loss limit, maximum loss, drawdown type, minimum trading days, consistency rules and instrument restrictions. A risk plan follows from those numbers: maximum risk per trade, maximum trades per session, and the conditions under which trading stops for the day. Then the evaluation is traded, with progress logged session by session.

How long does it take to pass?

On average around 14 days for a two-phase forex evaluation with no time limit. Futures evaluations with trailing thresholds and minimum trading days typically run two to four weeks. A timeline is agreed before the attempt starts and updated if a firm's rules complicate it.

Can you pass it in a few days?

Only if the firm's rules allow it. Many firms require a minimum number of trading days before verification, and racing to a target inside the daily loss budget usually means gambling rather than executing. If an aggressive timeline conflicts with the rules, the timeline is declined.

How much communication do I get during the evaluation?

Session-by-session logging with four numbers: closing equity, distance from the failure floor, percentage of target completed, and trading days used. You see the log rather than a curated summary, including flat and losing sessions.

Do I need to do anything during the evaluation?

No. The account must remain funded and in good standing, and you should avoid logging in and trading it yourself, which would be an obvious breach of the firm's rules and would confuse the risk plan.

How is the funded account handed over?

The credentials are passed to you directly, along with the funded-phase rules summary — minimum trading days, payout thresholds, consistency checks and KYC requirements — so you know what the first withdrawal requires before you start trading.

Do I need a trading platform installed?

Not for the evaluation, since the trading happens on our side. You will need access to whichever platform your firm uses — MT4, MT5, cTrader, TradingView, Tradovate, NinjaTrader and similar — for the funded phase.

The log is the record of progress. Live screen sharing is not part of the standard service, mainly because it invites second-guessing of the plan mid-session, which damages the discipline the plan depends on.

Failures and retries

What happens if the evaluation fails?

The attempt is reset on the same firm and account size with no additional service fee. Retries are covered because a per-attempt fee would make failure profitable for us, which is the wrong incentive for a service built on rule discipline.

How often do evaluations fail?

Roughly one in twenty managed evaluations ends in a rule breach and is reset. Failures cluster around firm-specific edge cases — an ambiguous trailing rule, a news restriction applied differently than published, a platform outage during an open position — rather than around strategy.

Do I have to pay the firm's fee again after a failure?

That depends on the firm. Some offer a free reset with a coupon or within a time window; others charge a reset or a full new challenge fee. That cost sits with the firm and is outside our fee.

What counts as a failed attempt?

A breach of any published rule that ends the account: daily loss limit, maximum loss or trailing threshold, consistency requirement, prohibited trading during restricted periods, or an unpermitted instrument. A flat or losing week that stays inside the limits is not a failure.

Can an evaluation be failed by a firm's mistake?

It happens occasionally — a payout or drawdown calculation that contradicts the published rules. In those cases the firm is contacted with the session log, and most firms correct genuine platform errors. The log exists partly for this reason.

Is there a limit on how many retries I get?

Managed evaluations are traded until the account is passed or the client decides to stop. In practice, repeat failures on the same ruleset indicate that the ruleset itself is the problem, and a different firm is recommended instead.

Choosing a firm when you plan to use a passing service

Not every evaluation is equally suitable for third-party execution, and the differences are structural rather than a matter of opinion. Four criteria matter more than account size or marketing offers.

1. Drawdown type

A static maximum loss is the most forgiving structure, because the failure level never moves. An end-of-day trailing drawdown is the next most workable: the floor follows closed profit, so intraday noise does not tighten it. An intraday trailing threshold is the hardest, because every unrealised gain drags the failure level upward and a normal pullback can end the account even while the day's net result is positive. If two firms are otherwise equal, the one with the static floor is the safer assignment.

2. Consistency rules and when they apply

Some firms apply a consistency requirement during the evaluation, some only at payout, and some not at all. A rule that applies at payout is more forgiving, because the distribution of profit can be adjusted by adding additional winning days before requesting a withdrawal. A rule that applies during the evaluation shortens the time you have to fix a lopsided distribution.

3. Time limits and minimum trading days

No time limit is better than a thirty-day window, for an obvious reason: a flat week costs time rather than the account. Minimum trading days cut the other way — they prevent a fast pass from being eligible for funding, which is why the log tracks days used as carefully as it tracks profit.

4. Terms on third-party trading

This is the criterion that decides whether the arrangement is appropriate at all. Firms that explicitly require the account holder to place the trades make a passing service a poor bet regardless of how favourable the evaluation rules look. Checking the current terms — not a review from two years ago — is part of the decision, not an afterthought.

How to read a prop firm rulebook in fifteen minutes

Most of the questions on this page reduce to one skill: reading a firm's terms and extracting the four numbers that decide whether an evaluation is forgiving or brutal. Once that skill is in place, the answers stop being firm-specific and start being arithmetic.

Minute 1–3: find the loss limits and identify the measurement point

Every rulebook states a daily loss limit and a maximum loss. The question that decides everything is what each is measured against. Daily limits are usually measured on equity including floating positions, which makes them stricter than they appear; maximum loss may be measured from the starting balance, from current balance, or from peak equity on a trailing basis. A 10% maximum loss measured from a static starting balance and a 10% trailing maximum loss are not the same rule with different wording — they are two different challenges, and the trailing version is materially harder near the target.

Minute 4–6: check what resets the trailing figure

Where a maximum loss trails, the next question is when it updates. Intradaily trailing moves with every tick of unrealised profit, which means a position that goes into profit and comes back can raise the failure level without the trader ever banking anything. End-of-day trailing updates once, at the close, on realised balance. The difference is dramatic on a volatile instrument and it is the single most under-read line in the entire document.

Minute 7–9: convert the target into trades

Divide the target by your per-trade risk to get the target in R. A 10% target with 1.5% risk is roughly 6.7R; at 3% risk it is 3.3R. That single division tells you how much of the challenge is analysis and how much is patience, and it explains why traders who size aggressively report fast passes and frequent resets in the same breath.

Minute 10–12: read the payout section as though it were the challenge

For most firms the payout terms contain more traps than the evaluation. Minimum trading days, a profit threshold before the first withdrawal, a consistency requirement on the best day, a processing schedule, and sometimes a cap on how much can be withdrawn at once. A trader who passes cleanly and then discovers a 30% consistency rule on a 40% best day has not been funded in any useful sense — they have been given an account they must keep trading before they can be paid.

Minute 13–15: check the prohibitions and the enforcement clause

The last section lists what is banned: third-party trading, account sharing, certain strategies such as high-frequency arbitrage or latency exploitation, sometimes holding through specific news events. The important part is not the list but the enforcement clause — whether breaches are detected during the evaluation or after funding, and whether the remedy is a reset or a forfeit. A prohibition enforced after funding is the most expensive kind, because it arrives when the trader has the most to lose.

What to do when two sections contradict each other

They often do. A marketing page will say "no time limit" while the terms say something narrower, or a headline will describe a static drawdown while a footnote describes a trailing one. The resolution is to ask the firm in writing and keep the reply. Where no reply comes, or the reply is evasive, that is itself the answer: an unclear rulebook becomes a dispute at exactly the moment a trader cannot afford one.

Putting the four numbers on one card

The output of a fifteen-minute read should be a single index card, because a rulebook that lives in memory is a rulebook that gets misremembered on the day it matters. The card carries the daily loss budget in dollars, the current distance to the failure level, the target expressed as a number of R multiples at the chosen per-trade risk, and any constraint that applies at payout rather than during the evaluation. Three of those four numbers change as equity moves, which is why the card is rewritten each morning rather than laminated once.

There is a second use for the card: it makes a rulebook comparison possible without reading two full documents side by side. Two firms whose cards differ in only one line are comparable assignments; two whose cards differ in three are different puzzles, and picking between them on fee alone is picking between difficulties at random. That is the practical reason this site keeps returning to the same four variables — not because they are the only things in a rulebook, but because they are the only things that reliably change an outcome.

The card also settles arguments with yourself. When a session is going badly, the temptation is to renegotiate the plan — a slightly larger position, a slightly wider stop, one more trade to get back to flat. A written number removes the negotiation, because the budget was decided by someone calmer than the person currently looking at the chart.

One warning about the card, since it is a tool rather than a guarantee. Firms revise terms, and a card written three months ago may describe a challenge that no longer exists. The card is dated for that reason, and the date is the first thing to check when a result seems to contradict the rules as remembered. A stale card is worse than no card, because it produces confident errors rather than cautious ones.

One last habit worth adding to the fifteen minutes: keep the firm's reply to your written question. Rules that arrive by email are the only version of a rulebook that cannot be revised later without someone noticing, and the reply costs one message.
