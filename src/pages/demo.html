How the seven days work

The trial is deliberately simple, because the point is to remove the reason not to check. There is no application form, no sales call required, and no payment step hidden at the end.

  1. Open a demo at a broker you already use. Any broker offering MT4, MT5 or cTrader will do. Most let you create a demo in a couple of minutes from the platform or the client area. Choose the account size you would eventually want to pass — $50,000 or $100,000 is a sensible test size, because the arithmetic is the same as a $200,000 account scaled down.
  2. Send us the demo login. Broker name, server, account number and password. That is all. You do not need to fund anything, and a demo cannot be funded anyway.
  3. We write the plan the same day. Before a single trade, you get the four numbers that govern the week: risk per trade, the daily stop, the weekly target, and the point at which we stop and reassess. The plan is written for the demo's balance, not copied from another account.
  4. We trade it for seven days. You keep the login, so you can open the platform at any time and see the positions, the equity and the history for yourself. Nothing is hidden behind a screenshot.
  5. You get a written summary at the end. What was traded, what the plan allowed, where the week went badly, and what the same sequence would have looked like against a prop firm's daily and maximum loss rules.
  6. You decide. Continue with a paid evaluation, ask about the smaller trial challenge, or stop. There is no follow-up campaign and no obligation.

Seven days is short on purpose. It is long enough to include a losing day, a news release and a decision to do nothing — the three situations where a service either follows a process or improvises. A month would tell you more about a strategy and less about whether the person trading it is disciplined, which is the thing you are actually buying.

Why we offer this at all. The passing-service market runs on screenshots, and a screenshot cannot show you how a provider behaves on a bad day. A week of live demo trades can. It also costs us nothing except time, which is the right thing to risk before asking a client to risk $220 and a challenge fee.

What the demo proves, and what it does not

It is worth being precise, because a demo is often oversold by services that want credit for a funded-looking equity curve.

What you can judge

  • Pacing. Whether the account is pushed toward the target or worked there over several sessions.
  • Position size. Whether risk per trade is constant and derived from the stop, or changes with confidence.
  • Behaviour on a losing day. Whether the daily stop is respected or quietly widened.
  • Reporting. Whether the explanation after the fact matches what the account actually shows.
  • Communication. How quickly questions are answered during the week, not just before payment.

What it cannot prove

  • A prop firm pass. A demo has no daily loss breach, no verification stage and no minimum trading days.
  • Execution on the firm's server. Spreads, slippage and order handling on a prop firm feed differ from a broker demo.
  • The funded phase. Passing and keeping a funded account are different jobs, and the demo tests neither payout rules nor consistency at payout.
  • Future results. Seven days is a sample of process, not a forecast.

The honest way to use the trial is as a filter, not as proof. If the week looks like a plan being followed, the paid evaluation is a reasonable next step. If the trades look improvised, or the answers to your questions are vague, the demo has done its job by costing you a week instead of a fee.

One practical note: choose a demo with a balance close to the account size you are considering. A $10,000 demo traded at 0.01 lots tells you very little about how a $100,000 evaluation would be handled, because the position sizing arithmetic is different. The plan is scalable, but the sample is more useful at a realistic size.

What to send, and what never to send

A demo login is low-risk by design, but the habit matters. Send the broker name, the server, the demo account number and the password. Change the password at the end of the week, or ask us to confirm that access has stopped — both are fine.

Never send live account credentials, a bank statement, card details or identity documents for a demo trial. None of those are needed, and a provider that asks for them before a demo has told you something important about how it handles client data. The same rule applies to prop firm accounts later: the evaluation login is the only credential the work requires, and the funded login should never be handed over.
