Apex Trader Funding rules, targets and payouts

Apex is the futures evaluation most traders meet second, after Topstep, and it is the one where a good account most often dies for a reason that has nothing to do with trading skill: the loss threshold follows the peak, and it does so in two different ways depending on which product was bought. This page states the parameters in dollars, separates the end-of-day model from the intraday model, and works through what each one does to a plan in its final third.

Apex rules at a glance

  • Evaluation: a single-phase evaluation sold in several products, with the drawdown model chosen by product.
  • Loss threshold: $1,000 to $4,000 depending on account size, and it rises with the high-water mark.
  • Trail model: end-of-day on the standard plans, intraday on the intraday-trailing ones — two different accounts in practice.
  • Daily loss limit: $500 to $2,000 on the end-of-day plans.
  • Payout condition: the best day may not exceed 50% of profit on current accounts, 30% on legacy ones.
  • Sizes: sold up to $300,000 per account.
  • Verdict in one line: the account does not fail on a bad trade, it fails when the peak moves and the plan does not.

What Apex actually sells

Apex Trader Funding is a futures evaluation firm. Its product is not a brokerage account and not a funded account in the ordinary sense: it is a one-phase test in which a trader must reach a fixed dollar profit target while keeping equity above a loss threshold that rises as the account makes new highs. Pass the test and a funded account follows, with its own payout conditions. Fail it, and the fee is spent — there is no refund for a rule breach, which is why the breaching mechanism deserves more attention than the target does.

The structural difference from a two-phase forex evaluation is worth stating plainly, because it changes the shape of the job. A two-phase plan asks for a percentage twice, with a static floor underneath both attempts. Apex asks for a dollar figure once, but the floor climbs. On a two-phase plan the risk to the account is roughly constant; on Apex the risk rises with every good day, so the evaluation gets tighter as it gets closer to the end. That inversion is the single most important fact on this page.

Everything else — the account sizes, the daily loss limit, the payout consistency rule — is a modifier on that basic geometry. Read the trailing behaviour first and the numbers second, because a trader who understands the trail can price any size, and a trader who has only memorised targets is guessing.

Two trails, one brand: the choice that decides the account

Apex runs two drawdown conventions, and they behave so differently that they should be treated as separate firms. The end-of-day model recalculates the threshold once a day, at the close. The intraday model recalculates continuously, against equity that includes open positions.

The consequence of the intraday version is easy to state and easy to underestimate: an open winner raises the failure level before that winner is booked. A position that runs $1,800 in profit lifts the floor by up to $1,800; if the trade then reverses and closes at breakeven, the floor does not come back down. The trader has kept nothing and lost room. On the end-of-day version the same sequence is invisible, because the close is what counts.

This is not a small technicality to be checked at the end of the reading. It decides position size on every trade. On an intraday account the maximum adverse excursion of an open trade is what the floor measures, so a method that routinely gives back half of its open profit is paying for that habit twice: once in the give-back, and again in permanently reduced room.

If you already know which product you are buying, the rest of this page still applies; if you have not chosen, choose on this axis rather than on the price difference, which is usually small next to the cost of running the wrong model.

The arithmetic of a floor that only goes up

Suppose the threshold on the size you are buying is $3,000 and the target is $6,000 across a $50,000 account. Both figures are illustrative here — substitute your own from the plan page — but the arithmetic is what matters.

Day one, equity is $50,000 and the failure level is $47,000. Room is $3,000. If you risk $400 a trade, seven consecutive losers end the account, which is a comfortable buffer for a method with a normal losing streak. Now suppose the account reaches $53,000. On a trailing model the floor has moved to $50,000, and room is still $3,000 — but room measured from a much higher equity, and with $6,000 of target still $3,000 away, you have made half the target and kept the same absolute risk. That sounds neutral. It is not, because the number of trades required to cover the remaining distance is now smaller than the number required to cover the first half, and each of those trades still carries $400 of downside against a fixed $3,000 of room.

The ratio that governs everything is remaining room divided by remaining target. Early in the evaluation it is generous; at $58,000 with a $6,000 target, room from the peak is $3,000 and remaining target is $1,000, a ratio of three to one against you. That is the moment at which full size stops being defensible. The evaluation has not become harder because the market changed; it has become harder because the distance left is short and the room is the same $3,000 it always was, sitting under an equity that is now much closer to the failure level than it looks.

The practical answer is a size taper. Risk the full per-trade amount until roughly two thirds of target, then cut by a third, then by half in the final stretch. It feels like giving up speed exactly when the finish is visible, and it is the only version of the plan that survives a normal adverse sequence near the end.

Why the last third takes as long as the first half

Because the target is fixed in dollars and the floor rises, the ratio of room to remaining target degrades monotonically. A trader who does not taper finishes the first two thirds in a week and then spends another week protecting a shrinking margin, occasionally losing it. A trader who does taper takes longer overall and arrives.

There is a second, less obvious reason: after a certain point, a good day is genuinely bad news. On an intraday account, a strong session can lift the floor to within a normal stop of the current price, and then the next ordinary loss ends the evaluation without any rule being broken and without any reckless trade being placed. That outcome is indistinguishable, in the account history, from a blow-up — and it is far more common.

The daily loss limit, and where it applies

On Apex's end-of-day plans there is a fixed dollar daily loss limit published by size, in the $500–$2,000 range. On intraday plans the trailing floor does the work instead, which has a specific and rather harsh meaning: a large intraday loss can end the account before any daily figure would have been reached, because the floor is live and the loss is measured as it happens.

For the trader, the operational difference is that on an EOD plan the day has a hard stop that is knowable in advance, and a bad session costs a day; on an intraday plan a bad session can cost the account. That difference should be reflected in the per-trade risk, not only in the session stop. If a single trade can move equity by enough to breach a live floor, the trade is oversized regardless of what the percentage says.

A workable allocation on a $3,000 threshold: $250 per trade, a $750 session stop, and a $1,000 reserve that is treated as untouchable. That leaves room for four losing trades in a session without approaching the floor, and it means a three-day losing streak is survivable without a single change of method. Traders who skip the reserve are the ones who discover the limit exists on the day they meet it.

The 50% consistency rule at payout

The consistency term is enforced at the payout stage rather than during the evaluation, which is why it does not appear in the list of reasons an attempt fails and still ruins attempts. The requirement is that no single best day may account for more than half of the profit being withdrawn. Legacy accounts carry a 30% version, which is materially stricter.

Read carefully: this is not a rule about how you trade, it is a rule about the shape of the profit you present. An account passed in two large sessions can satisfy the target and fail the payout test, leaving a funded account that cannot withdraw cleanly until more ordinary days are added to the total. The 50% and 30% versions therefore have a hidden cost: the 30% one requires at least four roughly comparable profitable days before the withdrawal becomes straightforward, and a trader who never checked which version they hold will find that out at the worst moment.

The response is to plan the pass as a curve rather than a spike — not because a spike is against the rules, but because a spike creates an account whose first withdrawal is blocked by arithmetic. If your method genuinely produces occasional large days, the honest options are to accept a slower first payout or to add ordinary days deliberately after the target is reached. Both are legitimate; discovering the constraint after the fact is not.

Choosing an account size

Apex publishes sizes up to $300,000, and the temptation is to buy the largest one that is affordable, because the dollar figures look like leverage on your skill. The correct way to choose is the opposite: pick the size whose threshold you can trade without changing the method you already use.

Work it in one direction only. Take your average risk per trade as a percentage of account equity — the number your own records produce, not the one you would like — and check how many consecutive losses the threshold absorbs. If the answer is fewer than six, the size is too large for the way you trade, and the evaluation is measuring luck rather than method. A $4,000 threshold on a large account and a $1,000 threshold on a small one can be equally demanding if the per-trade risk scales with the size, which it usually does.

The second check is the daily limit relative to the target. If the target requires more than about a third of the daily budget per session to finish in a reasonable number of days, the plan is asking for a pace that no bad session can tolerate. A smaller account with a target that can be reached at a fifth of the daily figure per day is a better instrument than a larger one that has to be rushed.

The third check is the fee, and it is the one traders skip. Every size carries a purchase price and, on some products, a recurring element; the only accurate figure is the one on the checkout page at the moment of buying, because Apex discounts frequently and the sticker price is not the price. Compare the fee against the target, not against other firms' fees, since the target is what you are actually buying the right to attempt.

Fees, billing and what the invoice does not tell you

There is no honest way to publish a single Apex price on a page like this, because the firm's promotional cadence means the number changes by the week and by the account size. What can be said without hedging is what to look for: whether the fee buys one attempt or a reset, whether a monthly or recurring charge attaches after the evaluation begins, and what happens to the fee if a rule is breached on day two rather than day twenty.

Those three questions decide the real cost of an attempt far more than the headline price does. A cheap evaluation with no reset is more expensive than a dearer one that includes a second attempt if your historical pass rate is under fifty percent, which for most traders it is. And a monthly charge that quietly accrues while an evaluation sits half-finished changes the arithmetic of pacing: it rewards finishing, which is exactly the pressure that makes people rush the final third and lose the account.

The funded stage is a different job

Passing is one gate; the funded account has its own threshold, its own payout schedule and its own consistency check. The most common mistake in the transition is to treat the funded account as a victory lap and increase size, on the theory that the money is now real and the evaluation is over. Structurally the opposite is true: the funded account usually keeps a live floor, so it is still an account that can be ended by a normal adverse sequence, and the payout conditions add a second set of gates that do not exist during the test.

Three things change in ways that matter. First, the objective shifts from reaching a target to producing a withdrawal, which is a different optimisation: a payout requires a qualifying profit that survives the consistency test, not merely a good equity curve. Second, the first payout typically requires days that qualify, so calendar time becomes part of the plan in a way it was not during the evaluation. Third, and most dangerous, the psychological permission to risk more arrives at exactly the moment the rules became no more forgiving.

The funded plan that works is boring: keep the session cap that produced the pass, keep the per-trade risk, and treat the first payout as the objective rather than the balance. The first payout timeline sets out how the qualifying days sequence in practice, and the payout denial cases are worth reading before rather than after, because the refusal reasons are almost always visible in the account history in advance.

Who Apex suits — and who should buy a two-step instead

Apex suits a trader who thinks in dollars per contract, who is comfortable cutting size as the account grows, and whose method produces a steady sequence of moderate sessions rather than occasional large ones. It particularly suits someone who has already failed a trailing evaluation elsewhere and has learned the taper, because that lesson transfers directly and is the main skill the product requires.

It is a poor fit for three styles. A method that depends on letting a winner run through a wide excursion will be punished on an intraday account, because the floor tracks the excursion. A method built on a small number of large sessions will struggle at the payout stage even after a clean pass. And a trader who cannot resist increasing size after a good week is buying an instrument designed to end that behaviour expensively — a static-floor two-step plan such as FTMO is the more honest purchase, because it removes the mechanism rather than asking for discipline against it.

That last comparison is the one worth making explicitly, since both firms come up in the same search. Apex asks for one target and charges a rising floor; FTMO asks for two targets and keeps the floor still. If you have failed a trailing account twice, the second model is not a compromise, it is the correct product for the evidence you have.

Seven ways an Apex attempt ends

None of these is a market opinion that turned out wrong. Each is a rule event, visible in the account history before it happened, and six of the seven have nothing to do with the target.

  • The floor catches an open winner. On an intraday account, a position that ran $1,500 and returned to breakeven lifted the threshold permanently. The loss was recorded as zero; the room was not.
  • A normal retracement after a strong week. Three good days raised the floor close to the current equity. The fourth day's ordinary pullback ended the account.
  • Size increased near the finish. With 85% of the target reached, the trader doubled up to finish quickly. One stop was enough, because remaining room was small by then.
  • The daily limit was crossed on an EOD plan while trying to recover a two-loss morning, converting a recoverable day into a breach.
  • Overnight exposure against a dollar figure. A gap moved equity through the threshold while no session was open, and no session stop can defend a level that is crossed while the market is closed.
  • A payout blocked, not a pass failed. The account reached the target in two dominant days and the best-day test refused the withdrawal. The evaluation was never the obstacle.
  • The wrong product was bought. The trader planned an end-of-day trail and received an intraday one, and the position sizing that worked on paper was too large for the floor that actually existed.

Five of those seven are planning failures, one is a product-selection failure, and one is a payout-rule failure. Not one is a failure to predict the market. That ratio is why this page spends its length on geometry instead of setups.

A worked ten-session plan

Take an account with a $3,000 threshold and a $6,000 target, purely as arithmetic. Divide the target by ten sessions: $600 a session. That is the pace, and the first thing to check is whether $600 a session can be produced at a per-trade risk that the threshold tolerates.

At $250 risk and a two-to-one reward expectation, three winning trades produce roughly $1,500 and one full losing day costs $750 against the session stop. Ten sessions at that rhythm finish the target with room to spare, and the account never approaches the floor early on. The taper starts at $4,000 of profit: risk drops to $175, then to $125 in the final $1,000. Total return falls, and the probability of arriving rises.

Now stress it. Suppose the first four sessions are all losing — $1,000 down, floor at $47,000, room $2,000 from a $49,000 equity. The plan still works, but the pace must be recalculated: $7,000 is needed from six sessions, which is not achievable at $250 risk without a much better hit rate. The correct response is not to double size; it is to extend the horizon, because the fee is already spent and calendar time is the cheapest resource available. Traders who cannot extend the horizon are the ones who buy the account twice.

What to confirm before you pay

Five questions, all answerable in writing before the fee leaves your account. Getting these wrong is the difference between a fair test and an unwinnable one.

  • Which drawdown model, exactly. EOD or intraday, named on the product you are buying. These are different products with the same brand, and a plan built for one is unsafe on the other.
  • The parameters for your size. Target, threshold and daily limit are published per size. A figure from a $50,000 plan does not describe a $150,000 one, and the ranges on this page are not a substitute for the plan page.
  • Whether the threshold ever stops rising. Some trailing models cap the trail at the starting balance, some do not. Where it stops determines whether the final stretch is merely tight or genuinely hostile.
  • Which consistency version applies. 50% or the 30% legacy figure, and at which stage it is tested. The answer changes how many ordinary days a payout needs.
  • The third-party trading clause. Apex's published rules describe how an account is scored, not who may place the trades. Whether another person may trade your evaluation is a separate permission, and it decides whether a passed account can ever pay out. See the risk disclosure for how we check that in writing.

One more, which is not a rule but a record: keep the plan page and the checkout page as they stood on the day you bought. Firms revise parameters, and when a figure is disputed six weeks later the only version that matters is the one attached to your purchase.

Frequently asked

Is the Apex drawdown trailing or static?

Trailing, in both of the firm's published versions: end-of-day trailing on EOD plans and intraday trailing on the others. The threshold rises with the peak and does not come back down. A static floor is a different product entirely and is the reason some traders pass at FTMO and fail here with an unchanged method.

How much is the Apex maximum loss?

Published as a dollar figure by account size — $1,000, $2,000, $3,000 or $4,000 across the range — rather than as a percentage. Confirm the figure for the size you are buying on the plan page, because a threshold quoted for one size does not describe another.

Does Apex have a daily loss limit?

On EOD plans there is a fixed dollar daily limit published by size, in the $500–$2,000 range. On intraday plans the trailing floor does the work instead, which means a large intraday loss can end the account before any daily figure is reached.

What is the Apex 50% consistency rule?

At payout, no single best day may account for more than half of the profit being withdrawn. Legacy accounts carry a 30% version, which is stricter and requires more comparable profitable days before a withdrawal is straightforward. The rule does not stop a pass; it stops a lumpy pass from becoming a payout.

Can I pass an Apex evaluation in a few days?

The target can be reached quickly at a large size, and that is usually how accounts are lost rather than passed. A fast pass also tends to produce a dominant day, which then collides with the consistency test at payout. A paced pass over one to three weeks is slower and considerably more likely to convert.

What happens if I breach the threshold on an open position?

On an intraday account the breach is live, so an open position can take the account out without the trade ever being closed. On an end-of-day account the measurement happens at the close. That difference should decide your maximum position size, not just your stop placement.

Which account size should I buy?

The largest one whose threshold absorbs at least six consecutive losses at your real per-trade risk. If the threshold survives fewer than that, the size is too big for the method and the evaluation is testing luck. Larger sizes are not easier; they are simply bigger, with proportionally identical geometry.

Is the Apex fee refundable if I fail?

A rule breach ends the attempt and the fee is spent. Resets and second attempts are product- and promotion-specific, so the checkout is the only document that states what a purchase includes.

Do I need a minimum number of trading days?

Apex's evaluation is built around a dollar target rather than a day count, so the calendar is not the binding constraint in the way it is on a plan with a four- or five-day minimum. The binding constraints here are the threshold and, at payout, the consistency shape.

Can Apex rules change while my evaluation is running?

Firms revise parameters, and the version attached to your purchase is the one that governs. Keep the plan page and checkout page as they stood on the day you bought, and treat any figure you read elsewhere as provisional until you have matched it to your own account documents.

Apex or Topstep for a first futures evaluation?

Topstep's Combine publishes an optional daily limit and a 55% best-day expectation during the test, while Apex applies its consistency test at payout instead. A trader who wants the constraint visible during the evaluation tends to prefer Topstep; a trader who wants one dollar target and is confident about pacing tends to prefer Apex. The Topstep rulebook covers the other side of that comparison in the same detail.

Can someone else trade my Apex evaluation?

That is a separate question from every rule on this page, and the answer is in the firm's terms rather than in its evaluation parameters. We check the clause in writing before accepting a job and decline where third-party trading is prohibited. Read the risk disclosure before assuming a service can operate on your account.
