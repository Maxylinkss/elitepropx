Verified against The5ers' High Stakes plan

The5ers High Stakes rules, targets and payouts

High Stakes publishes numbers that look like half the market: ten percent, then five, a static floor at ten and a five percent daily limit. The rule that separates it from every plan beside it is a single word in the day requirement — the minimum is three profitable days, not three trading days. That word turns a calendar requirement into a run-rate requirement, and it is the most commonly misread line in this rulebook.

High Stakes rules at a glance

  • Evaluation: the High Stakes two-phase plan, one product among several with different targets.
  • Targets: 10% then 5% of the starting balance.
  • Maximum loss: a static 10% floor, fixed from the first day.
  • Daily loss: 5% of the starting balance per session.
  • Minimum days: three profitable days — a run-rate requirement, not a calendar one.
  • Consistency: none stated beyond the profitable-day requirement.
  • Verdict in one line: normal-looking numbers with one word that changes the schedule, and the schedule is where attempts fail.

What High Stakes is

The5ers is a forex and CFD evaluation firm, and High Stakes is one of its two-phase programmes. The structure is conventional at the surface: a ten percent first target, a five percent second target, a static maximum loss of ten percent measured from the starting balance, a five percent daily loss limit, and a minimum number of qualifying days per phase.

The firm runs more than one programme, and they are not interchangeable: targets, day counts and sometimes a consistency term differ between them. Everything on this page describes High Stakes specifically, and the plan name — not the brand — is what the figures belong to. A reader who buys a different programme and applies these numbers will be planning against a rulebook they do not hold.

Two features deserve a warning before the table. The first is the profitable-day minimum, which behaves nothing like a trading-day minimum and is the subject of the longest section below. The second is that no consistency clause is stated on the plan we read, which is a statement about disclosure rather than a guarantee about payout conditions — the funded stage has its own terms, and they are the ones enforced when money moves.

Three profitable days is not three days

A trading-day minimum counts any day on which a position was opened. A profitable-day minimum counts only the days that finished in the green. The distinction sounds academic until it is applied to a real week: a trader who needs three green days and has a fifty percent hit rate should plan for six sessions, and a trader with a worse hit rate should plan for more.

The arithmetic is unforgiving in a specific way, because losing days do not merely fail to help — they consume calendar. Need three green days and lose the first two, and the earliest possible completion moves out by two sessions plus however long the three wins take. The phase has no fixed length at all; it has a length determined by the trader's consistency, which is the one variable the rulebook refuses to fix.

This is what makes the plan harder than its headline numbers suggest. Ten and five with a static floor is unremarkable; almost every two-step plan publishes something similar. The profitable-day rule converts a target problem into a run-rate problem, and a run-rate problem cannot be solved with size. Doubling risk does not produce green days faster, it produces them less often, because the variance of the daily result rises while the expected value stays the same.

The style that fits is therefore narrow and specific: a method that produces many small positive sessions rather than a few large ones. A trader who takes two trades a week with a wide target will find three green days take three weeks. A trader who takes a handful of setups a day and finishes most sessions slightly up will satisfy the rule in under a week and never notice it existed.

Before buying, run the rule against your own records rather than your intuition. Count the proportion of your last fifty sessions that closed positive. If that number is above sixty percent, three green days cost you roughly five sessions and the plan is comfortable. If it is below forty, the minimum is the binding constraint on the whole evaluation and no amount of target planning will change that.

What "profitable" actually means

There is a question hiding inside the word that the plan summary does not answer: does a session that ends up twelve dollars count as profitable, or is there a minimum gain? Some firms count any green close, others require a threshold, and the difference decides whether a plan built on micro-sessions is viable or foolish.

The practical response is to ask support in writing before paying, and to phrase it as a scenario rather than a definition: if a session closes up twelve dollars on a hundred thousand dollar account, does that count as one of the three? The answer is either useful or evasive, and both are informative.

While waiting for the answer, plan as though a token green day does not count. A day that produces a meaningful fraction of the daily pace — say at least half a percent on the account — is profitable under any reasonable reading and carries no risk of being disqualified after the fact. Building the plan around the strict interpretation costs a little pace and removes an entire category of unpleasant surprise.

The static floor suits a wider method

Ten percent maximum loss measured from the starting balance does not move as the account grows. A $7,000 gain leaves the failure level exactly where it was, so profit is genuine progress rather than an exchange for a tighter wire. For a method with meaningful retracements — a swing approach, a wider stop, a strategy that tolerates a drawdown within a session — that is the most valuable structural feature on the page.

It also changes the finish. On a trailing account the last twenty percent of the target is the most dangerous stretch, because every new high raises the floor and narrows the buffer. Here the last twenty percent is the safest part: the target is close and the room beneath the account has never been larger relative to the distance left. Nothing forces a size taper near the end, and nothing punishes a normal pullback.

What it does not do is make oversizing survivable. Ten percent is ten percent, and a trader risking two percent a trade still ends the account in five consecutive losses. The floor removes one failure mechanism — the rising threshold — and leaves the other one intact. Traders who arrive from a futures evaluation often over-read the generosity: this plan will not take the account from an open winner, but it will take it from a run of ordinary losers at the wrong size.

The cost of a static floor is usually a longer path. Nothing forces the pace, so the pace has to be chosen, and a plan without a forced rhythm tends to drift toward either impatience or inertia. That is why the run-rate analysis above matters more here than on a plan where the rules impose the schedule.

The daily limit, and how to spend it

Five percent a day is $5,000 on a $100,000 account. Split into three tolerable losses it is about $1,650 a trade; split into four it is $1,250. That conversion is the whole of position sizing on this plan, and it should be done before the first session rather than during a bad one.

The reason the split matters more here than on a plan with a forced pace is the profitable-day rule. A trader who has spent most of the daily budget recovering a loss finishes the day flat, which is not a profitable day, which means the calendar has advanced and the count has not. The daily limit and the day requirement interact: big recovery days produce neutral closes, and neutral closes are worthless under this rulebook.

The corrective is a session stop at half the budget and a session profit cap that is modest — a fraction of the daily pace rather than a multiple of it. Ending a green day early is not a sacrifice on a plan that counts green days; it is the objective. A trader who banks half a percent and stops has produced one of the three required sessions. A trader who presses for two percent and gives back to breakeven has produced nothing at all.

The calendar is the real budget

Every other plan on this site budgets money: a target in dollars, a daily limit in dollars, a floor in dollars. High Stakes budgets time as well, and the two budgets behave differently. Money is spent and can be earned back; calendar time is spent and cannot. A session that ends flat has cost a day and bought nothing, and under a profitable-day rule that is a real loss even though the balance did not move.

This is why the plan should be laid out as a calendar before it is laid out as a P&L. If the method produces a green close on six sessions out of ten, then three green days take five sessions on average, and a phase needing several of those cycles takes weeks. Laying that out in advance does two useful things: it makes the fee's exposure duration visible, and it removes the surprise that arrives on day nine when the target is met and the count is not.

There is a second consequence that changes tactics rather than scheduling. On a plan where the count matters, a session that is up half a percent should often be closed rather than pressed. The money argument for pressing is that more profit shortens the phase; the calendar argument against it is that a give-back converts a counted day into an uncounted one, and the phase lengthens by a whole session. Once the arithmetic is written down, closing a modest green day stops feeling like timidity and starts looking like the cheapest available progress.

The opposite habit is equally costly. A trader who is down on the day and trades back to breakeven has spent the session, produced no counted day, and taken risk to get there. Under a target-only rule that is neutral; here it is a wasted session with a real chance of becoming a breached one. The correct response to an early loss is to stop and take the uncounted day, because the count is already lost and the budget is not.

One practical device makes this manageable: keep a simple tally beside the platform — green days counted, sessions elapsed, target remaining. Two of those three numbers are invisible in the platform's own statistics, and the phase is governed by all three. The rule checklist generator produces a sheet with the cash figures for your size, and the tally can be added by hand in a minute.

Phase two is the short version of phase one

The second phase asks for five percent instead of ten, under the same floor, the same daily limit and the same profitable-day requirement. Most traders read that as easier and trade it identically. It is tighter, for a reason that has nothing to do with the target: the five percent daily limit is now equal to the entire phase target, so a single maximum-loss day costs a phase's worth of progress.

The day requirement compounds it. Three green days are needed for a five percent target, which at a realistic pace means three sessions of around 1.7 percent. That is a third of the daily budget per session, which is comfortable — but a losing session in between means four or five sessions, and each of those sessions carries the same risk. Phase two is short and it is not cheap.

The correct adjustment is to cut size by roughly a third and extend the horizon rather than repeat the phase-one plan. Traders who scale up because the target halved are the ones who reach four percent, take one full-size loss, and find themselves needing to repeat the whole five percent with less room than they had at the start.

The funded stage and the first payout

Passing both phases produces a funded account, and the conditions that matter at that point are different from the ones that got you there: a qualifying profit, a number of trading days, and a payout cycle that decides when a request is processed. None of this appears during the evaluation, which is why so many traders arrive at their first withdrawal request without having read the terms that govern it.

The behavioural trap is familiar from every firm. The evaluation taught a size; the funded account tempts a larger one because the money is now real. Structurally nothing has relaxed — the floor and the daily limit are still live — so the correct funded plan is the evaluation plan with the same numbers, written down before the pass rather than improvised after it. The The5ers review covers payout conditions and the funded phase in more detail than this page attempts.

One thing worth checking at the funded stage is whether the consistency position changes. No clause is stated on the evaluation plan, which is not the same as no clause existing at payout. Ask at the point of the first request rather than assuming the evaluation's silence carries over.

Who High Stakes suits

It suits a trader with a high-frequency, small-target method — several setups a day, most sessions closing modestly green — who wants a static floor and does not need the rules to impose a schedule. It suits a swing trader with a wide stop as well, provided the win rate is high enough that green days arrive reliably, because the floor will not punish a retracement.

It is a poor fit for three styles. A trader who takes a handful of large bets a month will find three green days take a month and the fee is exposed for far longer than expected. A trader whose results are lumpy — many small losses and occasional large wins — will pass the target and struggle with the day count simultaneously, because the green sessions are rare. And a trader who recovers losses within the session, producing neutral closes, is actively penalised by a rule that counts only green days.

Six ways a High Stakes attempt ends

  • The daily limit spent on a recovery. Two losses, a size increase, a third loss. The most common failure and the least related to the market.
  • A target reached with too few green days. The profit is there, the calendar is not, and the account stays open with the daily limit live.
  • Neutral closes mistaken for progress. Sessions that end flat count for nothing under a profitable-day rule, and a week of them advances the calendar without advancing the count.
  • Oversizing phase two. With the floor unchanged, a smaller target buys a tighter account rather than a kinder one.
  • Buying the wrong programme. Targets, day counts and consistency terms differ between The5ers products; the brand does not identify the rulebook.
  • A payout condition read late. The funded stage has its own qualifying terms, and the evaluation's silence on consistency is not evidence about them.

Four of the six are consequences of the day rule or of the phase boundary, which is a fair summary of this plan: it rarely punishes a market view and frequently punishes a trader who planned a target without planning a run rate.

A green day is cheaper than a good day

The instinct carried over from target-based plans is that a session should be maximised: if the day is going well, press it. Under a profitable-day rule that instinct is wrong in a specific, quantifiable way.

Suppose a session reaches half a percent and the trader presses for two. The upside is that the target shortens slightly. The downside is that a give-back converts a counted session into an uncounted one, which lengthens the phase by a whole session and re-exposes the account to the daily limit. The trade is roughly a small gain against a large cost, and it is taken at the point where the trader feels most confident — which is precisely when it is least likely to be examined.

The inversion is that a modest green close is not a compromise on this plan, it is the objective stated in the rulebook. Three of them complete a phase, whatever their size, provided each clears whatever threshold the firm applies to "profitable". A trader who banks half a percent a day and stops will finish an evaluation that a trader chasing two percent a day keeps restarting.

That does not mean the target can be ignored: ten percent still has to be earned, and half a percent a day takes twenty green sessions. It means the trade-off between pace and counted days should be made deliberately, at a size the account can defend, rather than resolved in the moment by whichever impulse is strongest.

A worked two-week plan

Take a $100,000 account and a two-week horizon. Phase one needs $10,000 and three green days. At half a percent a day — $500 — ten green sessions are required, which at a sixty percent win rate means around seventeen sessions, or three and a half weeks. That is the honest arithmetic of a small-target plan and it is why the horizon should be set in weeks rather than days.

The alternative is a larger daily target: one percent a day, $1,000, which reaches $10,000 in ten green sessions and takes perhaps sixteen sessions at a sixty percent win rate. One percent of the account is a fifth of the daily budget, so the per-trade risk can sit at $700 to $800 and survive three losses inside a session. That is the version most traders should run.

Phase two then needs $5,000 and three green days, so five green sessions at one percent, or eight to ten sessions at a realistic win rate. Keeping the per-trade risk where it was rather than scaling it with the smaller target is the discipline that carries the plan into the funded stage.

Frequently asked

Do losing days count toward the The5ers minimum?

On a profitable-day rule, no. A day that ends flat or negative does not count, so the minimum is a count of green sessions rather than of calendar activity. That is the difference between needing three days and needing three good days, and it is the rule most often misread on this plan.

What are the High Stakes targets?

Ten percent must be earned in phase one and five in phase two, both measured from the opening balance. Scale it: $10,000 then $5,000 at the $100,000 size, $2,500 then $1,250 at $25,000. The percentages are fixed across sizes.

Is there a consistency rule on High Stakes?

None is stated on the plan rules we read. That is a statement about the evaluation, not a guarantee about the funded stage, where payout conditions can differ. Confirm at the point of the first withdrawal request rather than assuming the evaluation's silence carries over.

Does the maximum loss trail on The5ers?

No — it is static at ten percent from the starting balance on the published High Stakes plan. Profit does not reduce your remaining room, so the final part of the target does not require cutting size the way a trailing futures account does.

What is the daily loss limit?

A single session may lose five percent of the starting balance — $5,000 on a $100,000 account. Split into three tolerable losses that is roughly $1,650 a trade. Because a max-loss day costs a whole phase-two target, most plans should stop at half the budget.

What is the fastest realistic pass?

Three green days is the legal minimum, but three sessions large enough to reach ten percent would each consume most of the five percent daily budget. A plan paced at about one percent a day, spread across two to three weeks, is the version that survives a bad session and actually produces green closes.

What happens if I reach the target but have only two green days?

The phase stays open until the count is met, with the daily limit still live. The remaining days should be traded at a fraction of normal size: the profit is already sufficient and further risk adds nothing to the target. A green close on the next session usually completes the phase.

How many trading days should I plan for?

At a sixty percent win rate and a one percent daily target, around sixteen sessions for phase one and eight to ten for phase two. Plan in weeks, not days, because a profitable-day minimum makes the phase length depend on your consistency rather than on the calendar.

Which The5ers programme should I buy?

The one whose day rule matches how often you produce a green close. A high-frequency, small-target method fits High Stakes; a trader who takes a few large bets a month will find the profitable-day count expensive in calendar time whatever the fee.

Do neutral days count?

No. A session that closes flat is not profitable, so it advances the calendar without advancing the count. That is why recovery trading is actively harmful here: it tends to produce neutral closes, which are worthless under this rulebook.

How does High Stakes compare with FTMO?

Both publish a static ten percent floor and a five percent daily limit, and both ask for ten then five. FTMO requires four trading days and states no consistency rule; High Stakes requires three profitable days, which is a stricter calendar test for most methods. The FTMO rulebook page sets out the other side.

Can someone else trade my The5ers evaluation?

That is answered by the firm's terms, not by its evaluation parameters. We check the clause in writing before accepting a job and decline where third-party trading is prohibited. Read the risk disclosure before assuming a service can operate on your account.
