FundedNext Stellar rules, targets and payouts

Stellar looks like the standard two-step plan until you read the first target and the day count. Eight percent instead of ten, five days instead of four, and a consistency term that is not printed on the plan summary at all. Each of those differences is small on its own and they point in different directions, which is why this page works through them one at a time instead of quoting a headline.

Stellar rules at a glance

  • Evaluation: the Stellar two-phase plan, sold in parallel with other products that do not share these numbers.
  • Targets: 8% in phase one, 5% in phase two — a smaller first target than the market standard.
  • Maximum loss: 10% of the starting balance, static for the life of the evaluation.
  • Daily loss: 5% of the starting balance per session.
  • Minimum days: five, one more than the four most two-step plans ask for.
  • Consistency: a term exists in the product documents and is not stated on the summary — confirm it at checkout.
  • Verdict in one line: the smaller target is offset by the extra day and the unprinted clause, so read both before choosing it over a 10% plan.

What Stellar is, and what the name is doing

FundedNext sells several evaluation models, and Stellar is its two-phase CFD plan. The name matters more than a brand usually does, because the firm's one-phase product publishes a different target, a different day requirement and a consistency rule that is stated rather than implied. A question about "the FundedNext rules" therefore cannot be answered without first asking which model, and a page that gives one set of numbers as though it were the firm's rules is setting the reader up for a surprise at checkout.

The structure of Stellar itself is conventional: two phases, a static maximum loss, a daily loss limit, and a minimum number of trading days before a phase can complete. What distinguishes it from the two-step plans beside it is the arithmetic — a smaller first target, a slightly longer calendar — and the way the consistency term is disclosed.

That last point is not a technicality. A rule that is confirmed at checkout is a rule most buyers will not read until they are already inside an evaluation, and it is the one clause on this page that can block a payout after a perfectly good pass. Treat the rest of the page as mechanics and that sentence as the risk.

Eight percent is not a rounding difference

Two percentage points off the first target removes $2,000 of required profit from the phase most traders fail on a $100,000 account. More usefully, it changes the ratio between the target and the daily budget: eight percent against five percent is 1.6 daily budgets, where ten against five is two. That ratio governs how many sessions the target realistically needs and how much of the daily budget each session may consume.

At 1.6 budgets, a phase-one target can be produced across five sessions at roughly 1.6 percent a day, which is under a third of the daily limit. That is a comfortable pace, and it leaves room for a losing session without recalculation. Under a ten percent target the same five-session plan needs two percent a day, which is still inside the limit but begins to require a hit rate and, more importantly, begins to make a single bad session expensive relative to the remaining distance.

So the smaller target is a real advantage in phase one. The question is what is paid for it, and the answer is partly the fifth day and partly the consistency clause — both of which land later in the process, which is exactly why they get ignored.

The fifth day is a real constraint, not a formality

Five minimum trading days instead of four adds one protected session, and its cost is not the day itself but what the day forces. With an eight percent target and five sessions, the pace is under two percent a day, which is comfortable. But the fastest legal completion is day five regardless of how quickly the target arrives, so a trader who produces eight percent in two sessions has three days of open exposure with a live daily limit and nothing to gain.

The correct behaviour in that position is to stop, or to trade at a fraction of normal size. It is worth saying plainly because it is counter-intuitive: after the target is reached, every additional trade has only downside. The profit is already sufficient; the phase is waiting on a calendar, not on performance. Traders who keep trading because the account is "hot" are the ones who discover the minimum the hard way.

The second-order effect is on planning. A five-day phase one plus a five-day phase two is a ten-session floor for the whole evaluation, before any losing sessions are counted. A realistic pass therefore occupies two to four weeks, and a plan that assumes a week will feel broken by day six even when nothing has gone wrong.

The static floor, and why it changes the finish

The maximum loss is ten percent measured from the starting balance and it does not follow the peak. Make $6,000 and the failure level is where it always was; the room beneath the account is unchanged, so the profit is banked rather than converted into a higher wire.

On a plan like this, the final stretch of a phase is the safest part of it. The target is close and the buffer has not shrunk, so a normal retracement costs progress rather than the account. That is the opposite of a trailing futures evaluation, where the last twenty percent is where size must be cut hardest, and it is why traders who fail one model often pass the other with the same method.

What a static floor does not do is make oversizing survivable. Ten percent is still ten percent, and a trader risking two percent a trade ends the account in five consecutive losses regardless of whether the floor trails. The floor removes one failure mechanism, not the need for sizing discipline.

The consistency clause that is confirmed at checkout

This is the sentence on the plan summary that says to confirm the consistency term at checkout rather than stating it. Read as a disclosure, it is honest; read as a plan input, it is a gap, because a rule enforced at payout can invalidate an otherwise successful evaluation.

Consistency terms in this market usually compare a single best day's profit either to the total profit or to the target. A threshold of fifty percent means no day may contribute more than half the withdrawal; a thirty percent version requires at least four comparable days. Either version is satisfiable by an ordinary curve and hostile to a spike, which means the practical response is to plan the pass as a spread of sessions rather than a small number of large ones.

The correct move before paying is to ask support, in writing, three questions: what the term compares, at which stage it is applied, and whether it constrains only the first payout. Keep the reply. A screenshot of an answer from support is worth more than any summary on a comparison site, including this one.

What happens between the pass and the payout

A passed evaluation becomes a funded account with its own qualifying conditions: a profit requirement, a number of trading days, and a payout cycle. On a plan whose consistency term is disclosed late, those conditions deserve a second read at the point of the first withdrawal request rather than at the point of purchase, because that is when they are enforced.

The behavioural trap is the same one that appears on every funded account. The evaluation taught a size and a session stop; the funded stage tempts a larger size because the money is now real. Keeping the evaluation's numbers is the entire discipline, and it is easier to keep if the funded plan is written before the pass rather than improvised after it. Fees, resets and what the price does not include

FundedNext runs frequent promotions, and the sticker price of an evaluation changes with them, so any figure printed on a page like this would be stale before it was read. What can be described without hedging is the structure to check: whether the fee buys a single attempt or includes a reset, whether the account size can be changed after purchase, and what happens to the fee if a rule is breached early.

Those questions decide the real cost more than the headline does. An evaluation at a promotional price with no reset is more expensive than a full-price one that includes a second attempt if your pass rate is below fifty percent, which is true for most traders at most firms. The comparison that matters is cost per completed evaluation, not cost per purchase.

There is also a cost that never appears on an invoice: the calendar. On a five-day minimum, a fast failure and a slow pass differ by weeks, and weeks are the resource that a trader with a day job has least of. That is a legitimate reason to trade a smaller size on a longer horizon rather than a large size on a short one.

Who Stellar suits, and who should look elsewhere

It suits a trader who wants a two-phase structure with a slightly easier first target and does not object to a longer calendar. The static floor rewards a method with retracements, the eight percent target rewards a steady pace, and the five-day minimum is only a problem for someone who needs to finish inside a week.

It is a poor fit for a trader whose method produces occasional dominant sessions, not because the evaluation would fail but because the payout stage is where shape is judged and the term is not stated on the plan summary. It is also a poor fit for anyone who has not asked support about that term in writing — an unread clause is a plan built on an assumption.

For a trader who wants the same two-phase geometry with the consistency question settled before purchase, the FTMO 2-Step rulebook states no consistency rule at evaluation, and the comparison is worth making on that single line rather than on the target percentage.

Six ways a Stellar attempt ends

  • The daily limit spent on a recovery attempt. Two losses, a size increase, a third loss. The most common failure at any firm and entirely avoidable.
  • Trading after the target, waiting for day five. The phase is complete except for the calendar, and the profit is put back at risk for nothing.
  • A payout blocked by an unread consistency term. The target was reached in two sessions and the shape of the profit fails the test applied later.
  • Oversizing the second phase. The same ten percent floor against a smaller target leaves less room, not more.
  • Assuming the one-phase model's numbers. Different target, different day count, different consistency disclosure, same brand.
  • Buying a size whose daily budget cannot absorb three losses. At $10,000 the budget is $500, so a per-trade risk above roughly $165 makes three in a row a breach.

Only the first of those is a trading decision in the ordinary sense. The rest are reading decisions made before the first trade.

Reading the plan summary against the objectives page

FundedNext presents an evaluation in two places, and they do not say the same things. The plan summary, which is what a buyer sees first, carries the headline numbers: the target, the loss limits, the day count, the price. The objectives page carries the definitions behind those numbers, and occasionally a sentence that changes them — such as the instruction to confirm the consistency term at checkout.

The practical rule is to treat the summary as a table of contents and the objectives page as the contract. Three specific questions are usually answered in one place and not the other: how the daily loss is measured, whether the maximum loss trails, and where the consistency term applies. A trader who reads only the summary will believe all three are settled by the headline percentages, and none of them is.

There is a second, less obvious asymmetry. The summary is written to be compared — it is designed to sit beside a competitor's summary and look reasonable. The objectives page is written to be enforced. Those are different documents with different purposes, and the gap between them is where most of the unpleasant surprises in this market live. Reading the enforced version before paying is not diligence for its own sake; it is the only way to know what the account will actually do to you.

Eight percent against a five percent day

The ratio between the target and the daily budget is the most useful single number on this page, and it is not published anywhere. Divide the first target by the daily limit: eight divided by five is 1.6. On a plan with a ten percent target the same calculation gives two.

What the ratio tells you is how much of the daily budget each session is entitled to consume if the target is to be reached at a survivable pace. At 1.6, the whole target is worth less than two maximum-loss days. That is a tight relationship, and it means a single day in which the full five percent is lost has cost more than half the entire phase-one objective. Put differently: on this plan a blow-up day is not a setback, it is a phase-ending event in slow motion.

At a pace of 1.6 percent a day across five sessions, each session uses about a third of the daily budget, which is comfortable and leaves a losing session recoverable. At three percent a day — still inside the limit — the target arrives in under three sessions, which the day count does not permit, so the extra pace buys nothing and costs risk. That is the argument for planning to the day minimum rather than to the fastest possible finish: on a plan whose target is only 1.6 budgets wide, speed is pure cost.

Choosing a size: the daily budget test

Account sizes are marketed as rungs on a ladder, and the implied advice is to climb as far as the budget allows. The correct test has nothing to do with the balance: pick the size whose daily loss limit can absorb three ordinary losses at your real per-trade risk.

At $10,000 the five percent budget is $500. Three losses inside it means about $165 a trade. If your method risks twenty pips on a major pair with a normal position size, $165 may be a legitimate trade — but it is a small one, and the temptation to round up is the whole problem. At $25,000 the budget is $1,250 and the per-trade figure is roughly $415; at $100,000 it is $5,000 and $1,650. The geometry is identical at every size. Nothing becomes easier as the account grows, except the granularity with which you can size.

That last point is the only real argument for a larger account, and it is a technical one rather than a psychological one. Small accounts produce lumpy sizing: a $10,000 account with a $500 daily budget cannot express a half-percent risk cleanly on many instruments, because the minimum lot or contract is already a meaningful fraction of the budget. Larger accounts allow finer granularity, which makes a disciplined plan easier to execute exactly. It does not make the rules more generous.

The second test is the target against the day minimum. Eight percent over five sessions is 1.6 percent a day, which is a third of the daily budget — comfortable. If the size you are considering makes that pace impossible without using more than half the budget per session, the size is wrong for the plan regardless of what it costs. The position size calculator turns the per-trade figure into a defined position, and the daily drawdown calculator keeps the session inside the limit.

A third test is worth running before paying, and almost nobody does: count how many consecutive losses the static ten percent floor survives at the per-trade risk implied by the size. If the answer is under ten, the plan is being funded at a size the method cannot defend, and the consistency clause will never become the binding constraint — the streak will get there first.

What a payout request actually checks

By the time a payout is requested, the evaluation rules are history and a different set of conditions applies. The request is checked against a qualifying profit, a number of trading days, the payout cycle for the account, and — on plans that carry one — the consistency term. Only the first of those is about how well the account traded.

The consistency check is the one that surprises people, because it is a test on the shape of the profit rather than its size. If one session produced more than half the total, the withdrawal is blocked until more ordinary days are added, which means the funded account continues to carry risk for weeks after the target was met. A trader who knew the term in advance would have spread the profit; a trader who did not will describe the firm as unfair, when the rule was in the documents all along.

The day count works the same way and is easier to satisfy. The qualifying-profit figure is the straightforward part: an amount that must be earned and held. None of these checks cares how quickly the evaluation was passed, which is a useful thing to remember when the temptation to accelerate appears — the payout stage rewards a curve, and a curve takes the time it takes.

Before the first withdrawal, it is worth reading the funded-phase terms as carefully as the evaluation terms, because they are enforced on money rather than on an attempt. The consistency rule guide sets out the versions in use across firms and what each one compares.

Why the smaller target does not shorten the plan

Eight percent looks like a two-day job on a good week, and the five-day minimum is what stops it from being one. The consequence is worth stating directly: the smaller first target does not shorten the evaluation, it shortens each session's required contribution. That is a better kind of help, because it reduces the per-session pressure without reducing the number of sessions the account must survive.

Laid out honestly, a Stellar evaluation is ten sessions minimum across the two phases, plus whatever losing sessions the plan absorbs. At a sixty percent hit rate that is closer to fourteen. A trader who budgets three weeks is being realistic; a trader who budgets one week is planning for a hit rate no method sustains, and will respond to the shortfall by increasing size, which is the failure mode the smaller target was supposed to prevent.

There is a useful discipline hidden in that arithmetic. If the plan needs fourteen sessions and the target needs eight percent, then the average session contributes about 0.6 percent, which is a seventh of the daily budget. That is a genuinely comfortable number, and it means the evaluation can be completed by a trader who is merely competent rather than exceptional — provided they do not convert a comfortable pace into an aggressive one out of impatience.

A worked three-week plan

Take a $100,000 account. Phase one asks for $8,000 and cannot complete before five sessions, so plan eight sessions at $1,000 a day — one percent of the account, a fifth of the daily budget. At that pace the per-trade risk can sit at $700 to $900 and still survive three losses inside a session without touching the limit.

Eight sessions against a five-day floor leaves three days of slack, which is the point. A losing day does not require a size increase; it consumes slack. Phase two then asks for $5,000, again with a five-day floor, so plan six sessions at roughly $850 a day, and keep the per-trade risk unchanged rather than scaling it with the smaller target.

The consistency shape should be checked at the end of phase one, not at the end of phase two. If one session produced more than half the phase-one profit, the same pattern is likely to appear at payout, and the cheapest correction is to spread the remaining sessions more evenly rather than to trade bigger later.

What to confirm before you pay

  • The model name on the plan. Stellar is two-phase; other FundedNext products are one-phase with different targets and an explicitly stated consistency rule.
  • The consistency term, in writing, from support. What it compares, where it applies, and whether it constrains only the first payout.

Frequently asked

Is the FundedNext Stellar target 8% or 10%?

Stellar publishes eight percent for phase one and five percent for phase two. Other FundedNext models use different figures, so check the model name on the plan in your cart rather than the brand. At $100,000, eight percent is $8,000.

Does Stellar have a consistency rule?

The plan summary says to confirm it at checkout rather than printing the term. That is exactly the sentence to get in writing from support before paying, because a consistency clause enforced at payout can stop a passed evaluation from withdrawing.

How many trading days does Stellar require?

Five per phase, one more than the four-day minimum common on two-step plans. It is not a long delay, but it means the fastest possible finish is day five and the target should be paced across at least that many sessions. A whole evaluation therefore takes ten sessions minimum.

Is the maximum loss static on Stellar?

According to the published CFD objectives, yes — a static ten percent measured from the starting balance. Confirm it on the specific plan page before buying, because trailing structures appear on some FundedNext products.

What is the daily loss limit?

The session stop is five percent of the starting balance, so $5,000 against a $100,000 plan, and it resets each day. Split into three tolerable losses that is roughly $1,650 a trade, and most plans should stop for the day at half the budget rather than spending it all.

Can I finish phase one before five days if I reach the target?

No. The phase stays open until the day count is met while the daily limit stays live. The correct response is to trade flat or at a fraction of normal size, because the target is already sufficient and further trades carry only downside.

How does Stellar compare with the FTMO 2-Step?

Both are two-phase plans with a static ten percent floor and a five percent daily limit. Stellar asks for eight percent in phase one against FTMO's ten, requires five days rather than four, and leaves its consistency term to checkout where FTMO's 2-Step states none at evaluation. The FTMO rulebook page covers that comparison from the other side.

Which account size should I buy?

The one whose five percent daily budget absorbs three losses at your real per-trade risk. At $10,000 that budget is $500, so a per-trade risk above about $165 makes three consecutive losses a breach. Larger sizes scale the same geometry rather than making the test easier.

Is the fee refundable if I fail?

A rule breach ends the attempt and the fee is spent. Any reset or retry attached to an attempt is set by the product and by whatever promotion is running; read the checkout, and treat comparison sites — this one included — as a starting point only.

Can FundedNext rules change during my evaluation?

Firms revise parameters, and the version attached to your purchase governs. Keep the plan page and the checkout page as they stood on the day you bought, and treat every figure on this page as provisional until you have matched it to your own account documents.

That is a separate question from the evaluation parameters and is answered by the firm's terms, not by its objectives page.
