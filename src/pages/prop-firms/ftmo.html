Verified against FTMO's own challenge objectives

FTMO challenge rules, targets and payouts

FTMO is the rulebook the rest of the industry is measured against, and it is also the one most often described from memory rather than from the objectives page. Two phases, a floor that does not move, four days that must pass whether or not the target did — and a consistency clause that belongs to a different product than the one most people buy. This page states the 2-Step figures in cash, then works through the two rules that actually end attempts.

What the FTMO 2-Step asks for

The evaluation is a two-phase test on a simulated account. Phase one requires a ten percent gain; phase two requires five percent. Behind both phases sits a maximum loss of ten percent measured from the starting balance, which does not move as the account grows, plus a five percent daily loss limit that resets each trading day. Neither phase can be completed before four trading days have elapsed, even if the target is reached on day one.

That is the whole rulebook in a paragraph, and its shape is unusual in one respect that is easy to miss: nothing in it rewards speed. There is no trailing floor, so profit does not tighten the account; there is no consistency clause at evaluation, so a large day is an asset rather than a liability; and the day minimum removes the only incentive to rush. A trader arriving from a futures evaluation with a rising threshold will find this structurally gentler and psychologically harder, because the pressure to perform quickly has to be generated internally instead of being imposed by the rules.

The other thing worth stating at the start is that FTMO sells more than one product under one brand. The 1-Step carries a different target and a consistency term that the 2-Step does not have. Nearly every confused FTMO question on the internet is a 1-Step rule applied to a 2-Step account, so the plan name matters more than the firm name throughout this page.

The static floor is the most valuable line on the page

Ten percent maximum loss, measured from the starting balance, means a $100,000 account fails at $90,000 and keeps failing at $90,000 no matter what equity does in between. Make $6,000 and the failure level stays at $90,000; the room available is unchanged, so the profit is genuinely banked rather than exchanged for a higher wire.

This single structural fact changes how the final stretch of a phase should be traded. On a trailing account, the last twenty percent of the target is the most dangerous part of the evaluation, because every new high raises the floor and shrinks the buffer. Here the opposite is true: the last twenty percent is the safest part, because the target is almost reached and the room underneath has never been larger relative to the distance left.

That asymmetry is why traders who fail a trailing evaluation often pass here with an identical method and no additional skill. It is worth being honest about what that means: the plan is more forgiving of a drawdown, not more forgiving of bad sizing. A static floor still ends at ten percent, and ten percent is a normal bad month for an oversized trader.

The daily limit is the rule that ends attempts

Five percent a day sounds roomy until a session goes wrong, and then it removes the one thing a struggling trader wants most, which is time. The sequence is always the same: two losses at one and a half percent each, a size increase to recover, and the third trade closes the day. Nothing about the market caused the failure; a loss budget was spent faster than it was earned.

The arithmetic is worth doing once, in cash, at your own size. On $100,000 the daily budget is $5,000. Split into three tolerable losses, that is roughly $1,650 a trade. At a twenty-pip stop on a major pair, $1,650 defines a position size rather than expressing a preference — which is exactly what the position size calculator converts. On $25,000 the same budget is $1,250 and the per-trade figure falls to about $415, and the trade that felt small at the larger size is suddenly too large.

The second-order effect is the one traders miss: because the daily limit is five percent and the phase-two target is also five percent, a single maximum-loss day costs an entire phase's worth of progress. That is a bad trade in a literal sense, and it justifies a session stop well inside the limit — most experienced FTMO traders stop at half the budget and treat the remainder as protection against a mistake rather than as capacity to use.

Four minimum days, and why they matter more than they look

A target reached in two sessions does not end the phase. It leaves the evaluation open with the profit exposed to the same daily limit, and the job changes from producing a result to protecting one. Traders who have never read the day count discover this on the afternoon the target is hit, usually by giving back enough to need the target again.

The corrective is to plan the phase across at least five sessions from the beginning, so the calendar never becomes a surprise. A ten percent target over five days is two percent a day, which sits comfortably inside a five percent daily limit and leaves a per-trade risk that survives three consecutive losses. Over four days it is two and a half percent a day, which is still inside the limit but begins to require a hit rate that a normal week does not guarantee.

The minimum also interacts with phase two in a way that is easy to miss. Phase two asks for five percent against the same rules, so a trader who arrives at phase two in a strong week can reasonably finish the target inside the four-day window and then spend the remaining days doing nothing at all. Deliberately trading flat for two days is a legitimate strategy here in a way it is not on a plan that rewards activity, and it is the cheapest form of risk management the rulebook offers.

The consistency clause belongs to the other product

FTMO's 2-Step evaluation states no consistency rule. Its 1-Step does. Since the brand is the same and the marketing pages often sit side by side, a trader who reads the wrong page plans for a constraint that is not there, while a trader who buys the 1-Step believing it behaves like the 2-Step plans for one that is.

Practically, the absence of a consistency term on the 2-Step removes a real objection to a lumpy method. On a plan with a best-day rule, a single dominant session can pass the target and still block the payout; here there is no such test at evaluation, so a strategy that produces occasional large winners is not penalised for its shape. The payout stage has its own conditions, which is where the question should be re-asked rather than assumed.

Phase two is not a smaller phase one

The second phase asks for half the profit, which most traders read as half the difficulty. It is not, because the floor underneath it is the same ten percent and the daily limit is the same five percent, while the target sits closer to the start. A five percent target with a five percent daily budget means a single maximum day consumes the entire objective's worth of progress, so phase two rewards caution disproportionately.

There is also a behavioural problem unique to phase two. A trader who passed phase one quickly arrives with confidence and size, and the smaller target offers no structural reason to reduce either. The correct adjustment is the reverse of the trailing-account taper: cut risk by roughly a third in phase two, not because the target is bigger but because the remaining distance is short enough that a single bad session is expensive relative to it.

The funded stage: what changes after the pass

Passing both phases produces a funded account, and the first withdrawal has conditions of its own: a qualifying profit, a minimum number of trading days, and a payout cycle that decides when the money can actually leave. None of these is visible during the evaluation, which is why the transition trips up traders who did everything right to get there.

The single largest change is psychological. During the evaluation, a loss costs time; on the funded account, a loss costs money, and the temptation is to behave differently — either by becoming so cautious that nothing is produced, or by treating the funded balance as a windfall and increasing size. Both are deviations from the plan that earned the account, and both are avoidable by deciding the funded-phase rules before the pass rather than after it.

The structural change worth knowing is that the funded account keeps a loss floor and a daily limit, so the geometry does not relax. A trader who has been running a session stop at half the daily budget should keep running it. The FTMO review covers the funded side and the payout cycle in more detail than this page attempts, and the challenge passing guide describes how the evaluation is traded session by session.

Restricted periods and holding rules

FTMO publishes policies about news events and about holding positions over weekends and rollovers on some account types, and those policies remove sessions from the calendar rather than adding risk to a trade. The practical consequence is that a plan built around a fixed number of sessions has to account for days that are simply unavailable, which is another argument for spreading the target across more days than the minimum.

The habit that prevents problems is to read the holding policy for the specific account type being bought, not the general marketing page. Swing and normal accounts differ, and a strategy that holds through the weekend on an account that does not permit it will produce a breach that looks arbitrary and is entirely predictable.

Who FTMO suits

This rulebook suits a trader with a slower, wider method: someone whose edge expresses itself over days rather than minutes, who can tolerate a drawdown without changing size, and who does not need the calendar to force discipline. The static floor is a genuine advantage for a method with meaningful retracements, because profit does not shrink the buffer.

It also suits a first evaluation, for an unglamorous reason: the rules are the least surprising in the market, so a failure here is informative. If a method cannot pass a static floor with a ten percent buffer and a five percent daily limit, the problem is the method rather than the rulebook, and no amount of firm-shopping will fix it.

It is a poorer fit for two styles. A scalper who trades dozens of times a day will find the five percent daily limit generous and the four-day minimum irrelevant, but the spread cost of producing two percent a day that way is usually underestimated. And a trader whose method depends on a small number of very large days will pass comfortably and then discover that the funded phase's payout conditions are where shape actually matters.

Five ways an FTMO attempt ends

  • The daily limit, spent early. Two losses, a size increase, a third loss. The most common failure, and the least mysterious.
  • Holding a position through a restricted period. A news or weekend policy that was never read, enforced on an account that had been trading well.
  • Reaching the target before day four and then trading. The target is met, the phase is still open, and the profit is given back inside the same daily limit.
  • Oversizing phase two. Half the target is not half the risk; the floor is the same and the distance is shorter, so the same size is proportionally larger.
  • Passing and then changing the method. The funded account is traded as though the rules relaxed, when in fact only the objective changed.

Four of the five are calendar or sizing errors, which is a useful summary of this rulebook: it rarely punishes a market view, and frequently punishes a plan that ignored a page of the objectives.

Two phases, one risk budget

The mistake almost every FTMO plan makes is to budget the phases separately. Phase one gets a weekly target, phase two gets its own, and the floor is treated as a fresh ten percent each time. It is not. The maximum loss is measured from the starting balance of the account you are trading, and the daily limit applies to both phases without distinction, so the correct unit of planning is the whole evaluation rather than either half of it.

Budgeted as one job, the evaluation asks for fifteen percent of profit — ten and then five — while tolerating ten percent of total loss from the start. That is a reward-to-risk ratio of one and a half to one on the whole exercise, which is a more honest description of the task than either target alone. It also explains why a trader who is up eight percent after phase one is not in a strong position: they have made half the total profit, and they still carry the entire ten percent of downside underneath the second phase.

Two consequences follow. First, the phase boundary should be a point at which risk falls, not resets. A trader who finishes phase one with a comfortable buffer should carry a smaller per-trade risk into phase two, because the remaining target is shorter and a full-size loss is proportionally more expensive against it. Second, the day count is a total, not a per-phase formality: eight sessions minimum across the two phases, plus however many losing days the plan absorbs, is the shortest realistic schedule. Any plan that assumes one week for the whole evaluation is assuming a hit rate no method delivers.

There is a third consequence that is less about arithmetic and more about behaviour. Splitting the evaluation into two objectives invites a celebration at the halfway mark — a larger size, a looser session stop, a belief that the hard part is done. The hard part is not done. The floor is unchanged and the daily limit is unchanged, and the only thing that has changed is that there is now less profit left to earn against the same total risk.

A losing streak against a static floor

Static floors feel generous, and the way to test whether that feeling is justified is to run a losing streak through one. Take a $100,000 account with a $10,000 floor and a $5,000 daily limit, and a plan risking one percent per trade — $1,000.

Five consecutive losses cost $5,000, which is exactly one maximum-loss day and half the total buffer. The account is not dead, but the phase-one target now needs fifteen percent instead of ten, which is a different proposition entirely. Ten consecutive losses end the account. So the honest statement about a static floor is that it tolerates a bad week and does not tolerate a bad fortnight, which is a narrower margin than the words "ten percent" suggest.

Now run the same streak at half a percent — $500 a trade. Five losses cost $2,500, ten cost $5,000, and the account survives both with the target still reachable. Twenty consecutive losses end it, which is a streak that essentially does not occur in a method with a real edge. That difference between one percent and half a percent is the entire content of position sizing, and it is the variable a trader actually chooses. Everything else on this page — the targets, the day count, the reset hour — is fixed by the firm.

The reason traders resist halving size is that it appears to double the time required, and time feels like the scarce resource when a fee has been paid and a calendar is running. It is not the scarce resource. The fee is already spent whether the evaluation takes two weeks or six, and the only outcome that wastes it completely is an account that ends before the target is reached. Sizing down is not caution, it is the only lever that changes the probability rather than the pace.

A useful discipline is to write the streak arithmetic down before the first trade: at my size, this many losses costs this much, and that many ends the account. If the second number is under twelve, the size is too large for the method, whatever the daily limit allows. What the four-day minimum costs in practice

The day minimum is usually described as a minor formality, and in one sense it is: four days out of a two-week evaluation is not a meaningful delay. Its real cost is that it removes the option to finish early, and that option is worth something to a trader who has just had an unusually good week.

Consider a trader who produces six percent on the first two sessions. Under a plan with no minimum they would be finished and funded; here they have two more sessions to survive with the target already met and the daily limit still live. The rational response is to stop trading, and the emotional response is to keep going because the account is running well. The gap between those two responses is where most of the avoidable failures on this rulebook happen, and the minimum is what makes the gap exist.

There is a legitimate way to use the extra sessions instead of merely surviving them: treat them as a rehearsal of the funded phase. Trade the same size, the same session stop and the same instruments, and produce ordinary sessions rather than spectacular ones. A trader who does that arrives at the funded account already accustomed to operating under the rules without a target to chase.

Where the minimum genuinely hurts is on a small account with a tight fee budget. At $10,000, four days of a five percent daily limit is a lot of exposure relative to a $1,000 target, and a trader whose method produces only a few setups a week will find the calendar, not the target, deciding when the phase ends. In that case the honest answer is that the size is too small to make the calendar comfortable rather than that the rule is unfair.

A worked four-week plan

Take a $100,000 account and a four-week horizon, which is deliberately slower than the minimum. The target is $10,000 across twenty sessions, or $500 a day — half a percent a day against a five percent daily budget. At $500 a day the per-trade risk can sit at $800 to $1,000 and still survive three consecutive losses inside a session, which is a comfortable place to be.

Four weeks is not padding. It converts a hit-rate requirement into a pacing requirement, and pacing is the one variable a trader actually controls. The first week is allowed to be flat or slightly negative; the plan does not need to produce until week two. That allowance is worth more than any setup, because it removes the pressure that causes the size increase in week one.

Phase two then takes a week at the same pace: $5,000 across five sessions is $1,000 a day, double the phase-one rate, so either the size rises or the horizon extends. Extend the horizon. Doubling size to halve the calendar is the exact trade that produces the fifth failure above, and the fee does not care how long the pass takes.

What to confirm before you pay

  • 2-Step or 1-Step. Different targets, and only one of them has a consistency clause. Buy the plan whose objectives page you have actually read.
  • How the daily loss is measured. Equity including floating positions, or closed balance, and the hour at which the day resets. The same five percent produces materially different room under each convention.
  • The holding and news policy for your account type. Swing and normal differ, and the policy removes sessions rather than adding risk.
  • The payout conditions you will meet after the pass. Qualifying profit, minimum days and cycle length are the gates that decide whether a pass becomes money.
  • The third-party trading clause. Whether another person may trade the evaluation is a separate permission from every rule on this page, and it decides whether a passed account can ever pay out. Our position is in the risk disclosure.

Frequently asked

What are the FTMO 2-Step targets?

Ten percent in phase one and five percent in phase two, measured on the account's starting balance. At $100,000 that is $10,000 and then $5,000; at $25,000 it is $2,500 and $1,250. The percentages are fixed across sizes, which is why FTMO is easy to compare with other percentage-based plans.

Is the FTMO maximum loss static or trailing?

Static on the 2-Step: ten percent below the starting balance, unchanged by profit. A gain does not raise the failure level, so profit does not reduce your remaining room. This is the opposite of a trailing futures account and is the main structural difference between the two models.

Does FTMO have a consistency rule on the 2-Step?

None at evaluation on the 2-Step as published. The 1-Step carries a consistency term, which is why the plan name matters more than the brand. Check the objectives page for the product in your cart rather than assuming the two behave alike.

What is the FTMO daily loss limit?

Five percent of the account per trading day, which is $5,000 on a $100,000 account. It resets daily and applies to both phases. A single maximum-loss day therefore costs an entire phase-two target's worth of progress, which is why most plans stop at half the budget.

How many trading days does the FTMO challenge take?

Four minimum per phase. A target reached sooner leaves the phase open until the day count is met, and the daily limit stays live throughout, so the remaining days are spent protecting the result rather than producing it. Plan five sessions per phase so the calendar is never a surprise.

What happens if I hit the target on day one?

The phase continues until four trading days have elapsed. The sensible response is to trade flat or at a fraction of normal size for the remaining days, because the profit is already sufficient and the only thing that can change is that you lose it.

Can I hold positions overnight or over the weekend?

It depends on the account type. FTMO publishes separate holding and news policies for normal and swing accounts, and a strategy that holds through a restricted period on an account that does not permit it will breach. Confirm the policy for the specific product before trading, not after.

Is phase two easier than phase one?

It asks for half the profit but runs under the same ten percent floor and five percent daily limit, so a five percent target against a five percent daily budget is proportionally tighter. Most traders should reduce size in phase two rather than repeat the phase-one plan.

Can I pass FTMO with a passing service?

Only if the firm's terms permit third-party trading, which has to be confirmed for your own account and is not established by any rule on this page. We check the clause in writing before accepting a job and decline where it is prohibited — see the risk disclosure.

Which account size should I buy?

The one whose five percent daily budget can absorb three losses at your real per-trade risk. On $10,000 that budget is $500, so a per-trade risk above about $165 makes three consecutive losses a breach. Larger sizes scale the same geometry, so a bigger account is not easier — it is the same test with larger numbers.

Is FTMO a good first evaluation?

Yes, for a specific reason: the rules are the least surprising in the market, so a failure tells you something about the method rather than about the rulebook. A static floor, a generous daily limit and a four-day minimum leave very little room to blame the structure.

How does FTMO compare with the Apex rules?

FTMO asks for two percentage targets with a floor that never moves; Apex asks for one dollar target with a threshold that trails the peak. A method with deep retracements tends to survive here and fail there. The Apex rulebook page sets out the other side of that comparison.
