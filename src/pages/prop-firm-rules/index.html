Prop firm rules, explained one at a time

Every failed evaluation is a failed reading of a rulebook. This page is the index: the eight rules that decide most challenges, what each one actually measures, and the guide that takes it apart in full. Use it to find your firm's rule, then read only that one.

How to use this page

  • Find the rule that ended your last attempt — or the one you cannot explain out loud.
  • Read the guide, then check the same rule in your firm's plan document, not in a comparison table.
  • Put the numbers into the prop firm calculator before the first trade, not after the first loss.
  • Where a firm publishes several plans, the plan name is the rulebook. Compare plans, never firms.

Why the rulebook beats the strategy

A trader can hold a genuinely good plan and still fail four evaluations in a row, because the plan is answering a question the firm did not ask. A strategy decides whether you make money. The rulebook decides whether the money counts. They are different documents and they are scored by different tests.

The asymmetry is in the penalty. A mediocre strategy usually produces a small loss, which the daily limit absorbs. A misread rule produces an instant failure regardless of profit — a position held three minutes into a news window, a profitable day that was too profitable, an account closed on a Friday evening it should have been flat. The distribution of outcomes is why reading time is better spent on the terms than on one more indicator.

The three numbers almost always misread

Balance versus equity. A 5% limit measured on equity counts an open losing position; the same limit measured on closed balance does not. Two firms with identical percentages can therefore have materially different room, and the wording — not the number — is the tell.

The drawdown base. A static 10% floor sits still for the whole evaluation. A trailing 10% floor follows the peak upward, so profit reduces room instead of creating it. The same headline figure produces completely different arithmetic, and it is why an account can be "up 6%" and closer to failure than when it started.

The stage a rule applies at. Consistency is the clearest example: it may be absent at evaluation and enforced at payout, so an evaluation passed in nine good days produces a funded account that cannot withdraw. Reading the evaluation document alone is how that happens.

What this hub is not

It is not a substitute for your firm's terms, and it is not a claim that any firm's figures are permanent. Firms revise targets, drawdown models and consistency thresholds without notice, and they publish different rule sets under different plan names. Every figure on this site is stamped with the date it was read, and how we check firm rules sets out what that date does and does not promise.

Questions this hub gets asked

Which rule fails the most challenges?

The daily loss limit, because it is the rule a trader meets most often. Maximum loss ends more accounts in total, but the daily limit is the one that ends an ordinary bad afternoon, and it is the one most often breached while trying to recover a nearly-spent budget.

Do all firms measure the daily limit the same way?

No. Some measure equity including floating positions, some measure closed balance, and reset times vary between server midnight, the broker's daily close and a rolling window. The percentage is the least interesting part of the rule; the measurement basis is the part that decides outcomes.

Is a trailing drawdown worse than a static one?

It is stricter in the middle of an evaluation and can be the difference between passing and failing on the same trade sequence. A trailing floor rises with the peak, so the account has to reach the target without ever giving back more than the permitted distance from its best point. Static floors do not move at all.
