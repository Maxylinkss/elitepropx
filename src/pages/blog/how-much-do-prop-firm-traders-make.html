How Much Do Prop Firm Traders Really Make?

The honest answer is uncomfortable: most traders who buy an evaluation never receive a payout, and most of those who do receive a small one before the account fails. The average income figure is pulled up by a small group whose accounts survive, which makes averages a poor guide for planning. This page is about the distribution behind the number: who earns, how long it takes, what changes between the traders who stay and the traders who disappear, and how to assess your own position honestly.

Key takeaways

  • The median trader earns far less than the average, because a small group of survivors pulls the average up.
  • Most first payouts are small, and the first year is usually a net cost rather than a net income.
  • The difference between earners and payers is rarely strategy; it is survival, sizing and payout discipline.
  • Any income claim should be assessed against the number of attempts, the time taken and the costs paid to reach it.

The distribution behind the average

Imagine a hundred traders who each buy one evaluation in a year. A realistic distribution might look like this: sixty never pass; twenty-five pass but fail the funded account before a payout; ten receive one small payout and then fail; four receive several payouts and earn a modest annual income; one earns a large income and accounts for a disproportionate share of the total. The average of that group is pulled up by the top five, while the median is close to zero.

The exact numbers vary by firm, market and year, and no public dataset is reliable enough to quote precisely. The shape, however, is consistent across the industry: a long tail of traders who pay fees and never receive money, a middle group with small and irregular payouts, and a small top group with large and consistent income. Any statement about "how much prop traders make" that does not describe the shape is describing the top group as if it were the whole.

The practical implication is that the median is a better planning figure than the average. A trader planning an income should ask what the median trader with a similar account size and experience earns, not what the average of the whole population earns. The median is lower, and it is closer to the experience the trader is likely to have in the first year.

The survival curve

Prop trading income is a function of survival. An account that survives produces payouts; an account that fails stops producing. The survival curve for funded accounts is steep in the first three months and flattens after six. Most failures happen early, when the trader is adjusting to the funded rules and the psychological pressure of trading an account that is not theirs.

Why early failures cluster

There are three reasons. First, the funded account often has different rules from the evaluation: a consistency requirement, a payout threshold, a different drawdown. A trader who carries the evaluation plan into the funded phase may breach a rule they did not read. Second, the psychological weight changes. A simulated evaluation is a test; a funded account is money. Traders who were disciplined on the test often become impulsive on the account. Third, the first payout is a milestone, and the pressure to reach it can lead to oversizing after a slow start.

What survival looks like after six months

A trader who survives six months has usually settled into a size that fits the funded account, a payout rhythm that matches the firm's schedule and a process that survives a losing month. The income is rarely spectacular, but it is repeatable. The trader has also learned which rules are enforced and which are soft, which reduces the risk of a surprise.

The second-account effect

Survivors often add a second account at another firm. The second account diversifies the rule risk and provides a second income stream if the first fails. The effect on income is not linear: two accounts do not double the income, because the trader's attention is divided and the size on each account is smaller. But the survival probability of the combined operation is higher, which improves the expected income over a year.

What the first year looks like

The first year is usually a net cost. The fees, the failed attempts and the time spent learning the rules outweigh the payouts for most traders. That is not a reason to avoid prop trading; it is a reason to plan the first year as a build-up rather than an income.

Months one to three: fees and learning

The first quarter is dominated by preparation and the first attempt. The trader pays an evaluation fee, learns the platform and discovers which rules are harder than expected. Many traders fail the first attempt and pay for a second. The payouts, if any, are small.

Months four to six: the first funded account

If the trader passes, the funded account begins. The first payout may take a month or more to arrive, depending on the firm's conditions. The trader learns the payout process and the consistency rules. The income is irregular and often smaller than expected.

Months seven to twelve: survival or reset

By the second half of the year, the trader either has a stable process and a modest income, or has failed one or more accounts and is resetting. The difference is rarely the strategy; it is the behaviour around size and payouts. A trader who survives the first year has usually learned to reduce size after a drawdown and to plan the payout distribution.

The first-year curve is the reason many traders quit. The income arrives late, and the costs arrive early. A trader who expects a salary in month one is likely to be disappointed; one who expects a build-up is more likely to persist long enough to see the curve turn.

Six patterns that separate earners from payers

The difference between traders who earn and traders who pay is rarely the entry signal. It is a set of behaviours that show up in the first few months.

Pattern one: they size from the daily budget

Earners calculate the per-trade risk from the daily loss limit, not the account balance. Payers size from the balance, which makes the daily limit a target rather than a boundary. The difference is visible in the first week: the earner's worst day is a fraction of the budget, the payer's worst day is the budget.

Pattern two: they stop at the written rule

Earners stop after two losses or a fixed percentage of the budget, regardless of how they feel. Payers keep trading until the limit is reached or breached. The stop rule is the single most reliable predictor of survival in the first month.

Pattern three: they read the payout terms before starting

Earners know the consistency rule, the minimum days and the payout schedule before the first trade. Payers discover them at the payout request and then have to keep trading to satisfy a rule they did not plan for. The difference in income is not the pass rate; it is the time to the first payout.

Pattern four: they reduce size after a win on a trailing account

Earners recalculate the distance to the floor every morning and reduce size when it narrows. Payers keep the same size and give back the profit in a normal pullback. The behaviour is specific to trailing accounts, but the principle applies everywhere: the size should follow the room.

Pattern five: they keep a record

Earners keep a journal with the session numbers, the highest floating loss and the rule issues. Payers rely on memory and screenshots. The record is what allows the earner to see a pattern and fix it; the payer repeats the same mistake across attempts.

Pattern six: they treat the fee as sunk

Earners do not let the fee influence the trading. They follow the plan even if it means a flat week. Payers feel pressure to get their money's worth and overtrade. The sunk-cost response is one of the most common reasons a funded account fails in the first month.

Four trader profiles

The patterns are easier to see in profiles. These four are illustrative, but each is common.

The fee collector

This trader buys evaluations regularly and never passes. The strategy may be fine, but the size is too large for the daily limit, and the stop rule is not followed. The trader blames the firm or the market and buys another evaluation at a different firm, repeating the pattern. The income is zero, and the cost is a series of fees.

The one-payout trader

This trader passes and receives one payout, then fails the funded account within a few months. The first payout is usually small, and it is often consumed by the fees of the next attempt. The trader has demonstrated the ability to pass but not the ability to survive, which is the harder skill.

The steady earner

This trader has one or two funded accounts, a modest monthly return and a payout rhythm that matches the firm's schedule. The income is not spectacular, but it is repeatable, and the accounts survive normal drawdowns. The trader has usually been through at least one failure and has adjusted the size and the process.

The large earner

This trader has several accounts or a large account, a consistent process and a long survival record. The income is large because the account size is large and the return is sustainable, not because the return is spectacular. The large earner is rare, and the public claims about prop trading income are usually describing this profile.

Reading income claims honestly

Income claims are common in the industry and rarely complete. A screenshot of a payout does not show the fees paid to reach it, the number of failed attempts, the time spent or the accounts that failed afterwards. A claim of "$10,000 a month" may be true for one month and irrelevant for the year.

The questions to ask

When you see a claim, ask: how many attempts did it take, how much was paid in fees, how long did it take, how many accounts failed before and after, and is the figure gross or net. A claim that cannot answer those questions is a marketing statement, not a data point. The same applies to a service that advertises a pass rate: the rate is only meaningful with the number of attempts and the definition of a pass.

The selective evidence problem

The people who post payouts are the ones who received them. The traders who failed do not post, and the accounts that failed after a payout are rarely mentioned. The visible sample is biased toward success, which makes the industry look more profitable than it is. The bias is not unique to prop trading, but it is strong because the income is public and the failures are private.

The net-versus-gross problem

A payout is gross. The net figure subtracts the evaluation fees, the resets, the data costs and the taxes. A trader who received $5,000 in payouts but paid $3,000 in fees and taxes has a net of $2,000, which is a different story. The net figure is the one to compare with the time and the risk.

The time cost nobody counts

Prop trading income is usually expressed per month, which hides the time cost. A trader who spends twenty hours a week to earn $1,000 a month is earning $12.50 an hour before costs, which is below many alternatives. A trader who spends five hours a week for the same income is earning $50 an hour, which is more attractive.

The time cost varies with the strategy and the market. A scalper may need to be at the screen during specific sessions; a swing trader may need only a few checks a day. The income figure should be compared with the time spent, not just with the account size. A modest income from a low-time strategy can be a better outcome than a larger income from a full-time one, especially in the first year.

The time cost also includes the learning period. The first year may require hundreds of hours of preparation, journaling and review. That time is an investment, but it is not free. A trader who plans the income should plan the time as well, and should decide whether the expected return justifies the hours.

Assessing your own position

The most useful exercise is to assess your own position against the distribution rather than against the average. Six questions are enough.

  1. How many evaluations have you bought, and how many did you pass? The ratio is your current pass rate. If it is below one in three, the size or the plan fit is the likely cause.
  2. How many funded accounts have you kept for more than three months? The survival rate matters more than the pass rate, because the income comes from survival.
  3. What is your net income after fees and taxes? The gross payouts are not the income. Subtract the costs and the tax rate.
  4. How many hours a week do you spend? Divide the net income by the hours to get an effective hourly rate. Compare it with your alternatives.
  5. What is your worst losing streak, and did it breach the floor? The streak is the risk that decides survival. If it breached the floor, the size is too large.
  6. Do you have a written process that you followed on the last attempt? If not, the result is not repeatable, and the next attempt will be a new experiment.

The answers produce a profile: fee collector, one-payout trader, steady earner or large earner. The profile is not a judgement; it is a starting point. A fee collector who changes the size and the stop rule can become a one-payout trader within a few months. A one-payout trader who learns the payout terms and reduces size after a drawdown can become a steady earner. The progression is not guaranteed, but it is more likely than a sudden jump to large income.

The profile should be reviewed every quarter. A trader who is still a fee collector after four quarters has not changed the behaviour that causes the failures, regardless of how much strategy research they have done. A trader who has moved from fee collector to one-payout trader is making progress, even if the income is small. The review is the mechanism that turns the profile from a label into a plan.

The cost of survival

Survival has a cost that does not appear in the payout figure. A trader who keeps an account alive for a year has usually paid for platform subscriptions, data fees, resets and possibly a second evaluation after a failure. Those costs are the price of the survival record, and they should be subtracted from the payouts before the income is compared with the time.

The maintenance cost

A funded account that is not producing still costs money if it carries a monthly platform or data fee. A $50 monthly fee is $600 a year, which can be most of a small account's first-year payout. The fee is also a psychological cost: a trader who is paying for an idle account may feel pressure to trade it, which leads to overtrading. The maintenance cost is a reason to choose a firm with low recurring fees, not just a low evaluation fee.

The reset cost

Every failed attempt carries a reset or a new evaluation fee. A trader who fails twice a year has a reset cost that can exceed the profit from a small account. The reset cost is not a sign of failure; it is a normal part of the learning curve. But it should be budgeted, not discovered. A trader who keeps a reserve for two attempts has a more realistic view of the first-year cost than one who spends the budget on one attempt.

The opportunity cost

The time and capital spent on prop trading could be used for something else. A trader who spends a year and $2,000 in fees to earn $1,500 in net payouts has not made a profit in economic terms; the time could have been spent on a job, a course or a different business. The opportunity cost is not a reason to avoid prop trading, but it is a reason to be honest about the first-year return. A trader who enjoys the process and is building a skill may accept a negative first-year return; one who is treating it as an investment should expect a positive return or stop.

The cost of survival is the part of the income equation that marketing ignores. It is also the part that a trader can control: lower recurring fees, a reserve for resets and a realistic time budget reduce the cost and improve the net. The trader who plans for it is more likely to survive long enough to earn.

What a realistic path looks like

A realistic path for a new trader is a sequence of small improvements, not a leap. The first step is to pass one evaluation with a small account and a conservative size. The second is to keep the funded account for three months and receive one payout. The third is to repeat the process on a second attempt or a second account. The income grows with the account size and the survival record, not with a spectacular month.

The timeline is usually measured in quarters, not weeks. A trader who expects a first payout within three months is optimistic but not unrealistic; one who expects a full-time income within a year is likely to be disappointed. The traders who reach a full-time income usually have several years of experience and a record of surviving accounts, not just passing them.

The honest summary is that prop trading can produce a meaningful income for a small proportion of traders who treat it as a business, and that most traders earn little or nothing. The difference is not the strategy alone; it is the survival, the sizing, the payout discipline and the time. A trader who plans for that reality is more likely to be in the small group that earns.

A three-year view

A useful way to plan is to look at three years rather than three months. In year one, the goal is to pass one evaluation and receive one payout, even a small one. In year two, the goal is to keep a funded account for six months and receive several payouts. In year three, the goal is to add a second account or a larger account and to make the income more regular. The sequence is not a guarantee, but it is a pattern that many steady earners follow.

The three-year view changes the decisions. A trader in year one should prioritise the smallest account with the cleanest rules, not the largest account with the highest split. A trader in year two should prioritise survival and payout discipline, not a faster return. A trader in year three can consider scale, because the process has survived. Applying year-three decisions in year one is one of the most common reasons traders fail and quit before the curve turns.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the average prop firm trader make?

The average is misleading because it is pulled up by a small group of consistent earners. The median is much lower, and most traders who buy an evaluation never receive a payout.

What percentage of prop firm traders are profitable?

Public data is unreliable, but the shape is consistent: a small minority earn consistently, a larger group receive one or two small payouts, and most never pass or fail before withdrawing.

How soon do traders see a first meaningful payout?

The first payout usually arrives a few months after the first evaluation, and it is often small. A meaningful ongoing income takes much longer, because it depends on surviving several payout cycles rather than passing once.

Why do most traders fail before their first payout?

Mostly sizing and discipline rather than strategy. The daily budget is overspent, the stop rule is not followed, or the funded-account rules are not read before the first payout request.

Is a payout screenshot proof of income?

No. A screenshot does not show the fees paid, the failed attempts, the time spent or the accounts that failed afterwards. The net figure over a year is the meaningful number.

Can I earn a living from one funded account?

It depends on the account size and the return. A single small account rarely produces a living income; a larger account or several accounts can, if the trader survives long enough to withdraw consistently.

What is the most important behaviour for earning?

Survival: sizing from the daily budget, following a written stop and reading the payout terms. The strategy matters, but these behaviours decide whether the account lasts long enough to pay.
