Sizing guide · reviewed October 2026

How much money you need to trade micro futures, honestly

Micro contracts exist so that a strategy's stop distance can fit an account that would be too small for the full-size contract. But "fits" is not the same as "is worth doing": below a certain account size the fixed costs — data, platform, commissions — consume more than the edge produces. This page works out the account size from the strategy upward, what it costs to run, and where the line sits between a micro account that makes sense and one that is a monthly subscription with extra steps.

Key takeaways

  • A micro typically carries one tenth of the full-size contract's tick value, which is what makes a small account viable at all.
  • The binding question is not the minimum margin; it is whether the strategy's stop distance fits inside a survivable risk per trade.
  • Fixed costs — data feed, platform, commissions — set a floor on the account size below which the plan cannot pay for itself.
  • For an evaluation, use the smallest account whose micro sizing carries the strategy, not the largest you can finance.
  • Never let an account size dictate the stop distance; if the stop does not fit, the account is wrong, not the stop.

The question behind the question

"How much money do I need to trade micro futures" usually means one of three things. How much capital is required to open a position at all. How much is required to trade a specific plan without the position size rounding to zero. Or how much is required to make a living from it. The three answers differ by an order of magnitude, and confusing them is expensive.

The first question has a small answer, because a micro contract's margin requirement is a small number and a broker will happily open a position with a few hundred dollars. That number is not a plan; it is the threshold at which the platform permits a trade. A trader who funds to that threshold is one normal losing sequence away from an account that cannot hold the position it has.

The second question is the useful one, and it has an arithmetic answer that depends entirely on the strategy. The stop distance the setup requires, the dollar risk the plan can absorb per trade, and the tick value of the contract between them determine a size. If that size is zero, the account is too small for the plan — and the correct response is a smaller contract, a bigger account, or a different plan, never a wider stop.

The third question — can this pay a living — follows from the second and from the fixed costs. On a funded account, the answer depends on the allocation size, the profit split and the payout cadence rather than on the contract. On a personal account, it depends on the edge in dollars rather than in ticks. Both are covered below, and both are more modest than most marketing suggests.

What a micro contract actually changes

A micro future is economically the same instrument as its full-size counterpart — the same underlying, the same price action, the same contract months — with a smaller contract multiplier. In practice, a micro index future typically carries one tenth of the tick value of the full-size contract: where the full-size contract moves a fixed dollar amount per tick, the micro moves a tenth of that.

Two things follow, and they are the reason micros exist. First, dollar risk scales down by the same factor while the stop distance in ticks stays identical. A strategy whose stop is thirty ticks away risks the same thirty ticks of market movement, at a tenth of the dollars. Second, position size granularity improves: the full-size contract forces whole-number jumps that can double risk, while the micro sits at a tenth of that increment, so the derived size tracks the plan far more closely.

What a micro does not change is equally important. Commissions are charged per contract per round turn, so trading ten micros costs in the same region as trading one full-size contract — the multipliers are smaller but the contract count is larger. The data feed is usually identical, and priced the same. And the market's behaviour — spread, slippage in fast conditions, the settlement window — is unchanged, because it is the same order book.

The consequence is a trade-off that decides whether a small account is viable. A micro reduces the per-trade dollar risk to a tenth, which makes a small account survivable; it does not reduce the fixed monthly costs, which are set by subscriptions rather than by contract size. On an account below a certain size, the second effect dominates the first, and the plan cannot pay for the tools it needs. Starting from the stop, not the balance

The mistake almost every account-size question contains is that it starts with a balance. The correct starting point is the strategy's stop distance, because that is the parameter the market imposes and the trader cannot negotiate.

Measure the stop in ticks on the instrument and timeframe the plan actually trades. A scalping approach on an index future might work with a stop of a handful of ticks; a swing approach on the same instrument might need thirty or more. The number is a property of the setup, and it should be measured on historical examples rather than chosen for convenience.

Then multiply by the tick value of the contract being considered — full-size or micro — to get the dollar cost of the stop at one contract. That figure is the minimum risk the plan can take per trade. It is not adjustable downward: the only way to risk less on the same setup is a smaller contract multiplier, which is exactly why micros exist.

There is a second measurement to make before sizing: the realised cost of a round turn at the hours the plan trades. Spread plus commission plus average slippage on entry and exit. On a plan with a small expectation per trade, that cost is a meaningful fraction of the risk, and on a micro account the commission is charged on more contracts for the same economic exposure, so it deserves measuring rather than assuming.

Once the stop cost and the transaction cost are known, the account-size question becomes answerable: the account must be large enough that a survivable number of consecutive losses fits inside the drawdown the trader is willing to accept, while leaving room for the fixed monthly costs. That is the whole calculation, and it starts from the market rather than from the balance.

The drawdown floor sets the size

With the stop cost known, the position size comes from the drawdown — the amount the account can lose before it is no longer viable, whether that is a firm's maximum loss or a self-imposed floor.

Divide the drawdown by the number of consecutive losses the plan must survive. Eight to ten is a reasonable design case for a discretionary plan with a win rate in the forties, because runs of that length recur within a few hundred trades. The result is the maximum dollar loss per trade; the contract count is that figure divided by the cost of the stop, rounded down.

On an evaluation account the drawdown is published, which makes the calculation mechanical. On a personal account it is a choice, and the honest choice is the amount the trader can lose without changing their life — not the amount the broker permits. A personal account funded to the margin minimum has a drawdown of nearly zero in practical terms, because the first normal losing sequence removes the ability to hold the position.

Keep a quarter of the drawdown in reserve, and recompute whenever the drawdown available changes — which on an evaluation with a trailing floor is every week. The trailing drawdown guide covers the room formula, and the risk calculator sets out the same derivation in step form.

One further constraint binds earlier than most traders expect: the daily loss limit. If the daily limit permits only two losses at the derived size, the session is decided by its first two trades and the plan will spend many sessions on the sideline. Four to six losses of capacity is a workable design target, and if the derived size cannot deliver that, the size is too large rather than too small.

The minimum viable account

The minimum viable account is the smallest size at which three conditions hold at once: the derived position size is at least one contract, the daily limit absorbs four losses, and the monthly fixed costs are a small fraction of the expected monthly profit.

The first condition is the one usually checked, and the second is usually forgotten. A plan that fits at one micro but whose daily limit absorbs only two losses is not viable on that account — it is viable on an account twice the size, or with a stop distance half as wide. Trader after trader solves the sizing problem and then fails the session problem, because the two are calculated separately.

The third condition is the one that surprises personal-account traders. Data feeds and platforms are monthly subscriptions, and on a small account they can exceed the expected monthly edge before it is realised. A plan producing a modest expectation per trade over a hundred trades a month may generate an amount comparable to the cost of the tools, which means the account is not yet a business — it is a training cost, and should be budgeted as one.

Expressed as a rule: the account should be large enough that the derived size is at least one contract at the strategy's real stop, the daily limit absorbs four losses at that size, and the monthly subscriptions are under a quarter of the plan's expected monthly profit. When all three hold, the account is viable and can be scaled by adding contracts rather than by changing the plan.

Costs that make small accounts unprofitable

Four costs scale badly on a small account, and together they explain why so many micro traders describe their first year as a subscription rather than a business.

Market data. Exchange fees for real-time depth are charged per exchange and are usually bundled per month. A trader watching two index products is paying for two feeds whether or not the plan trades that week. Delayed data is free and adequate for a swing plan with wide stops; for a scalping plan on the opening range it is not.

Platform subscription. Often the same across firms and sometimes waived with a minimum commission volume. On a small account the waiver threshold is rarely reached, so the subscription is a fixed monthly cost against a small edge.

Commission per contract. Charged per round turn, which means a plan that trades ten micros pays roughly what a plan trading one full-size contract pays. A high-frequency micro plan can therefore have a higher cost per dollar of profit than the full-size equivalent — the opposite of the intuition that micros are cheaper.

Slippage in fast conditions. Unchanged by contract size, because it is a property of the order book. On a plan that trades the first minutes of a session, slippage can exceed commission, and on a small account it is the largest single drag on the measured expectation.

Two responses make the costs manageable. The first is to choose tools by the plan rather than by habit: a swing plan does not need real-time depth on two exchanges. The second is to measure the realised cost per round turn over a month and put it into the expectation calculation, because a plan whose edge is smaller than its cost is not a plan — it is a subscription with a chart attached. What a micro account can realistically pay

Income expectations follow from the same arithmetic, and on micro contracts the honest numbers are modest at small sizes.

Take a plan with a measured expectation of a fraction of a contract's tick value per trade, two trades a session and twenty sessions a month. Multiply to get an expected monthly result in dollars, then subtract commissions and subscriptions. On a single micro contract the gross figure is often a few hundred dollars a month, and the net figure after costs is smaller. That is not a reason to avoid micros; it is a reason to treat them as a way of building and validating a plan rather than as income.

The scale that changes the picture is contracts, not tactics. Eight micros produce eight times the dollars at the same stop distance and the same win rate, which is why a funded evaluation account is a legitimate route: a firm's allocation is a multiple of anything most traders would fund personally, and the profit split applies to that allocation rather than to a personal balance.

On a funded account the constraint moves to the payout conditions. The consistency rule caps a single day's share of withdrawn profit, so the largest day sets the floor on total profit; a $500 best day under a one-third rule needs $1,500 of total profit before a full withdrawal. On a modest allocation the first payout is therefore a function of patience rather than of size, which is why a daily profit cap belongs in the plan from the first funded session. Evaluation accounts versus personal accounts

The same instrument is available through a personal account and through a firm's evaluation, and the two have different economics for a micro trader.

A personal account costs the balance plus subscriptions plus commission. Its advantage is that there are no rules: no daily limit, no consistency ratio, no target. Its disadvantage is that the balance is the ceiling — a trader with $2,000 cannot trade eight micros without the drawdown consuming the account in a normal losing week.

An evaluation costs a fee and its subscriptions, and it comes with a published drawdown, a daily limit and a target. The advantage is size: a $50,000 allocation supports a micro position count that a personal balance of the same size could not sustain with the same risk, and the funded stage pays a split on the allocation. The disadvantage is the rulebook, and the fact that a failed attempt is a sunk cost.

For most micro traders the evaluation route is the better first step, with one condition: the account size must be chosen from the sizing arithmetic rather than from the marketing. A trader whose plan fits at two micros on a $25,000 account gains nothing from a $150,000 allocation except a larger fee and a habit of oversizing. Worked examples at four account sizes

The same strategy measured against four account sizes, with placeholder contract values. The pattern is the transferable part: size grows in steps, not smoothly, because contracts do not subdivide.

The strategy. One index future traded intraday, a 30-tick stop, a measured 45% win rate at a 1:2 reward-to-risk ratio, two setups a session, twenty sessions a month. Assume a tick value of $1.25 on the micro and $12.50 on the full-size contract, and a per-contract round-turn commission.

$5,000 account, micro. A 10% drawdown is $500; divided by a streak tolerance of ten, risk per trade is $50. A 30-tick stop costs $37.50 at one micro, so the derived size is one micro with a thin margin. The daily limit at 5% is $250, which absorbs six losses at $37.50 — the session works. The account is viable at one micro, and the expected monthly result before costs is small.

$25,000 account, micro. Drawdown $2,500, risk per trade $250, stop cost $37.50, derived size six micros. Daily limit $1,250 absorbs five losing trades at $225. Monthly expectation is six times the $5,000 account's, which is the first size at which subscriptions are comfortably covered.

$50,000 account, full-size. Drawdown $5,000, risk per trade $500, stop cost $375 at one full-size contract, derived size one contract. The daily limit at $2,500 absorbs six losses. Note the jump: the step from six micros to one full-size contract is a 67% increase in exposure that cannot be tuned, which is why the transition is the most dangerous point in the progression.

$150,000 account, full-size. Drawdown $15,000, risk per trade $1,500, so four full-size contracts, or three hundred micros. Either way the arithmetic is identical in ticks and different in dollars — which is the point worth remembering: the market does not know which contract size is being traded, and the plan does not change because the balance did.

When to move to full-size contracts

The move from micros to full-size is a step change rather than a ramp, and three conditions should be true before taking it.

First, the micro plan has a measured edge over a meaningful sample. Not a good month: a few hundred trades with a recorded win rate, average win, average loss and realised cost per round turn. The measurement is the point of the micro phase, and it is much cheaper to obtain there than on a full-size contract.

Second, the account can carry a full-size contract at the same risk per trade as the micro version. That means the drawdown must be large enough that one full-size contract's stop cost equals a tenth of the drawdown, which usually means an account ten times the size of the micro account — or a decision to accept a larger fraction of risk per trade, which is not the same plan.

Third, the cost per round turn remains a small fraction of the risk. On the transition this is often true, because the full-size contract carries ten times the risk and only a modestly larger commission. The cost problem is a micro problem, which is one honest argument for the move.

Where the intermediate step is unavailable — an account too large for one micro but too small for a full-size contract at the required stop — the answer is usually more micros rather than a full-size contract, since micros scale in tenths. The transition should be made when the arithmetic says the size has landed on a full-size contract, not because traders are expected to graduate. Four sizing mistakes

Four errors appear repeatedly on small futures accounts, and each is arithmetic rather than psychological.

Sizing from the margin requirement. Margin is the deposit the broker requires, not the risk the position carries. A contract that can be opened with a few hundred dollars can still lose many times that if the stop is wide, and the margin figure says nothing about it.

Keeping the stop distance and shrinking the account. If the account shrinks, the size shrinks — not the stop. Widening a stop to make a position fit is the same as removing it, and it converts a planned loss into an unbounded one.

Ignoring the daily limit when the drawdown looks generous. A large maximum loss with a small daily limit produces a plan that fits the account and cannot hold a session. The daily limit is the constraint that ends attempts, and it has to be sized in as carefully as the floor.

Adding contracts instead of markets. A trader whose size has hit the daily limit's ceiling and who wants more return has two options: a larger account, or a different plan with a tighter stop. Trading a second instrument at the same size doubles the daily exposure without doubling the daily limit, which is how an account that was passing becomes one that fails. The daily drawdown guide covers the reference-point arithmetic that makes this visible.

There is a fifth mistake that is really a misreading of the first four: treating the contract count as a measure of progress. A trader who has moved from two micros to four has not improved; they have increased the dollar risk of the same plan. Progress on a futures account is measured by the ratio of the plan's expectation to its risk, by the consistency of the record, and by the size of the account the plan can be run on — in that order. Contract count is a consequence of the third, not evidence of the first two.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to trade micro futures?

Enough that the drawdown covers ten consecutive losses at the strategy's real stop distance, and enough that the daily limit absorbs four losses at that size. On a 30-tick stop with a micro tick value, that usually means a few thousand dollars minimum, and considerably more before subscriptions are comfortably covered.

What is the minimum account to open a micro futures position?

The margin requirement, which is a small number — often a few hundred dollars. That is the threshold at which the platform permits a trade, not a viable plan. Funding to it means the first normal losing sequence removes the ability to hold a position.

Is a micro contract just a tenth of the full-size contract?

In economic terms, typically yes: the same underlying and the same contract months with a tick value around a tenth of the full-size contract. The data feed and the order book are the same, so spread and slippage are unchanged.

How many micros should I trade?

Divide the drawdown by the number of consecutive losses you must survive, then divide by the dollar cost of the stop distance at one micro, and round down. Check the result against the daily limit — four losses of capacity is a workable minimum.

Are micros more expensive to trade than full-size contracts?

Per dollar of risk, usually yes, because commission is charged per contract per round turn and ten micros cost in the same region as one full-size contract. Per unit of market exposure the cost is comparable; the disadvantage appears on high-frequency micro plans.

What fixed costs should I budget for?

Market data per exchange and a platform subscription, both monthly, plus commission per round turn and expected slippage. The rule of thumb: keep monthly subscriptions under a quarter of the plan's expected monthly profit, or the account is still a training cost.

Can I make a living trading one micro contract?

Realistically no, unless the plan trades large size in ticks. A single micro produces a few hundred dollars a month at typical expectations before costs. Scale comes from contracts or from a funded allocation, not from tactics.

Should I use a prop firm evaluation instead of my own account?

Often yes, because the allocation is a multiple of a typical personal balance and the funded stage pays a split on it. Choose the account size from the sizing arithmetic rather than from the marketing, and read the funded terms before paying the fee.

When should I move from micros to full-size contracts?

When the micro plan has a measured edge over a few hundred trades, the account can carry a full-size contract at the same fraction of risk, and the cost per round turn is still small relative to the risk. The move is a step change, so make it deliberately.

What if my strategy's stop does not fit any size that rounds up?

Then the account or the instrument is wrong, not the stop. Options: a smaller contract multiplier, a larger account, or a plan with a tighter stop on a more appropriate timeframe. Widening a stop to fit a position is the one response that converts a planned loss into an unplanned one.

Does a daily loss limit change the account size I need?

Yes. If the daily limit only absorbs two losses at your derived size, the session is decided by its first trades. Size down until the limit absorbs four to six losses, which usually means a larger account or a wider daily limit relative to the drawdown.

Does a passing service make sense for a micro account?

It makes sense where the firm's terms permit third-party trading, which must be confirmed for the evaluation and the funded account separately.
