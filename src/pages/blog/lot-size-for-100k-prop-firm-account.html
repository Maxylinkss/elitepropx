Lot Size for a $100,000 Prop Firm Account

Lot size is not a setting you pick once. It is the output of three numbers: the dollar risk the account can afford, the distance to the stop, and the value of one pip on the instrument. Get those three right and the lot size follows; get any one wrong and the account is carrying a risk the rulebook never priced. This guide works the arithmetic on a $100,000 account across forex, gold and indices, and shows where the margin check and the daily loss limit change the answer.

Key takeaways

  • The formula is lot size = dollar risk ÷ (stop distance × pip value per lot). Nothing else belongs in it.
  • On a $100,000 account, 0.5% risk is $500. A 20-pip stop on EURUSD gives 2.5 standard lots, not 5.
  • Pip value is not always $10 per standard lot. It depends on the pair and on the account currency.
  • Gold and index contracts have their own contract sizes, and sizing them like a currency pair is the most common error.
  • The daily loss rule caps the total, not the trade. Ten 0.5% risks exactly consume a 5% daily limit.

Most sizing mistakes are not arithmetic errors. They are unit errors: pips mixed with points, dollars mixed with account currency, a contract size assumed rather than checked. A trader who divides correctly but uses the wrong pip value gets an answer that looks precise and is wrong by a factor of ten.

The discipline that avoids this is to write the three inputs down before every trade. Risk in dollars, stop in the instrument's own units, value of one unit per lot. The lot size is then a calculation rather than a feeling, and the calculation can be checked by anyone, including a reviewer reading the trade history.

The only formula you need

Position size in lots is the dollar risk divided by the loss per lot at the stop.

StepCalculationExample
1. Dollar riskAccount × risk percentage$100,000 × 0.5% = $500
2. Stop distanceEntry minus stop, in pips1.0850 − 1.0830 = 20 pips
3. Pip value per lotContract size × pip size100,000 × 0.0001 = $10
4. Loss per lotStop × pip value20 × $10 = $200
5. Lot sizeDollar risk ÷ loss per lot$500 ÷ $200 = 2.5 lots

The result is a position of 2.5 standard lots, or 250,000 units of the base currency. That is a notional exposure of 2.5 times the account, which sounds aggressive until the leverage is considered: at 1:100 margin it requires $2,500 of margin, and the actual risk at the stop is $500, or 0.5% of the account. The notional multiple and the risk are different numbers, and confusing them is one of the six errors below.

The formula holds for any instrument once the stop and the per-lot value are expressed in the same units. The rest of this guide is about getting those two numbers right.

Choosing the dollar risk

The risk percentage is a policy decision, and on a prop account it is constrained by three rules at once: the maximum loss, the daily loss and, often, the consistency threshold.

Risk per tradeDollar riskLosses to a 10% max lossLosses to a 5% daily loss
0.25%$2504020
0.50%$5002010
1.00%$1,000105
2.00%$2,00052.5
5.00%$5,00021

The third and fourth columns are the argument for small size. At 1% risk, five consecutive losses consume an entire 5% daily limit — and five losses in a row is a common event for a strategy with a 50% win rate, occurring roughly 3% of the time in any five-trade sequence, which is often within a month of active trading. At 0.5%, ten losses are needed for the same outcome, which is a much rarer sequence.

The common recommendation for an evaluation is 0.25% to 0.5% per trade. Below that, the target takes an impractical number of wins; above it, a normal losing streak breaches the daily limit before the method has had a chance to work. A trader who cannot reach the target at 0.5% risk usually has a target problem, not a sizing problem — the answer is a longer schedule, not a larger position.

Pip value, pair by pair

A pip is the fourth decimal in most pairs, and the value of one pip per standard lot depends on which currency the pair is quoted in.

Pair typeExampleValue of 1 pip per standard lotNote
XXX/USDEURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD$10.00The simplest case; the quote currency is the account currency
USD/XXXUSDJPY, USDCHF, USDCADVaries with the rateDivide 0.0001 by the rate, then multiply by 100,000
JPY pairsUSDJPY at 150.00≈ $6.67The pip is 0.01, and the value falls as the rate rises
CrossesEURGBP, GBPJPYVaries with both ratesConvert the quote currency to the account currency
MetalsXAUUSD$1 per $0.01 move per 0.01 lotContract size 100 ounces; a $1 move is $100 per lot

The JPY row is where the first unit error appears. On USDJPY at 150.00, a standard lot is 100,000 dollars and a one-pip move is 0.01 yen per dollar, or 1,000 yen — which converts to about $6.67. Using $10 instead overstates risk by 50%, so a trader who sizes for $500 of risk is actually risking $750. The error is invisible because the number looks plausible.

The crosses add a second conversion. On EURGBP, the pip value is in pounds, and a dollar-based account has to convert at the current GBPUSD rate. A calculator that ignores the conversion is wrong by the exchange rate, which is a 20–30% error rather than a small one.

Two things follow. First, the lot size is inversely proportional to the stop, which is why a "standard" lot size does not exist. A trader who always trades one lot is risking ten times more on a 10-pip stop than on a 100-pip stop, even though the trade looks the same on the chart.

Second, the top-left cells are impractical on a retail platform. Ten lots at 1% risk on a 10-pip stop is a $1,000,000 notional position, which will hit a margin or position limit at many firms before the size is reached. When the table produces a size that the platform will not accept, the answer is a wider stop or a smaller risk, not a request for more leverage.

Gold: a different contract

Gold is where forex habits cause the largest sizing errors. A standard XAUUSD lot is 100 ounces, so the notional is the price multiplied by 100 — at $2,000 an ounce, one lot is $200,000 of exposure, twice the account.

Stop in goldLoss per standard lotLots at $500 riskNotional at $2,000/oz

The first row is the trap. A five-lot gold position with a one-dollar stop risks $500 and carries a million dollars of notional, which will exceed the margin on most accounts and may breach a leverage or position cap even where margin allows it. The trade is "correctly sized" by risk and unusable in practice, which is why gold sizing has to be checked against margin and caps as well as risk.

Gold's volatility makes the second issue worse. A one-dollar stop on gold is tight by the instrument's standards, so a trader who is used to 20-pip forex stops will find that gold stops need to be wider in dollar terms — which reduces the lot size further. The correct order is stop first, then size, then margin check, not size first and stop fitted around it.

Indices and their point values

Index CFDs are quoted in points, and the value of a point per lot varies by contract and by firm. Some quote one currency unit per point, some ten, and some use a contract multiplier that is not obvious from the ticket.

Contract typeTypical point value per lotNotional at 5,000Stop of 20 points
1 unit per point$1$5,000$20 per lot
10 units per point$10$50,000$200 per lot
100 units per point$100$500,000$2,000 per lot

The third row is unusual in retail but exists in some contracts, and a trader who assumes the first row is off by a factor of a hundred. The check is always the same: place a one-lot demo trade, move it one point, and read the P&L. That takes a minute and removes the assumption entirely.

A second index-specific issue is the session. Index CFDs often have a maintenance break and a cash-session open, and a stop placed during the break is not a stop that can be executed. A stop that is 20 points away at 17:00 may be several hundred points away at the 17:30 reopen, which converts a planned loss into a gap. Size for the overnight gap, not only for the intraday stop.

The margin check

Risk and margin are different constraints, and a position can satisfy one while breaching the other. Margin is the collateral the broker requires to hold the position; risk is the loss at the stop. A trade can have low risk and high margin, or the reverse.

PositionNotionalMargin at 1:100Margin at 1:30
2.5 lots EURUSD$250,000$2,500$8,333
5.0 lots EURUSD$500,000$5,000$16,667
1.0 lot XAUUSD at $2,000$200,000$2,000$6,667
5.0 lots XAUUSD at $2,000$1,000,000$10,000$33,333

On a $100,000 account, the last row consumes a third of the account as margin at 1:30. That is survivable until it is not: a margin call arrives when equity falls, and equity falls fastest when the position is largest. The margin number also interacts with the drawdown rule, because a position that consumes margin limits the trader's ability to add or to hedge — which is exactly when the ability matters.

The practical check is to keep total margin below about 20% of the account at all times. That leaves room for a second position, for a widened stop and for the equity decline that precedes a breach. A trade that requires more than that is a size decision made by the platform rather than by the plan.

Budgeting against the daily loss rule

The daily loss limit is a budget, and each trade spends from it. That makes the risk per trade a fraction of the daily limit, not a fraction of the account in isolation.

Daily limitRisk per trade at 10% of limitTrades before the limitAppropriate for
3% ($3,000)$300 (0.3%)10Intraday, several setups a day
5% ($5,000)$500 (0.5%)10The common middle case
5% ($5,000)$1,000 (1.0%)5Swing, one or two positions
2% ($2,000)$200 (0.2%)10Futures with tight daily limits

Ten trades before the limit is a useful default because a normal losing streak rarely exceeds six or seven for a decent method. Five is workable for a trader who takes one or two positions a day. Two or three is not a budget; it is a single bad morning.

The budget also has to account for correlation. Three positions in correlated pairs are one idea, and their combined risk is the sum, not the average. A trader with a $5,000 daily limit and three correlated positions at $500 each has $1,500 at risk on a single view — 30% of the budget — which is a different proposition from three independent trades.

Six sizing errors that end accounts

The sixth is small and constant. Rounding a 2.5-lot calculation to 3 lots raises the risk from 0.5% to 0.6%, which over fifty trades is a material difference in the drawdown distribution. Rounding down costs a little profit and never breaches a limit.

The fifth is the one that turns a controlled loss into a breach. Averaging down feels like improving the entry price, and in risk terms it is doubling the position at the worst moment. If the plan allows adds, the total risk has to be defined before the first entry, not discovered after the second.

A one-page sizing worksheet

Five lines, filled before every trade. The discipline is the point; the arithmetic is trivial once the inputs are explicit.

  1. Account equity today — not the starting balance. A trailing floor and a reduced equity change the dollar risk.
  2. Risk percentage — from the plan, fixed in advance, not chosen per trade.
  3. Dollar risk — equity × percentage.
  4. Stop distance in the instrument's units — pips for forex, dollars for gold, points for indices.
  5. Value per unit per lot — converted to the account currency.

Then lot size = dollar risk ÷ (stop × value per unit), rounded down. Two sanity checks follow: the margin required should be under 20% of equity, and the total risk across open positions should be under 10% of the daily limit per position and under the limit in aggregate.

A trader who fills these five lines for a month will find that the sizing decision stops being a decision. That is the goal: the risk becomes a constant, and the only variable left is whether the setup is good enough to take.

Sizing through a losing streak

A fixed percentage of equity reduces risk automatically as the account falls. That is the correct behaviour, and it is the opposite of what most traders do after a loss.

Equity0.5% risk, fixed percentage1% risk, fixed percentageFixed 2 lots at 20-pip stop
$100,000$500$1,000$400
$98,000$490$980$400
$95,000$475$950$400
$92,000$460$920$400
$90,000$450$900$400

The third column is the fixed-lot approach, and it is the one that ends accounts. Risk stays at $400 while the room to the floor shrinks from $10,000 to $0, which means the fifth loss takes a larger share of the remaining room than the first. The percentage columns shrink with the account, so each loss consumes a constant fraction of what is left rather than an increasing one.

The same logic applies upwards. A trader whose equity has grown should keep the percentage fixed, not the lot size, because the dollar risk grows with the account and the floor does not move on a static plan. On a trailing plan the floor moves with profit, so the percentage approach is doubly important.

One exception is worth noting: after a losing streak, some traders deliberately reduce risk below the percentage for a session or two. That is a psychological tool rather than a mathematical one, and it is harmless if the reduced risk is restored once the streak ends. It is harmful if it becomes permanent, because the account then runs a smaller risk than the plan can support and the target becomes unreachable.

Futures contracts and tick values

Futures sizing uses ticks rather than pips, and each contract has its own specification. The formula is unchanged: contract risk is the tick value multiplied by the number of ticks to the stop.

ContractTick sizeTick valueRisk on a 20-tick stop
Micro equity index0.25 points$1.25$25
Micro crude oil0.01$1.00$20
Micro gold0.10$1.00$20
Standard equity index0.25 points$12.50$250

The micro contracts are the reason small futures accounts are viable at all. On a $100,000 account with a $500 risk unit, a micro index contract on a 20-tick stop risks $25, so twenty contracts fit the budget. On the standard contract the same stop risks $250, so two contracts fit and the granularity is coarse.

Contract granularity is the hidden constraint in futures sizing. A trader who needs $500 of risk but can only trade in $250 increments may find that one contract is too small and two is too large. Micro contracts solve this at the low end; at the high end, the answer is a wider stop rather than more contracts, because the stop distance is the only continuous variable.

The daily loss limit interacts here too. A 5% daily limit on a $100,000 futures account is $5,000, and a trader with a $250 risk per contract can absorb twenty losing contracts in a day. That sounds generous until a correlated basket is counted: ten contracts across two correlated indices is one position at $2,500, or half the daily limit on a single view.

Auditing your sizing from the statement

The statement shows what was risked, not what was intended, and the difference is the useful information. Four checks take about ten minutes a week.

  1. Risk per trade in dollars. For each closed trade, compute the loss at the stop and compare it with the plan. A cluster of trades at 0.9% when the plan says 0.5% is a sizing drift.
  2. Risk as a share of equity at the time. Use the equity on the trade date, not the starting balance. This is where a fixed-lot habit appears.
  3. Worst single day. The largest daily loss as a share of the daily limit. Above 50% regularly means the size is too large for the rulebook.
  4. Correlation on the worst day. Whether the losses came from one idea expressed several times. This is the check that explains most breaches.

The first two are arithmetic; the third is the early warning; the fourth is the diagnosis. A trader who runs all four weekly will see a sizing problem before it becomes a breach, and will see the correlation that a per-trade risk view hides.

The audit is also the evidence trail. If a firm later asks why a particular position was that size, the calculation and the equity at the time answer the question. A trader who sized by feel has no answer, and the absence of a reason is harder to defend than a small error.

A useful habit is to record the three inputs — equity, risk percentage and stop distance — next to each trade in the journal. The journal then reconciles against the statement, and any difference between intended risk and realised risk becomes visible rather than suspected. Most sizing problems are found this way: not by a single bad trade, but by a line of trades whose recorded risk drifts upward over a few weeks.

The drift is usually caused by success. A trader who wins three trades in a row feels confident, rounds the lot size up, and the fourth trade risks 0.7% instead of 0.5%. Two more wins and the rounding compounds. The percentage is still in the plan; the execution has quietly moved. Checking the recorded risk against the plan every week catches it before a losing streak does.

Common questions

What lot size should I use on a $100,000 prop account?

It depends on the stop, not the account. At 0.5% risk ($500) and a 20-pip stop on a pair where a pip is $10 per lot, the size is 2.5 standard lots. On a 50-pip stop it is 1.0 lot. Calculate from the stop every time.

Is 1 lot too big for a $100,000 account?

Not in itself. One lot with a 50-pip stop risks about $500, or 0.5%. The same lot with a 10-pip stop risks $100, and with a 100-pip stop $1,000. The lot size is meaningless without the stop distance.

What percentage should each trade risk during a challenge?

Commonly 0.25% to 0.5% of the account. That gives ten to twenty losses before a 10% maximum loss is reached and five to ten before a 5% daily limit is consumed. Above 1% per trade, a normal losing streak breaches the daily rule.

Why is my pip value not $10?

Because the pair is not quoted in your account currency. On USDJPY the pip value depends on the rate, and on crosses it depends on the quote currency's exchange rate. Only pairs quoted in your account currency give a fixed $10 per standard lot.

How do I size a gold trade?

One standard lot is 100 ounces, so a $1 move is $100 per lot. At $500 risk and a $5 stop, the size is 1.0 lot, which is roughly $200,000 of notional at $2,000 an ounce. Check margin as well as risk.

Does leverage change my risk?

No. Leverage changes the margin required, not the loss at the stop. A position that risks $500 risks $500 at any leverage; the difference is how much of the account is tied up as collateral and how quickly a margin call arrives.

Should I size from the account balance or the equity?

From equity, because that is what the drawdown rule measures. After a losing streak, the same percentage of a smaller equity is a smaller dollar risk, which is the correct response. Sizing from the starting balance keeps risk constant in dollars while the room to the floor shrinks.

What if the calculated size exceeds the platform maximum?

Widen the stop, reduce the risk percentage, or choose a smaller contract. Requesting more leverage does not solve it: the position is too large for the account's margin and position limits, and the correct answer is a smaller position rather than a larger allowance.
