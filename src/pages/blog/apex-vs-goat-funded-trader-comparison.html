Apex vs GOAT Funded Trader: futures rulebooks compared

Apex and GOAT Funded Trader both sell futures evaluations, and both are often compared on account size and price. The differences that decide outcomes are elsewhere: how the trailing threshold is calculated, whether a daily stop exists, how many trading days are required, how the payout cycle works and which platform the account runs on. This comparison puts those rows side by side, models the same trade on both, and gives you a method to choose without relying on marketing pages.

Key takeaways

  • Both firms use a trailing threshold on futures accounts, so the calculation method matters more than the headline percentage.
  • A daily stop — where it exists — changes the sizing more than the target does.
  • Consistency rules and payout cycles decide whether a clean pass becomes money.
  • Compare the plan documents, not the brand pages, and confirm the current terms before paying.

Where the two firms are similar

Both Apex and GOAT operate futures evaluations with single-phase options, a trailing or end-of-day loss threshold, and a one-time fee rather than a monthly subscription. Both offer a menu of account sizes, both use the major futures platforms, and both pay a share of the funded profit. The similarities are why the comparison is useful: the products are close enough that the differences in the rules decide the outcome.

There is one structural difference to settle first. Apex sells one evaluation with two drawdown models: an EOD threshold recalculated at the close, or an intraday trail that follows peak balance including unrealised profit. GOAT sells several futures products, including GOAT Daily, which uses an end-of-day trailing drawdown, a daily limit and no activation fee, alongside one-step and two-step models with their own targets. The brand does not have a single rulebook, so the plan name has to be part of the comparison.

The one-time fee changes the cost arithmetic. Neither firm charges a monthly evaluation subscription, so a slow attempt does not increase the fee; what it does increase is the chance of hitting the access deadline or the consistency rule. On a one-time product, the constraint on pace is the calendar and the rulebook, not the invoice.

The trailing threshold

The trailing threshold is the rule that ends most futures attempts. It is a maximum loss that follows the account's peak, and the calculation method determines how quickly the floor rises.

Intraday trailing

An intraday trailing threshold follows the account's peak equity continuously, including unrealised profit. A position that goes $500 into profit raises the floor by $500, and the floor does not come back down if the position retraces. The practical effect is that a winning trade can permanently reduce the room, and a normal pullback can end the account even though the balance is higher than at the start. This is the most demanding version of the rule.

End-of-day trailing

An end-of-day trailing threshold updates once, at the daily close, on realised profit. The intraday peak does not move the floor; only the closing balance does. The rule is more forgiving because a position that goes into profit and retraces does not tighten the floor unless the profit is banked at the close. A trader who holds through a pullback keeps the room that existed at the previous close.

Why the method matters more than the percentage

A 6% intraday trailing threshold and a 6% end-of-day trailing threshold have the same headline number but are different products. The intraday version is harder because the floor moves with unrealised gains, which are the least stable part of the account. A trader comparing the two firms should identify the method for the specific plan, not just the percentage. The percentage is the size of the room; the method is how fast the room disappears.

The threshold also determines the sizing in the middle of the challenge. On an intraday trailing account, the trader should reduce size as profit accumulates, because the floor is rising. On an end-of-day account, the size can stay constant until the close, because the floor only moves once. The same profit produces different risk plans depending on the method.

Daily stops and targets

The daily stop and the target define the session and the distance. Their interaction is the challenge's shape.

The daily stop

A daily stop limits the loss in a single session. Where it exists, it is usually measured in dollars and resets at a defined time. The daily stop is the first constraint to bind on a volatile day, and it changes the sizing: a trader who risks a third of the daily stop per trade can absorb three losses before the session ends. Where a firm has no separate daily stop, the trailing threshold is the only loss constraint, and a bad day is limited only by the threshold.

The target

The target is the profit required to pass. On a futures evaluation, it is usually a dollar figure, which removes the percentage ambiguity but adds a contract-value question: the target is fixed in dollars, while the value of a point varies by instrument. A trader using micro contracts needs more points to reach the same dollar target, which takes longer but risks less per contract.

The ratio

The ratio of target to threshold is the difficulty in one number. A $6,000 target against a $6,000 threshold is a 1:1 challenge; a $6,000 target against a $4,000 threshold is 1.5:1, which is harder. The ratio should be compared before the fee, because it determines how many normal losses the challenge can absorb relative to the distance it requires.

Calendar and minimum days

This is another row where the brand-level assumption fails. Apex requires no minimum number of trading days and allows a pass in a single session; its calendar constraint is the 30-day access period. GOAT's futures products also publish no minimum-day condition on their daily model, but the one-step and two-step variants have their own rules. The minimum-day question has to be answered per plan, not per brand.

Why a minimum can exist

Where a firm imposes one, the minimum prevents a trader from passing with a single large trade and then withdrawing without demonstrating a process. It also gives the firm more data on the account's behaviour before funding. The rule is reasonable, but it changes the optimal pace: a trader who reaches the target in three days must keep trading until the minimum is satisfied, which adds exposure after the goal is met.

How to plan around it

Where a minimum applies, spread the target across at least that many days, with a buffer for a flat day. A target that requires one exceptional trade to finish is fragile on a minimum-day plan, because the trader may reach the target early and then have to trade flat for several days. Where no minimum applies, as at Apex, the better plan is to stop at the target rather than trade out the calendar.

Time limits

Apex gives 30 days of access and no extension. GOAT's products vary, with some marketed as having no time limit. A time limit on a trailing account is a risk because the trader may feel pressure to finish before the floor rises too far. The calendar should be checked on the plan document, not assumed from the brand.

Consistency and payouts

Futures firms often apply a consistency rule at payout, particularly on the first withdrawal. The rule limits how much of the profit can come from a single day or a single trade, and it can delay a payout even after a clean pass. The two firms apply it at different stages: Apex uses a 50% best-day rule at payout on current accounts (30% on legacy accounts), while GOAT's futures products publish a 50% consistency rule during the challenge and 30% once funded.

When the rule applies

A consistency rule can apply during the evaluation or only at payout. During the evaluation, it forces the trader to dilute a large day by continuing to trade, which increases exposure. At payout, it can be managed by adding winning days before the withdrawal request. The better arrangement for most traders is a payout-only rule, because it leaves the evaluation itself uncomplicated.

The payout cycle

Futures payouts are often processed on a fixed cycle, such as every two weeks or monthly, with a minimum profit threshold. The cycle determines how quickly the trader receives money after the target is met. A shorter cycle is better for cash flow but may require more frequent consistency checks. A longer cycle reduces the administrative load but delays the income.

The profit split

Both firms pay a share of the funded profit, and both may offer higher tiers after scaling or a number of payouts. The headline split is usually the starting rate. The comparison should use the net split after any payout fees, on the typical payout size, not the headline percentage.

Platforms and data

Futures trading is platform-specific, and the platform list is part of the product. The major platforms are Tradovate, NinjaTrader, Rithmic-based front-ends and TradingView integration. The firm's choice determines the execution quality, the data fees and the mobile experience.

Platform coverage

Both firms support the major futures platforms, but the exact list can vary by plan. A trader who uses NinjaTrader for automation needs a firm that supports it; a trader who uses Tradovate for mobile execution needs the mobile app. The platform list should be checked before buying, because a firm that does not support the trader's platform is not a fit regardless of the rules.

Data fees

Futures data is licensed per user, and the fee is usually monthly. The data fee is separate from the platform and the evaluation, and it applies even when the trader is not trading. The all-in cost includes the data fee, the platform fee if any, and the commission. A cheaper evaluation with a higher data fee can be the more expensive route over a year.

Mobile and execution

The mobile app matters for a futures trader who is not always at the desk. The app should allow viewing positions, modifying stops and closing trades. The execution should be tested on a demo during a volatile period, because a platform that requotes or rejects orders in fast markets can turn a planned stop into a worse fill.

Cost structure

Both firms charge a one-time evaluation fee, and both charge separately for data and platform if applicable. The cost comparison should use the fee per attempt and the expected number of attempts, not a monthly rate.

The evaluation fee

A one-time fee means the total cost depends on how many attempts are needed, not how long each one takes. A trader who passes first time pays once; one who needs three attempts pays three times. The expected cost is the fee divided by the pass probability, which is why a cheaper fee with a much lower pass probability can be the more expensive route.

Resets

Both firms offer reset or retry options, sometimes at a discount. The reset policy changes the expected cost more than the monthly rate. A firm with a discounted reset has a lower expected cost than one that charges full price, even if the monthly rate is higher. Check the reset conditions: same plan, same size, time limit and eligibility.

The cost per dollar of room

The cost per dollar of threshold room is a useful comparison. A $200 fee on a $50,000 account with a $2,000 threshold is $0.10 per dollar of room per attempt; a $350 fee on a $100,000 account with a $3,000 threshold is about $0.12. The larger account is not automatically cheaper per dollar of room once the fee is included, which is a common assumption in futures evaluations.

A worked comparison

The example below is illustrative. It shows how the rules interact on the same account and the same trade, not a claim about either firm's current schedule.

The account and the trade

A $50,000 futures account with a $2,500 threshold and a $3,000 target. The trader uses one MES contract, which has a $1.25 tick value and a 10-point stop worth $50. The per-trade risk is $50, which is 2% of the threshold and well inside a daily budget if one exists. The target is $3,000, or 600 ticks, or 60 ten-point trades at one contract. The trader expects to reach it over several weeks.

The intraday trailing case

After $1,000 of profit, the intraday floor rises by $1,000, leaving $1,500 of room above the starting balance. The trader is a third of the way to the target but has 40% less room. The correct response is to reduce the position to one contract or use micros, and to accept a slower pace. A trader who keeps the original size is risking 2% of the threshold per trade with only 60% of the original room.

The end-of-day case

On an end-of-day trailing account, the floor updates at the close. If the $1,000 was banked at the close, the floor rises by $1,000 and the room is $1,500, the same as the intraday case after the close. The difference is the intraday path: during the day, the end-of-day account keeps the original room, so a pullback that goes through the intraday peak does not tighten the floor unless the profit is realised at the close. The end-of-day rule is more forgiving for a trader who holds through pullbacks.

The lesson

The same profit produces different risk plans depending on the threshold method. A trader who understands the method can reduce size at the right moment; one who does not will be surprised by a floor that moved while they were not looking. The comparison between the firms should therefore start with the method, not the percentage.

Sizing on a trailing account

The sizing on a trailing account is not a one-time calculation. It has to be repeated as the floor moves, and the rule of thumb is to keep at least five normal risks between the current equity and the floor.

The daily recalculation

Every morning, write the current floor and the current equity. Subtract the two to get the room. Divide the room by the normal per-trade risk. If the result is five or more, the normal size is safe. If it is between three and five, reduce the size. If it is below three, stop for the day or trade micros only. The recalculation takes a minute and is the difference between a controlled account and a surprise breach.

The mid-challenge squeeze

The most dangerous moment is the middle of the challenge, when the account has accumulated some profit, the floor has moved up and the target is still ahead. The room is smaller than at the start, but the trader feels more confident. That combination is where many attempts fail. The correct response is to reduce size in the middle, not to press. The trader who reduces size may take longer to finish, but the finish is more likely.

The final phase

Near the target, the remaining room is at its smallest and the remaining target is at its shortest. The size should be at its lowest, because the cost of a breach is the whole challenge and the remaining reward is small. A trader who needs $500 to finish should risk a fraction of the normal size, not increase it to finish faster. The finish is a risk decision, not a trading decision.

The psychology of a single-phase challenge

A single-phase futures challenge has a different psychology from a two-phase forex evaluation. There is no phase boundary to reset the rules and no verification stage to confirm the result. The account is one continuous attempt, and the trader has to manage the whole arc without a natural pause.

The long middle

A two-phase challenge has two shorter middles; a single-phase challenge has one long middle. The long middle is where discipline erodes, because the trader has been trading for weeks without a milestone. The fix is to create artificial milestones: a weekly review, a size reduction at 50% of the target, and a written rule that the size does not increase after a good week. The milestones are internal, but they break the middle into manageable segments.

The absence of a reset

In a two-phase challenge, the phase boundary resets the target and sometimes the floor. In a single-phase challenge, there is no reset, so a bad week carries forward. The trader needs a bigger buffer to absorb the bad week, which means a smaller size and a longer calendar. The absence of a reset is a reason to be more conservative, not less.

The first week

The first week of a single-phase challenge sets the tone. A trader who starts with a small gain and an intact daily budget has a buffer; one who starts with a near-breach has pressure for the rest of the attempt. The first week should be traded at half size, with the goal of establishing the platform, the execution and the daily routine rather than reaching the target. The buffer built in the first week is what makes the long middle survivable.

The final stretch

The final stretch is psychologically the hardest, because the target is close and the temptation to press is strong. The trader should write the final-phase size before reaching it, when the decision is calm. A pre-written rule such as “at 80% of the target, halve the size” removes the negotiation at the moment it matters most.

Which trader fits which

The right choice depends on the trader's strategy and temperament, not on a universal ranking.

The intraday trader

An intraday trader who closes positions before the session ends can use either model, but the choice is usually made on the daily rule: a fixed daily stop suits a trader who takes several small losses, while a pure trail suits one who takes two or three planned trades. On Apex, the EOD model supplies the daily stop; the intraday model does not. The distinction is about how the session ends, not about the firm's brand.

The swing trader

A swing trader who holds positions overnight needs a firm that permits it and a threshold that does not rise with unrealised gains. An end-of-day trailing threshold is usually the better fit, because the overnight gap is the main risk and the intraday fluctuations are less relevant.

The scalper

A scalper needs tight execution and a daily stop that absorbs many small losses. A firm with no daily stop and an intraday threshold is a poor fit, because a series of small losses can raise the floor without a break. The platform and the commission also matter more for a scalper than for a swing trader.
