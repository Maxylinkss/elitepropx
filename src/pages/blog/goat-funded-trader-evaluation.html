Six models, and why the choice decides the pass

Most prop firms sell one evaluation shape with a choice of account size. GFT sells a product line, and the models are aimed at different traders rather than different budgets. That is convenient for a trader who knows what they want and dangerous for one who buys whichever tile is highlighted, because two models with the same account size and a similar price can require completely different trading.

The clearest way to see it is to compare the ratio inside each model: the daily limit against the total loss limit. On the 1-Step account that ratio is 3% to 6% — half the total budget is available in a single day, so a single bad session can consume a large share of the entire cushion. On the 2-Step Standard it is 5% to 10%, the same proportion but twice the absolute room. On the 2-Step PRO it is 4% against 8%, which is the same maths again with less room but a smaller second-phase target. Traders read the target and ignore the ratio, and the ratio is what actually ends accounts.

The second decision is whether the floor is static or trailing. Five of the six models use a static maximum loss: the failure line is fixed at the opening balance minus the stated percentage and does not move, whatever the account earns. GOAT Blitz uses a 5% trailing floor, which means profit lifts the failure line behind it. A static account can be traded with a simple rule — never lose more than X. A trailing account needs a second rule about when to bank a winner, because an open gain that retraces has already tightened the account whether or not it is closed.

The third decision is time. GFT markets no time limits on its evaluations, which is a genuine advantage over firms that cap an account at 30 days, but it changes the psychology of a pass: a plan with no deadline drifts, and drift is expensive when the daily limit is the binding constraint. The service still writes a target date into the plan, because a date is what turns a target into a schedule.

Where a client has no strong preference, the recommendation is the 2-Step PRO model: an 8% first phase and 4% second against a 4% daily and an 8% static total gives the widest useful cushion relative to the target, and the smaller second phase means the account is not asked to repeat the first one. The 1-Step account is the alternative when speed matters more than cushion — 10% in one phase with no second stage, at the cost of a 6% total loss limit that a single 3% day eats half of. Both are defensible; buying the cheaper tile without reading which constraint binds is not.

The 4% daily rule and the 5 PM EST reset

The daily drawdown is the constraint a GFT attempt touches first, and it has a definition that catches people who assume a trading day ends when the New York session does. GFT calculates the daily limit at 5 PM EST, based on account balance or equity. That means the boundary is a firm-clock event, not a market event: a position opened at 4:58 PM and held through the roll sits inside the new day's budget from 5:00 PM whether or not a single new trade is placed.

The practical consequence is that the daily budget has to be measured from the equity at the reset, not from the day's opening balance as the trader remembers it. If the account is 1.2% down when the clock turns, the next day's 4% limit is measured from that lower figure, so the room is smaller than the headline percentage suggests. Traders who keep a spreadsheet from the previous morning and stop trading at "4% below yesterday's open" are running a larger risk than they think.

There is a second consequence that matters more for a service pass. Because the limit is on equity, an open position that is 3.5% underwater at 4:55 PM is 3.5% of the next day's budget spent before the day begins, and if it moves further after the reset the daily breach can happen without a new order being placed. The plan therefore treats the reset as a decision point: positions are reduced or closed into 5 PM EST unless there is a written reason to carry them, and the reason has to be about the trade, not about wanting to be right.

On the funded account the same clock governs a different rule. Most GFT models impose a $3,000 daily profit cap once the account is funded, so a single enormous session does not count in full. That is not a pass condition and it is easy to ignore until a withdrawal is reviewed, which is why the plan keeps any single session's result modest relative to the total from the first week of the evaluation onwards. A record built in one heroic day is a record that has to be rebuilt later, on an account with its own limits.

The floating-loss clause most traders miss

Two GFT models restrict open, unrealised loss in addition to the daily and total figures: the 2-Step GOAT model carries a 1% floating loss limit, and GOAT Blitz carries a 2% one across all open trades combined. This is a different kind of rule from a stop-out level, and it changes position management rather than position size.

Under a floating limit, the breach happens while trades are open, not when they are closed. Two positions each 0.6% underwater are a 1.2% floating loss, which on the GOAT model is already past the limit even though neither has hit a stop and neither would have been closed by a discretionary trader waiting for a bounce. The rule effectively caps concurrent exposure, and it makes correlated positions dangerous in a way that a percentage daily limit does not: two long positions in EURUSD and GBPUSD are one bet on dollar weakness, and the floating figure reads them together.

For a pass this produces a concrete planning rule. On a model with a 1% floating cap, the sum of the stop distances on all open positions must stay under that cap at all times, which usually means one position at a time, or several very small ones. The temptation — open three small trades because the total risk "adds up the same" — is exactly what the clause is written to prevent. On Blitz the 2% cap is more permissive but pairs with a trailing 5% floor, so the two constraints interact: an open loss near the cap is also eating floor that has already moved up with profit.

How the plan handles it. On floating-cap models, exposure is treated as a single number that includes open loss: new entries are refused while the floating figure is above 0.6% on GOAT or 1.2% on Blitz, and hedged pairs are not used to "cancel" the number, because a hedge is two positions with two spreads and a firm that sums absolute exposure will read it as one.

This is also where a service decision has to be honest with the client. A floating rule makes a fast, aggressive pass harder to produce, so on those models the expected timeline is longer than on the 2-Step Standard at the same account size. Quoting a single timeline across all six models would be a sales answer, not a factual one.

Sizing a position from a percentage budget

GFT's limits are percentages, which makes the sizing arithmetic continuous rather than quantised: any lot size is possible, so the risk per trade is a decision rather than a rounding. That sounds easier than a futures rulebook and is in practice harder, because there is no natural unit that stops a trader from oversizing "just slightly". The plan fixes the unit before the first trade.

The sequence is short and every step is derived. Take the binding limit — on most GFT models that is the daily drawdown, not the total loss — and convert it into money: 4% of a $100,000 account is $4,000. Divide by the number of losses the day is allowed to contain: at four losses that is $1,000 of risk per trade. Convert that into lot size from the stop distance in pips and the pair's pip value, and round down. The answer is the maximum size for the day, and it is not adjusted upward because a setup looks unusually good.

The number that follows from the same maths is the one worth writing on the plan: how many consecutive losses the total-loss floor allows. On a 2-Step PRO account with an 8% static floor and risk at 1% of balance, that is eight losses with no recovery in between; at 1.5% it is five; at 2.5% it is three, and three is not a cushion, it is a coin toss with paperwork. If the answer is under six, the risk is too high for the account regardless of how the model's target reads.

One GFT-specific wrinkle: because the daily limit resets on equity at 5 PM EST, a plan that carries positions overnight is effectively borrowing from tomorrow's budget. The service prices that in by halving the permitted risk on positions held through the reset, which is a crude rule but an honest one — it makes the cost of holding visible at the moment of the decision instead of at the moment of the breach.

The numbers the plan is written from

The trading itself is unremarkable, which is the point of a plan. Six numbers are fixed before the first session and checked at the end of every week, and every one of them is derived from the model's own document rather than from a market view.

  1. Risk per trade as a fraction of the daily budget, sized so that two consecutive losses leave the day's room intact. On a 3% daily model that is a tighter constraint than the same trader would face on the 2-Step Standard, and the size follows automatically.
  2. A session stop at the point where the day's loss has consumed the planned budget — self-imposed on every model, because a firm's daily limit is a breach threshold, not a risk management tool.
  3. A per-session profit ceiling as a share of the remaining target, which is what keeps the funded-stage $3,000 daily cap from becoming a problem later and keeps the qualifying-day count honest.
  4. A qualifying-day plan: the calendar is built around days that clear 0.5% net, because three of them — four on newer accounts — are required before a phase counts, whatever the profit target says.
  5. A reset discipline around 5 PM EST: reduce or close into the clock unless there is a written reason to carry, and never size a new position as if the previous day's loss had not happened.
  6. A weekly circuit breaker — two losing sessions end the week. The cost is a few days of calendar; the alternative is the sequence that ends evaluations, which is a loss, a size increase to recover it, and a second loss into a limit that has already been touched.

Why the qualifying-day rule changes the target maths. A 6% target on a 3-Step model looks like two good weeks. If the plan averages 0.4% a day, the target arrives while the qualifying days do not, and the account sits at target waiting for sessions that clear 0.5%. The plan therefore sets a floor as well as a ceiling: a session either clears 0.5% net or is not counted as progress.

Reporting runs weekly at minimum, with same-day notification if an attempt is lost. What the client receives at the end is GFT's own confirmation of the pass in the dashboard, not a summary written by the service — a distinction worth insisting on, because a screenshot of someone else's equity curve is not evidence of anything.

Days, resets and the funded payout gate

A GFT pass is measured in weeks, and the drivers are worth naming precisely rather than quoting one flattering number for every model.

The qualifying-day requirement sets the floor. Three profitable days per phase on most models means a phase cannot be completed in a weekend of lucky sessions even if the target is met on the first one. On Blitz the requirement is five days, and paired with a 3% target it makes that model a patience test rather than a speed test despite the name.

The model sets the ceiling. A 10% target on a 3% daily budget is not three days of work; a plan that risks a full 3% per day to reach it is one that breaches on the first bad session. The realistic arithmetic on the 1-Step model is four to six weeks at a risk level that survives a losing streak, and the same trader on the 3-Step model is looking at a similar calendar for a smaller target across three phases. Where a client wants a shorter timeline, the honest answer is a smaller account size on a two-phase model, not more risk on the cheap one.

A losing week is budgeted, not unexpected. Two losing sessions trigger the pause, and the pause costs four or five sessions of the calendar. That is the trade being made deliberately: a longer project in exchange for an account that survives to use its own no-time-limit advantage. GFT's evaluations do not expire, which means the pause is affordable — on a firm with a 30-day window the same pause would be the whole evaluation.

Resets are a cost, not a strategy. GFT sells resets at both the evaluation and funded stages, and a trader who plans to use them has stopped managing risk and started paying to repeat a mistake. The service treats a reset as evidence that the plan is wrong, and the plan is rewritten before the replacement account is traded. The failure recovery process is the same one used across firms.

Choosing a GFT account size and scaling

Account size decides the payout ceiling, not the difficulty. Because GFT's targets and limits are percentages, a $100,000 account and a $10,000 account require the same percentage move; what changes is the money attached to each percentage point, and therefore the money at risk per trade in absolute terms. A trader who is comfortable losing $300 in a session is not made more capable by buying a $200,000 account, only more exposed.

The usable question is what a realistic payout looks like at each size. GFT's funded accounts run an 80% split on several models and advertise up to 100% on demand on others, with a $30 million total paid out across the firm's history according to its own homepage — a marketing figure this page repeats as the firm's claim, not as an independent finding. At an 80% split, a 4% month on a $100,000 account is $3,200 to the trader before any cap or consistency consideration. That is the number worth comparing against the evaluation fee, and against the $220, before choosing a size.

GFT also runs a scaling path toward $2 million of simulated capital and sells accounts up to $400,000. Scaling is worth reading before purchase rather than after, because the conditions are usually a run of profitable months rather than a single good one, and because a scaled account carries the same daily profit cap as the original — a $600,000 account with a $3,000 daily cap is a different product from the one the plan was written for. A worked GFT pass, week by week

The example below is a $100,000 2-Step PRO account — 8% then 4%, 4% daily, 8% static total — and every figure is derived from those numbers rather than chosen for the narrative. The aim is to show what an honest schedule looks like, including the weeks where nothing much happens.

Week one — plan and calibration. Risk is set at $800 per trade, which is 0.8% of balance and one fifth of the daily budget. Four losses in a session would use 3.2% of the 4% limit, so a fifth entry is refused by rule. Target for the week: 2%, ideally spread across three qualifying days. Outcome in a normal week: two qualifying days, one flat day, one small loss, ending around 1.4%. Nothing dramatic, and the 8% total floor has barely been approached.

Week two — the first test of discipline. A 1.9% loss day arrives early. The session stop triggers at $4,000 of loss on the day and closes the platform; the weekly circuit breaker is not reached because the next session is flat. What matters is what does not happen: no size increase on the following day, and no doubling of the previous plan's risk to recover the week. The account sits at roughly 0.5% down on the month, with 7.5% of static room intact.

Week three — the target approaches. Two good sessions put the account 5.6% up. Risk tapers from $800 to $500 for the final third of the phase, because at 5.6% the remaining distance to 8% is small and the cost of a 1% drawdown near the finish is disproportionate. The phase completes at 8.2% on the eleventh qualifying day, with a best day of 1.3% — a distribution that will not create a payout problem later.

Week four — phase two. A 4% target with the same daily limit and the same floor, now measured from the higher balance. Two sessions at 1.1% and 1.2%, a flat week, then a third session at 1.8% completes it. Total calendar: under five weeks, which is a good outcome rather than a typical one. A more honest median for this model is six to eight weeks, and the plan says so before the client pays anything.

Two things about the example are worth stating explicitly because they are the parts a marketing page would cut. The distribution is deliberately flat — the best day is 1.3% — because the funded stage's daily cap and qualifying-day rules punish a lumpy record. And the losing week is not treated as a deviation to be recovered; it is treated as the week the plan was written for. Five ways a GFT attempt ends

These are not hypothetical. They are the patterns that show up across firms with percentage-based limits, and GFT's model set makes each of them slightly more likely for a specific reason.

Buying the cheapest tile instead of the right model. The 1-Step account is frequently the cheapest per dollar of target and the hardest to survive, because a 6% total floor with a 3% daily limit halves the cushion in one session. Traders choose it for the price and then trade it like a 2-Step Standard account.

Missing the qualifying-day rule. The target is reached in five strong sessions and the phase does not complete, because two of them were under 0.5% net. The trader then overtrades to manufacture qualifying days and breaches a limit instead. The plan builds the day count in from the first week for exactly this reason.

Treating the 5 PM EST reset as a market close. Positions are carried through the reset without the budget being recalculated, and the next day's limit is measured from an equity figure the trader never looked at. The breach looks sudden from the platform and obvious from the rulebook.

Ignoring the floating-loss cap on GOAT and Blitz. Two correlated positions, each comfortably inside its own stop, add up to a floating loss above 1% and the account closes while both trades are still open. No stop was hit; the sum was.

Buying several accounts after a promotion. Four discounted $100,000 accounts traded the same way are one position with four invoices. Firms that aggregate exposure can read that as a breach of the duplicate-account clause, and a discounted account is not a cheaper way to buy four attempts — it is a faster way to lose the permission to use any of them.

What this service will not do

The limits are stated in the same place as the offer, because a service page that only lists what it will do is a sales page.

It will not trade an account whose terms prohibit third-party trading, and it will say so in writing before any fee is discussed. It will not trade a funded account as part of the $220. It will not guarantee a pass, a payout, or a timeline shorter than the model's own qualifying-day rules allow. It will not use a strategy that requires breaching a rule to work — no news blackout violation, no duplicate-account arrangement, no hedge pair designed to hide exposure from a floating-loss cap. It will not take a share of profit, which means it also will not take a share of risk beyond the flat fee.

And it will not pretend that a $220 fee plus a firm's evaluation fee is a small amount of money. On a $100,000 GFT account the firm's own fee is several hundred dollars even after a 50% promotion, and a lost evaluation costs that fee again. The attempt is retried at no extra service fee while the evaluation is open, and the plan is rewritten before the replacement account is traded — a lost account is treated as evidence that the plan was wrong, not as bad luck.
