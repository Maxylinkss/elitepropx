How the AquaFunded rulebook is built

The AquaFunded evaluation is best understood as a set of constraints that interact, not as a headline profit target with some footnotes. The target decides how much you have to make. The loss floor decides how much room you have to make it. The daily stop, if there is one, decides how quickly a bad session ends. The minimum-day rule decides how long the attempt takes even when the target is reached early. Change any one of those and the same trading strategy produces a different outcome.

For AquaFunded, the published structure is a one-step and two-phase evaluations. The loss floor is static and trailing variants by plan, the daily stop is yes, and the consistency condition is plan-specific. Billing is one-off per attempt, payouts follow profit split on a fixed schedule, and the supported platforms are MT4, MT5, cTrader. Those are the rows that matter. Marketing pages tend to foreground the profit target and the profit split because both are easy to compare; neither tells you whether a given trade is survivable.

What AquaFunded does well

The strengths are structural rather than promotional. Choice between one-step and two-phase structures. Multiple platform options. Plan-level transparency. Those are the features that show up in the trading experience, not just on the comparison table.

  • Choice between one-step and two-phase structures.
  • Multiple platform options.
  • Plan-level transparency.

The strongest feature is usually the one that matches your own constraint. If your strategy holds positions through retracements, a static floor is worth more than a lower fee. If you need a fast pass, a one-step structure is worth more than a slightly larger profit split. If you trade a session that overlaps the firm's instrument hours, the platform list matters less than the execution quality during that session.

It is worth separating a feature from a marketing claim. "No minimum days" is a feature because it changes the calendar. "Best payout in the industry" is a claim unless the payout clause defines the interval, the minimum and the consistency condition. The review weights features by whether they change a decision you will actually make during the attempt.

Where AquaFunded is weaker

The cautions are equally structural. Drawdown style changes between plans. One-step products carry a tighter margin for error. Payout terms vary by plan. None of them is a reason to avoid the firm automatically, but each has to be priced into the plan before the fee is paid.

  • Drawdown style changes between plans.
  • One-step products carry a tighter margin for error.
  • Payout terms vary by plan.

The caution that matters most is the one that interacts with your strategy. A trailing threshold is harmless for a trader who takes one trade a day and closes it before the session ends; it is decisive for a trader who holds through a retracement. A monthly subscription is harmless for a trader who expects to pass in ten days; it is expensive for one who expects to take two months.

Operational cautions are harder to verify and more important over time. How quickly does support answer a mechanics question? Does the dashboard show the distance to the loss floor? Is the payout statement itemised? These are the things that decide whether a funded account is pleasant to run, and they are not visible on a sales page.

Bottom line

AquaFunded is worth considering if the rulebook matches your strategy and the billing rhythm matches your expected timeline. It is not worth considering as a generic "best firm" purchase, because the firm's mechanic is the product. A trader who buys a trailing loss floor and then trades it like a static account has bought the wrong product, whatever the fee.

The score that matters is not a star rating. It is the number of attempts you expect, multiplied by the cost per attempt, compared with the cost of a flat-fee service and the time you have. AquaFunded is a good deal when you pass early and a poor one when the rulebook fights your method. Judge it on that arithmetic rather than on the marketing.

The cost of a one-step plan

A one-step plan removes a phase but usually compresses the loss allowance. On a $100,000 account, a 6% static floor leaves $6,000 of room; a 10% two-phase floor leaves $10,000. At a $500 risk unit, the difference is eight losses: twelve on the one-step plan against twenty on the two-phase plan. If the one-step plan also trails, a gain reduces the room further.

The fee is one-off per attempt, so the faster plan is not automatically cheaper across a year. A tighter floor can cause more retries, and each retry is a full fee. A trader who expects two attempts on the one-step plan and one on the two-phase plan should compare two fees against one, not the sticker prices.

Payouts follow a fixed schedule after the first withdrawal. The split is applied to the profit, and the processor fee is proportionally larger on a small first payout.

Who should consider AquaFunded

AquaFunded fits a forex trader who wants a choice between one-step and two-phase structures and is willing to read the plan-level rules. It is less suited to a trader who wants one documented rulebook across the brand, because the floor style changes between plans. A trader who needs a static floor should confirm the plan before buying; a trader who wants the fastest path may accept the tighter one-step allowance.

Compare the plan you would actually buy with the equivalent plan at another firm, not the cheapest plan on the page. The floor definition and the payout condition decide the value.
