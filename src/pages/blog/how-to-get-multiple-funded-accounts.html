How to Get Multiple Funded Accounts

A second funded account doubles the capital and doubles every subscription, every risk budget and every breach opportunity. It is not automatically a better position. This guide covers the four legitimate routes to multiple accounts, the per-person and per-household caps that limit them, why mirrored positions are treated differently from independent ones, and the break-even arithmetic that decides whether the second account is worth its fee.

Key takeaways

  • There are four routes: pass more evaluations, scale an existing account, join a multi-account programme, or buy a firm's own add-on account.
  • Caps are usually per person or per household and are often lower than the number of accounts the dashboard allows you to buy.
  • Independent positions across accounts are usually permitted; mirrored or hedged positions across accounts are usually not.
  • A second account multiplies fixed costs. Below a certain payout size, the fee and subscription exceed the extra income.
  • Correlation is the real risk: three accounts holding the same view are one position at three times the size.

The appeal of multiple accounts is obvious: more capital, more payouts, more diversification of firm risk. The costs are less obvious, and they are mostly fixed. A trader with one account pays one evaluation fee, one subscription and one data feed; a trader with four pays each four times, and the fourth account still needs the same attention as the first.

There is a second, less discussed cost: the penalty for correlation. Multiple accounts do not diversify a strategy. If all four hold the same view in the same instrument, the trader has one position at four times the size, with four times the drawdown in dollar terms and four chances to breach a rule that was designed for a single account.

The four routes to a second account

Only one of these is "pass another challenge". The others are cheaper or faster in specific circumstances, and they are routinely overlooked.

RouteHow it worksCostBest for
Pass another evaluationBuy a fresh evaluation and trade it to targetFull fee, full effortTraders with a repeatable process and spare risk budget
Scale the existing accountMeet the plan's profit and consistency conditions for a size increaseNo new feeTraders already profitable on one account
Join a multi-account programmeFirm-specific structure that issues several accounts under one planUsually discounted versus separate purchasesTraders committed to one firm
Buy an add-on accountAn additional funded account offered at a discount to existing holdersOften a fraction of a full evaluationTraders whose firm explicitly offers it

Scaling is almost always the cheapest route and the slowest. It rewards exactly the behaviour an evaluation is meant to test — consistent profit within the rulebook — and it usually requires a minimum number of payout cycles or a stated profit level. The catch is that it is granted at the firm's discretion, so it cannot be planned as cash flow.

The multi-account programme is the middle path. It exists because firms would rather sell several accounts to a known trader than have that trader spread across competitors. Terms vary widely, and the important question is whether the accounts are independent for the purpose of the position cap or treated as one book.

Buying another evaluation is the most expensive route and the most controllable. It requires no permission and no track record, which is precisely why it is the default choice for traders who are still learning a rulebook.

Per-person and per-household caps

Almost every firm limits how much capital one person or one household can hold in funded accounts. The cap is expressed in one of four ways, and its form matters more than its headline number.

Cap formTypical expressionWhat it means in practice
Account count"Up to four funded accounts"Simple but blind to size; four $25k accounts equal one $100k
Total nominal value"Up to $400,000 in aggregate"Size-aware; the cap binds when accounts are large
Per-firm value"$200,000 per firm"Allows spreading across firms, which diversifies counterparty risk
Household"Across all accounts in the household"Cannot be multiplied by family members; the strictest form

The household form is the one that catches traders who assume a spouse can hold a separate allocation. Where the cap is written per household, two names do not produce twice the capacity. The clause usually names the household explicitly, and buying around it is a breach rather than a clever reading.

A cap can also interact with the dashboard. Platforms commonly allow a trader to purchase more accounts than the cap permits, because the purchase flow is a sales process and the cap is a compliance rule. Nothing stops the fourth purchase; something stops the fourth payout. Check the cap in the terms before buying, not after the accounts are funded.

Mirrored positions and cross-account hedging

The most common breach among multi-account traders is not fraud. It is a trader who sees a good setup, takes it on all their accounts, and does not think of that as a single concentrated position.

From the firm's side it is exactly that. Four accounts holding the same position means the firm's exposure to that view is four times what any single evaluation priced. The clause restricting mirrored positions exists to stop that scaling, and it usually applies whether the accounts are at one firm or across several affiliates.

BehaviourUsual statusWhy
Different setups on different accountsPermitted within the capIndependent decisions; risk is genuinely diversified
Same setup, scaled across accountsRestrictedOne position at multiple size
Long on one account, short on anotherRestrictedCross-account hedging defeats the per-account floor
Copying one account to anotherRestrictedCopy trading and, in substance, shared management
Same instrument, different direction, different dayUsually permittedNot simultaneous, not a hedge

The third row deserves a note because it feels prudent. A trader who is long on account A and short on account B has no net market exposure and believes they are safer. In the rules, each account is evaluated separately, so the hedge does nothing for either account's drawdown — it merely guarantees that one of them loses. The firm prohibits it because the arrangement converts two independent evaluations into a single hedged book, which is not the product that was sold.

The detection is straightforward: timestamp and instrument comparison across accounts belonging to the same verified person. A trader who holds the same instrument in opposite directions simultaneously across two accounts should assume it will be found.

Scaling versus buying another account

Scaling plans and additional accounts achieve the same end — more capital — through different mechanisms, and the trade-offs are not symmetric.

DimensionScaling an accountBuying another account
Cash costUsually noneFull or discounted evaluation fee
TimeMonths of consistent resultsWeeks per evaluation
RiskLow; the account is already provenFull evaluation risk on new capital
RulebookUnchanged; same plan, larger sizeNew evaluation, possibly new rules
Drawdown behaviourFloor usually scales with size, in dollarsSeparate floor per account
CorrelationNone — one bookMust be managed across accounts

The fifth row is where scaling wins clearly. When an account doubles in size, the loss allowance usually doubles in dollars, so the trader can risk the same percentage as before. Two separate accounts each have their own floor, and a losing streak across both consumes two floors at once — which sounds like diversification and is actually double the fixed cost of being wrong.

The fourth row cuts the other way. Scaling keeps the rulebook you already know, while a new evaluation may come with different minimum days, consistency rules or payout cycles. For a trader whose edge is fitted to a specific rulebook, that is a real cost of the second account.

Does a second account pay?

A second account adds income and adds cost. The break-even is a payout size, and it is usually larger than traders assume.

LineOne accountTwo accountsFour accounts
Evaluation fees$400$800$1,600
Monthly subscriptions$0–$120$0–$240$0–$480
Data and software$40$40$40
Year-one fixed cost~$440~$880~$1,760

Fixed cost as share of income11.0%11.0%11.0%

In this model the ratio is constant, which is the seductive part: each additional account appears to add income at the same cost ratio. The model is wrong in one specific way. The fixed cost is certain and the income is not. A second account that fails its evaluation produces a loss with no offsetting income, and the trader is now down the fee on an account that will never pay.

Second-account outcomeProbability (illustrative)Net effect on the year
Passes and pays twice35%+$4,000 − $440 ≈ +$3,560
Passes but pays once25%+$2,000 − $440 ≈ +$1,560
Passes, breaches before payout20%−$440
Fails the evaluation20%−$440
Expected value100%≈ +$1,404

The expected value is positive, which is the honest answer for a trader with a real edge. But the distribution is asymmetric in a way that matters for a trader with a small bankroll: 40% of outcomes lose the fee outright, and a trader who buys four accounts at once has a 40% chance of losing four fees before seeing any income. Sequencing the accounts — buy the second after the first pays — reduces that risk at the cost of some upside.

That is the practical recommendation for most traders. Add accounts one at a time, funded from realised payouts rather than from savings, so that the number of accounts grows only as the evidence accumulates.

Correlation across accounts

Two accounts holding two ideas are a portfolio. Two accounts holding one idea are a levered position. The difference is not in the account count but in the correlation of the positions, and correlation is the variable that multi-account traders underestimate most.

  • Same instrument, same direction. One position at double size. The worst case.
  • Correlated instruments, same direction. Long EURUSD and long GBPUSD is close to one dollar-short position, not two ideas.
  • Different instruments, same theme. Long gold and short the dollar is a single macro view expressed twice.
  • Different sessions, same setup. Not correlated in market terms, but the same edge and the same failure mode.
  • Genuinely independent. Different instruments, different drivers, different holding periods. Rare, and the only configuration that diversifies.

The first three collapse multiple accounts into one risk. The fourth does not collapse the market risk but does collapse the strategy risk: if the setup fails, it fails on every account at once. Only the fifth earns the diversification that the account count implies.

A practical test is to write the net exposure rather than the trade list. Long EURUSD and long GBPUSD at 1% each is a 2% long-dollar-area bet, and a rule that limits concentration should be applied to the aggregate. Traders who track per-account risk and never aggregate are running more risk than they believe.

Running several accounts without errors

The administrative burden of multiple accounts is where the avoidable breaches happen. A limit that is clear on one account becomes a memory exercise on four.

  1. Keep one ledger for all accounts. Date, account, instrument, direction, risk in dollars, remaining room to the floor. One row per trade, no exceptions.
  2. Track aggregate exposure separately. A second column that sums open risk across accounts by instrument and theme.
  3. Set the daily loss budget per account, not per trader. Each account has its own daily limit, and a shared mental budget will breach the smaller ones first.
  4. Note each account's rulebook separately. Minimum days, consistency thresholds and payout cycles rarely match across firms.
  5. Cancel pending orders on every account before a news window. This is the single most common multi-account error.

The ledger is not bureaucracy. It is the only way to see that four accounts holding correlated positions are one position, and it is the record that answers a payout review question about which account placed what. Traders who run several accounts without one almost always discover the correlation after a bad day rather than before it.

The time cost nobody prices

Each account consumes attention: rulebook reading, session planning, trade review, payout administration and support correspondence. On one account that is a manageable weekly routine. On four it is a part-time job, and the marginal account receives the marginal attention.

The quality effect is real and one-directional. A trader who is spread across four accounts takes fewer considered setups per account, reviews less thoroughly and is more likely to make an administrative error. The fourth account does not produce a quarter of the profit; it produces less, because the attention it receives is worse than the attention the first account received.

The practical ceiling for most traders is two or three accounts. Beyond that, the constraint is not capital or rules but attention, and the correct move is to scale the accounts you can handle rather than to add accounts you cannot. A trader with two well-run $100,000 accounts is in a stronger position than one with five accounts that are each slightly neglected.

Choosing firms for a multi-account book

If the point of several accounts is diversification, then the firms should differ in the dimensions that can fail. Two accounts at the same firm with the same rulebook are one account with two fees.

DimensionWhy to vary itWhat to avoid
Drawdown typeA static floor and a trailing floor fail in different marketsAll accounts on intraday trailing floors
MarketForex and futures have different sessions and gap behaviourTwo accounts on correlated currency pairs
Payout cycleStaggered cycles smooth cash flowAll payouts on the same monthly date
Firm and jurisdictionCounterparty risk is a real riskConcentration in one operator's affiliated brands
Rulebook strictnessA permissive plan and a strict one test different skillsBuying the strictest plan twice because it is cheapest

The fourth row needs care. Several brands can be operated by the same group with different marketing and similar terms. Spreading across them feels like diversification and is not, because a group-level problem affects all of them at once. Check who operates the brand rather than what the logo looks like.

The first row is the most useful in practice. A trader holding one account with a static floor and one with a trailing floor is diversified against the failure mode that most often ends accounts: a normal drawdown after a good run. The two floors respond differently to the same equity curve, and one of them is likely to survive a sequence that closes the other.

A two-account operating routine

Two accounts are manageable with a routine that takes twenty minutes a day. Four are not, unless the routine is stricter than most traders maintain.

  1. Before the session, one sheet. Open risk per account, remaining room to each floor, and net exposure by instrument across both. The net figure is the one that is usually missing.
  2. One setup list, two allocations. Decide the day's ideas once, then allocate them so that no single idea appears on both accounts in the same direction.
  3. A per-account daily loss cap. Each account has its own limit; the trader does not have a shared budget. A shared mental budget breaches the smaller account first.
  4. Rules check per firm. Minimum days, consistency thresholds and news windows differ. Keep one line per firm rather than one line for the book.
  5. End-of-day reconciliation. Confirm every order is recorded, every pending order is intentional, and no position is open that was meant to be closed.

The third item is the one that produces the most avoidable breaches. A trader who thinks in a single daily risk budget will allocate it across accounts in whatever order the setups appear, which means one account absorbs the day's losses and the other absorbs nothing. Each account's limit is separate, and the plan has to be written per account.

The fifth item is the cheapest insurance. Most multi-account errors are administrative — a pending order left live into a news window, a partial close that did not register — and they are found in a two-minute reconciliation rather than in a payout review.

Closing accounts and recycling capital

Accounts do not last forever. A breach closes one, a rule change makes another unusable, and a firm may withdraw a product. A multi-account book needs a policy for what happens to the capital when an account ends.

The first decision is whether to replace the account or reduce the number. Replacing immediately preserves the capital level but assumes the failure was random rather than informative. Reducing the number is slower but acknowledges that the account may have failed because the rulebook did not fit the method.

The second decision is where the replacement comes from. Doing it with realised payouts keeps the book funded from its own results; doing it with new savings increases the total capital at risk at the moment the trader has the least evidence. The first is slower and is the reason a book built over a year tends to survive longer than one bought in a week.

The third decision is what to do with the rulebook knowledge. A closed account on a trailing floor is evidence about trailing floors, and it is worth writing down before the next purchase. Most traders repeat the same rulebook mistake across two or three firms because the lesson was never recorded anywhere except in the loss.

How to tell you have too many

The ceiling is not a number of accounts. It is the point at which the marginal account receives less attention than it needs, and the symptoms are consistent.

  • You check one account's remaining drawdown from memory rather than from the dashboard.
  • You have taken the same directional view on two accounts without deciding to.
  • You cannot state each firm's minimum-day count without looking it up.
  • Payout administration has slipped past a cycle on at least one account.
  • A news window has caught a pending order you had forgotten.

Two of these symptoms is the signal to stop adding accounts. The fix is not to work harder; it is to close the weakest account and concentrate the risk budget on the ones that are being run properly. A trader with two well-managed accounts is in a stronger position than one with five that are each slightly neglected, and the difference shows up as a breach rather than as a lower return.

A model year for a trader who adds a second account only after the first pays. The figures are illustrative, but the sequencing is the part worth copying.

Three features of this sequence are worth noting. The second evaluation is funded from a realised payout, not from savings, so the trader never has more than one fee at risk before the book has produced income. The replacement after the breach comes from the same month's income rather than a new deposit, which keeps the capital at risk flat. And the year ends with two accounts and a net $17,200, with only $1,200 of total fees.

The counter-example is the trader who buys four evaluations in month one. If two fail — normal at a 35% pass rate — the year starts at minus $800 with two evaluations still running, and the pressure to force a result on the remaining two is exactly the condition that produces post-target breaches. The sequencing is not caution; it is the only way the fee cost stays proportional to the evidence.

A final note on the breach. Losing one account in a year is not a failure of the strategy; it is the base rate of running funded capital under a hard floor. A book that can absorb one closure and continue is a book that is sized correctly. A book where one closure ends the year is over-levered on fees, however large the accounts are.

Common questions

How many funded accounts can one trader have?

It depends on the firm's cap, which is usually expressed as an account count, a total nominal value, or a per-household limit. The cap is often lower than the number of accounts the dashboard allows you to buy, so read the terms rather than the sales flow.

Can my spouse hold a separate funded account?

Only if the cap is per person and not per household. Many firms write the cap per household precisely to prevent multiplying it across family members. Check the wording before buying.

Is it allowed to take the same trade on two accounts?

Usually not. Mirrored positions across accounts are treated as one concentrated position, and most terms restrict them explicitly. Independent setups on independent accounts are the permitted pattern.

Is scaling cheaper than buying a second account?

Almost always, since it costs no new evaluation fee. It requires a proven record over time and is granted at the firm's discretion, so it cannot be planned as cash flow. Buying another account is faster and more controllable.

Can I hedge one account against another?

Normally no. Cross-account hedging is restricted because each account is evaluated on its own floor, and a hedge that protects the aggregate leaves one account losing regardless. It is also easy to detect by comparing timestamps and instruments.

Do multiple accounts diversify my risk?

Only if the positions are genuinely independent. Same idea on several accounts is one position at multiple size. Diversification across firms is more useful than diversification across accounts at one firm, because it spreads counterparty risk as well as market risk.

What is the cost of running four accounts?

Four evaluation fees, up to four subscriptions, plus the attention each account needs. The fixed cost is roughly proportional to the account count, while the income is not, so a fair share of accounts will produce no income at all.

Should I buy several accounts at once or one at a time?

One at a time, funded from realised payouts. Buying several at once concentrates the loss if the evaluations fail, and a meaningful share of attempts do fail. Sequencing trades some upside for a much smaller chance of losing several fees before any income arrives.
