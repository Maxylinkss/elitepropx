The short verdict on FTMO

FTMO is a forex and CFD evaluator built on a two-phase evaluation followed by a verification phase, with a static loss floor, a daily stop (equity-based) and one-off per attempt billing. The product is a reasonable fit for traders who want the most conventional percentage-based evaluation available. It is a poor fit for a trader whose strategy needs the opposite drawdown mechanic, or whose budget cannot absorb the billing rhythm across several attempts.

How the FTMO rulebook is built

The FTMO evaluation is best understood as a set of constraints that interact, not as a headline profit target with some footnotes. The target decides how much you have to make. The loss floor decides how much room you have to make it. The daily stop, if there is one, decides how quickly a bad session ends. The minimum-day rule decides how long the attempt takes even when the target is reached early. Change any one of those and the same trading strategy produces a different outcome.

For FTMO, the published structure is a two-phase evaluation followed by a verification phase. The loss floor is static, measured from the starting balance, the daily stop is yes, equity-based, and the consistency condition is none on the evaluation. Billing is one-off per attempt, payouts follow first payout after a fixed seasoning period, then on demand, and the supported platforms are MT4, MT5, cTrader, DXtrade. Those are the rows that matter. Marketing pages tend to foreground the profit target and the profit split because both are easy to compare; neither tells you whether a given trade is survivable.

What FTMO does well

The strengths are structural rather than promotional. Static maximum loss means progress never moves the failure point closer. Deep platform coverage and a familiar percentage rulebook. Where FTMO is weaker

The cautions are equally structural. Daily loss is measured on equity, so open positions count against it. The verification phase resets the clock even after a clean first phase. Payout seasoning means the first withdrawal is not immediate. None of them is a reason to avoid the firm automatically, but each has to be priced into the plan before the fee is paid.

Bottom line

FTMO is worth considering if the rulebook matches your strategy and the billing rhythm matches your expected timeline. It is not worth considering as a generic "best firm" purchase, because the firm's mechanic is the product. A trader who buys a static loss floor and then trades it like a static account has bought the wrong product, whatever the fee.

The score that matters is not a star rating. It is the number of attempts you expect, multiplied by the cost per attempt, compared with the cost of a flat-fee service and the time you have. FTMO is a good deal when you pass early and a poor one when the rulebook fights your method. Judge it on that arithmetic rather than on the marketing.

The rulebook that became the reference

FTMO is the firm most traders learn percentage evaluations on: a static maximum loss measured from the starting balance, an equity-based daily stop and a two-phase path followed by a verification phase. The static floor is the feature that makes the product easy to size against. The daily stop is the feature that ends a bad session before it becomes a bad month. The verification phase is the feature that adds calendar time after a clean pass.

The evaluation itself has no consistency rule, which is unusual among firms that have added one. The payout stage still has conditions, including the seasoning period and the withdrawal rules. A trader who reads only the evaluation page has read the easier half of the contract.

What the platform list is worth

MT4, MT5, cTrader and DXtrade cover most retail workflows, including expert advisors on MT4 and MT5 and a modern order interface on cTrader. The breadth is a real advantage: a trader who already has a tested workflow does not have to rebuild it for the evaluation. The platform is not a reason to choose FTMO on its own, but it removes a common source of friction.

The costs are one-off per attempt, so a slow pass does not add a monthly subscription. A trader who expects to take two months should still add the platform or VPS cost, but the evaluation fee itself does not grow with the calendar. That makes FTMO easier to budget than a subscription product.

The verdict

FTMO fits a trader who wants the most conventional percentage rulebook, a static floor and a wide platform choice. It is less suited to a trader who needs a one-step path, a trailing futures product or an on-demand first payout. The verification phase and the payout seasoning are the two calendar costs to accept before buying.

If the rulebook fits, the operational experience is mature: the dashboard, the documentation and the support process are well established. The main risk is not the firm's operation but the trader's sizing. A static floor invites a fixed risk unit, and a fixed risk unit that is too large is the most common way to fail a conventional evaluation.
