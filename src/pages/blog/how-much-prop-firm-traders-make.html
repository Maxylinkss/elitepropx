How much do prop firm traders make: per payout, per month, per year

A single payout is not an income. This guide converts payouts into monthly and annual figures, subtracts the cost stack and shows why the account-survival probability matters.

Per payout, per month, per year

A single payout figure is not an income. The same account can produce a $2,000 payout once and nothing for three months, or $700 a month for a year. To compare the two, divide the annual net by twelve. A trader who makes $2,000 in one cycle and then breaches has an annual income of $2,000, not $24,000. The marketing headline is usually the single payout; the useful number is the annual net.

Build the annual figure from three inputs: the average net payout, the number of payouts per year and the probability of keeping the account. A trader with a $1,500 average payout, six payouts a year and a 50% chance of losing the account has an expected annual income of $4,500, not $9,000. The probability is the part that most income claims omit.

The cost stack behind the number

Subtract the evaluation fees, resets, platform and data costs, and payout processor fees from the gross payouts. A trader who pays $300 for an evaluation, $60 a month for data and $30 per payout has a different net from one who pays none of those. The first payout usually carries the setup costs, so the first-year net is lower than the steady-state net.

The account size changes the absolute numbers but not the percentages. A 2% monthly return on a $50,000 account is $1,000 gross; on a $200,000 account it is $4,000. The risk per trade also scales, so a trader who cannot produce the return on the smaller account will not produce it on the larger one. The larger account makes the same mistakes more expensive.

The three-month reality check

Model the first three months, not the first payout. Month one usually carries the evaluation fee and the platform setup; month two is the first clean payout if the account survives; month three is the first month that looks like a rhythm. A trader who expects month one to be profitable is ignoring the setup costs that every new account carries.

If the model shows a loss in month one, a small gain in month two and a stable gain in month three, the trajectory is reasonable. If it shows a large gain in month one followed by nothing, the first month was probably an outlier. The three-month view is the smallest sample that can distinguish a process from a lucky month.

The distribution is not a salary

Funded trading income is variable. A good month can be followed by a flat month, and a breach can reset the annual figure to zero. A trader who depends on the income for rent is taking a risk that a trader with another income source is not. Treat the first year as a build year: keep the costs low, keep the account alive and measure the payout rhythm before relying on it.

The realistic goal is not a salary in month one. It is a repeatable process that produces a payout every cycle and a buffer that survives a bad month. The income follows the process, not the other way around.

Common questions

How much do prop firm traders make per year?

It varies widely. A trader with a $1,500 average payout and six payouts a year grosses $9,000; after costs and the chance of losing the account, the expected net can be much lower. There is no reliable salary figure.

What costs reduce the income?

Evaluation and reset fees, platform and data subscriptions, and payout processor fees. The first payout usually carries the setup costs, so the first year is the least representative.

Is a larger account always more income?

No. The return percentage has to be produced first. A larger account makes the same mistakes more expensive and does not create an edge.
