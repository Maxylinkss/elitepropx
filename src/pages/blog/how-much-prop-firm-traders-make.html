How Much Prop Firm Traders Make

The honest answer is a range so wide that it is almost useless without a model. A trader with a $25,000 account and a 3% monthly return earns a very different income from one with four $200,000 accounts and a 2% return, even though the second may be the more consistent trader. This guide builds the income equation from its four inputs, models realistic monthly and annual figures, and subtracts the costs that the headline numbers usually ignore.

Key takeaways

  • Income is account size × monthly return × profit split × payout frequency, minus costs. Each input matters, and account size is usually the largest lever.
  • A 2% monthly return on a $200,000 account pays more than a 5% return on a $50,000 account, with less pressure to perform.
  • The first payout is a milestone, not a salary. Most traders earn little in the first quarter and more later if the account survives.
  • Net income is smaller than the headline: challenge fees, resets, data costs, taxes and failed accounts all reduce the figure.

The income equation

Prop trading income is not a salary, a dividend or a fixed return. It is a share of the profit a funded account generates, paid according to the firm's schedule. That makes it a product of four inputs, each of which can be estimated before the first trade.

Income = account size × monthly return × profit split × payouts per year − costs.

The equation is simple, but each input has a distribution rather than a single value. The account size is the most stable input: once funded, it is a number on a dashboard until a scale-up or a breach. The monthly return is the least stable: it varies with the market, the strategy and the trader's discipline. The profit split is contractual and usually fixed. The payout frequency is contractual but may depend on the account meeting minimum conditions. The costs are the most often ignored.

The first practical consequence is that a trader can improve income by changing any of the four inputs. Increasing the account size requires a scale-up or a second account. Increasing the return requires better trading, which is the hardest and least reliable lever. Improving the split requires choosing a better firm. Increasing the payout frequency requires meeting the firm's conditions consistently. The costs can be reduced by avoiding repeated attempts and unnecessary subscriptions.

The second consequence is that income is not the same as profit. The account may generate a 5% profit in a month, but the trader receives the split after the firm's share and any fees. A 5% month on a $100,000 account with an 80% split is $4,000 before costs, not $5,000. On a 90% split, it is $4,500. The difference between splits is real money, and it compounds over a year.

The table makes three points. First, a 1% monthly return on a $100,000 account pays $800 a month, which is not a living income in most countries. The returns in the table are gross and monthly. A trader who makes 3% a month for a year and withdraws the profit does not compound the account; the income is roughly 36% of the account size across the year, before costs. A trader who leaves the profit in the account may compound if the firm allows it, but most firms pay out the profit and keep the account at the funded level. The table is therefore a recurring income model, not a compounding model, unless a scale-up increases the base.

There is a fourth point that the table does not show: the probability of achieving each return consistently. A 5% monthly return is possible for a skilled trader in a good market, but sustaining it for twelve months without a losing month is rare. A 1–2% monthly return is more achievable for a disciplined trader, and on a large enough account it produces a better risk-adjusted income. The table should be read as a set of scenarios, not a menu.

The profit split and what it really means

The profit split is the percentage of the account's profit that the trader keeps. Most firms offer 80–90%, with some offering 70% at lower tiers and 90–95% at higher tiers or after scaling. The split is usually applied to the profit at the payout, not to the account balance, and it is separate from any fees the firm charges.

Why a 5% difference matters

On a $100,000 account making 3% a month, the gross profit is $3,000. At an 80% split, the trader receives $2,400. At a 90% split, the trader receives $2,700. The difference is $300 a month, or $3,600 a year, for the same trading. Over several years, the difference can exceed the account size. The split is one of the few inputs that can be improved without changing the trading, which makes it worth comparing carefully.

High-water marks and loss carry-forward

Some firms apply a high-water mark: the trader is paid on the profit above the previous peak, so a losing month must be recovered before the next payout. Others carry losses forward, reducing the profit on which the split is calculated. A high-water mark protects the firm and can delay payouts after a drawdown. The trader should understand which applies, because it changes the timing of income even when the split is the same.

Split tiers and scaling

Some firms increase the split as the account scales or after a number of payouts. A 70% starting split that rises to 90% after six months is a different product from a flat 80%. The headline split is often the starting rate, not the long-run rate. Check the schedule before comparing firms on the headline number.

Fees deducted from the split

Payout processing fees, currency conversion and data fees may be deducted from the trader's share. A 90% split with a $50 processing fee on a $500 payout is effectively 80% after the fee. The net split is the number that matters, and it should be calculated on the typical payout size, not the headline.

Payout frequency and the first payout

The payout frequency determines how often the income arrives, and it is often more important than a small difference in the split. A firm that pays monthly with a 80% split may produce more usable income than one that pays quarterly with a 90% split, because the money arrives sooner and the account is tested more often.

The first payout

Most firms require a minimum number of trading days and a profit threshold before the first withdrawal. A common sequence is five to ten trading days, a profit target of 1–5%, a consistency check and completed KYC. The first payout can take two to six weeks from funding, depending on the firm's processing time. A trader who needs income quickly should check the first-payout conditions before choosing a firm, not after.

Subsequent payouts

After the first payout, the cycle may be monthly, biweekly or on demand, subject to minimum profit and consistency conditions. Some firms allow a payout every two weeks; others require a full calendar month. The frequency affects the compounding of the trader's income and the account's risk: a trader who withdraws monthly reduces the account's buffer less often than one who withdraws biweekly, but also has less frequent access to cash.

The consistency condition

A consistency rule can delay a payout even when the profit threshold is met. If one day accounts for 45% of profit and the cap is 40%, the trader must earn more on other days before withdrawing. The rule is usually disclosed in the payout terms, but it is easy to miss on the challenge page. The practical response is to plan the distribution from the first trade, not to discover the rule at the payout request.

The cost side of the equation

The income equation is gross. The net figure subtracts the costs of getting funded and staying funded, and those costs can be substantial in the first year.

Challenge fees and resets

The evaluation fee is paid before any income. If the first attempt fails, the second fee is another cost. A trader who takes three attempts at $200 each has spent $600 before the first payout, which may be most of the first month's income. The expected cost is the fee divided by the pass rate, not the fee alone.

Data and platform fees

Futures evaluations often carry monthly data and platform fees, which continue between attempts and after funding. A $50 monthly fee over a year is $600, which is a meaningful fraction of a small account's income. Forex evaluations usually have lower or no data fees, but they may have platform or subscription costs at some firms.

Payout fees

Processing fees, currency conversion and withdrawal minimums reduce the net payout. A flat $30 fee on a $300 payout is 10%; on a $3,000 payout it is 1%. The fee structure matters most for small accounts and frequent withdrawals.

Taxes

Prop trading income is taxable in most jurisdictions, and the treatment varies: it may be treated as self-employment income, capital gains, or another category, depending on the trader's status and location. The tax rate can take 20–40% of the net income, which is a larger deduction than any firm fee. A trader should estimate the after-tax income, not just the payout, and should get local advice if the amount is material.

Failed accounts

A funded account can fail, and a failed account stops the income stream. The cost is not just the lost future payouts; it is the fee for a new evaluation and the time to pass it. A trader who fails two accounts a year has a cost that is easy to underestimate when looking at the gross monthly table.

A worked net-income example

Consider a trader with a $100,000 funded account, a 3% monthly return, an 80% split and monthly payouts. The gross income is $2,400 a month, or $28,800 a year before costs. Now subtract the costs.

The evaluation fee was $250, and the trader passed on the second attempt, so the total fee cost was $500. The firm charges a $30 payout fee per withdrawal, or $360 a year. There is no data fee on this forex account. The trader's marginal tax rate is 30%, applied to the net income after fees. The first-year net is roughly ($28,800 − $360 − $500) × 0.7, or about $19,558. That is 68% of the gross figure, and it assumes the trader never has a losing month and never fails the account.

Now add a realistic failure. Suppose the account fails in month nine and the trader takes two months to pass a new evaluation, paying another $500 in fees and earning nothing during the gap. The annual income drops by roughly three months of payouts, or $7,200, plus the new fee. The net figure becomes about $11,000, or 38% of the original gross. The example is not pessimistic; it is a normal year for a trader who is still learning to keep an account alive.

The lesson is that the gross table is the beginning of the calculation, not the end. The net figure depends on the pass rate, the failure rate, the cost structure and the tax rate. A trader who plans on the gross number will be disappointed; one who plans on the net number will choose a more conservative size and a more durable process.

Why account size beats return

The table earlier showed that a 1% return on a $200,000 account pays more than a 5% return on a $25,000 account. That is the central economic fact of prop trading: income scales with the account, and the account is easier to increase than the return.

There are three ways to increase the account. The first is to choose a larger account at the start, which requires a larger evaluation fee but often improves the cost per dollar of room. The second is a scale-up, which requires consistent performance over several months. The third is a second account at another firm, which requires another evaluation but diversifies the firm risk. Each has a cost, but all three are more reliable than trying to double the monthly return.

The reason is mathematical. A 1% improvement in monthly return is a large relative change in the strategy's edge, and it may not be sustainable. A 50% increase in account size is a change in the allocation, not the edge, and it can be achieved by passing a larger evaluation or adding an account. The income effect is the same or larger, with less pressure on the trading. The trader who focuses on size rather than return is optimising the input that is easier to control.

There is a limit, of course. A larger account produces larger dollar swings, and a trader who cannot handle a $4,000 daily move should not trade a $200,000 account. The optimum is the largest account on which the process is unchanged. That is a behavioural constraint, not a mathematical one, and it is the reason some traders earn more on a $50,000 account than on a $200,000 one.

Averages, medians and the distribution of outcomes

Any statement about "how much prop firm traders make" is a statement about a distribution, and the average is a poor summary of a skewed distribution. A small number of traders earn a lot; a large number earn little or nothing. The median is lower than the mean, and the gap is the story.

Why the average is misleading

If one trader earns $200,000 and ninety-nine earn nothing, the average is $2,000, which describes none of them. The prop trading industry has a similar shape: a small group of consistently profitable traders, a larger group who pass occasionally and earn modest payouts, and a large group who never reach a first payout. The average is pulled up by the top group, and the median is closer to zero. A trader planning an income should look at the median of traders with a similar account size and experience, not the average of the whole population.

The first-year curve

Most traders who eventually earn a meaningful income do not earn it in the first month. The first year is often a period of fees, failed attempts and small payouts. The income curve rises as the trader learns to keep an account alive, not as the strategy improves. A trader who expects a salary in month one is likely to quit before the curve turns; one who expects a build-up is more likely to persist.

The top decile

The top decile of traders tends to share three traits: a large account (or several accounts), a modest monthly return, and a long survival record. They are not the traders with the highest returns; they are the traders whose accounts last. That is consistent with the income equation: size and survival matter more than a spectacular month. The next page in this series looks at the behavioural side of that distribution.

There is a second reason the top group is small: the skills that produce a high return in a good month are not the skills that keep an account alive in a bad one. A trader can be excellent at finding entries and poor at reducing size after a drawdown. The income distribution reflects that difference, not just trading ability. The traders who last have usually built a process that survives their own worst behaviour, which is a different achievement from a strong quarter.

Estimating your own income

The most useful exercise is to estimate your own income from your own record, not from industry averages. Five steps are enough.

  1. Use your actual monthly return, not your best month. Take the median of the last six months, or the average if the variance is low. If you have no funded record, use a conservative 1–2% and treat the estimate as a hypothesis.
  2. Use the account size you can actually get funded. That is the size you can pass consistently, not the largest size on the firm's page.
  3. Use the net split after fees. Subtract the payout fee from the payout and apply the split. A 90% split with a $50 fee on a $500 payout is 80% net.
  4. Subtract the annual costs. Evaluation fees, resets, data fees and taxes. A simple spreadsheet with those rows is enough.
  5. Model a failure. Assume one failed account a year, or one three-month gap. If the income still meets your goal, the plan is robust; if it does not, the plan is too optimistic.

The output is a range, not a number. A trader who models a base case, a good case and a bad case is better prepared than one who plans on a single figure. The bad case should include a failed account and a losing month; the good case should not assume a return above the trader's demonstrated median.

There is also a decision rule worth writing down: the income target should be met at the base case, not the good case. If the plan only works when everything goes right, it is not a plan; it is a hope. The base case is the number to build the business around.

Common inflated claims

The prop trading space is full of income claims that do not survive arithmetic. These are the most common.

  1. "Make $10,000 a month with a $10,000 account." That is a 100% monthly return before the split, which is not a sustainable strategy. It is a marketing number.
  2. "Passive income." Prop trading is active risk management. Even with a service, the trader has to monitor the account, the rules and the payouts.
  3. "Guaranteed payouts." Payouts depend on the firm's terms, the account's performance and the trader's compliance. Nothing is guaranteed.
  4. "Your first payout in a week." Some firms pay quickly, but the evaluation, the minimum days and the processing time usually add up to weeks, not days.
  5. "The average trader makes $5,000 a month." The average is skewed by a small top group and is not a useful planning figure. The median is much lower.
  6. "No risk." The evaluation fee, the time and the risk of a failed account are all real. The risk is capped at the fee, but it is not zero.

The common thread is that the claims describe the best case as if it were the base case. A trader who plans on the best case will take more risk, fail more often, and earn less. A trader who plans on the base case will size conservatively, survive longer, and likely earn more over the year.

Frequently asked questions

How much do prop firm traders make per month?

It depends on account size, return and split. A 2% month on a $100,000 account at an 80% split pays about $1,600 before costs. A 1% month on a $200,000 account pays about $1,600 as well. Most traders earn little in the first months.

What is a realistic monthly return on a funded account?

Many consistent traders target 1–3% a month, with occasional better months and losing months. Returns above 5% consistently are rare and usually come with larger drawdowns.

Is prop trading income passive?

No. It requires monitoring, rule management and risk control. Even with a passing service, the trader has to manage the funded account, the payout conditions and the firm relationship.

How long does it take to earn a full-time income?

It varies, but most traders who reach a full-time income have been trading for years and have built up account size and survival records. The first year is often a build-up period with modest payouts.

Do I pay tax on prop firm payouts?

In most jurisdictions, yes. The treatment varies, so estimate the after-tax income and get local advice if the amount is material. The tax rate can be a larger deduction than any firm fee.

Is a bigger account always better?

It produces more income at the same return, but it also produces larger dollar swings. The best account is the largest one on which your process and risk tolerance remain unchanged.

What costs should I subtract from gross income?

Evaluation fees, reset fees, data and platform fees, payout processing, taxes and the cost of failed accounts. The net figure is often 50–70% of the gross figure in the first year.
