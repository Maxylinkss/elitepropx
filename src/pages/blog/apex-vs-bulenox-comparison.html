Apex Trader Funding vs Bulenox: which rulebook fits your trading

Both firms evaluate US futures with a trailing threshold, and both run on the same three platforms. The meaningful differences are in the trail's definition, whether a daily stop applies, and how the fee is charged — Apex on a monthly subscription per account, Bulenox on a one-off fee or a subscription depending on the promotion. On a futures account those three rows decide more than any target figure. The numbers below describe each rulebook's published shape; confirm the current plan terms before buying.

Key takeaways

  • Apex trails against peak equity including open profit, so an unrealised winner raises the failure point mid-trade.
  • Bulenox trails as well, but applies a daily stop on some plans, which changes how a bad session ends.
  • Both require a minimum-day condition, so neither can be completed in one good week.

What the two rulebooks measure

Two futures evaluations with the same market and the same platforms are separated by three questions. Does the floor follow the high-water mark including floating profit, or settle at some defined point? Is there a session limit on top of the floor? And how is the fee charged, because that determines what a slow attempt costs.

Apex answers the first question strictly: its threshold trails peak equity including open profit, so an unrealised gain is treated as banked for the purpose of the floor. Bulenox also trails, and applies a daily stop on some plans. The daily stop is a real difference, because it caps a session independently of the buffer, which is the one protection a trail does not provide.

The third question is where the products diverge commercially. Apex bills monthly per account, which makes duration a cost and rewards a fast, correctly sized attempt. Bulenox charges either a one-off fee or a subscription depending on the promotion, which means the same brand can produce two different cost structures across two purchase dates.

That variability is not a criticism; it is a fact to plan around. A trader comparing the two should compare the specific offer available at the moment of purchase, written down, rather than a remembered price or a review from last quarter.

Two trails, one important difference

A trail that includes open profit moves while a position is running. A trade that reaches $1,200 unrealised has lifted the failure point by $1,200, and a retracement to entry consumes that room without a dollar being lost. A trail that settles at a defined point, usually the close, does not move until the session ends, so a position can breathe within the day.

That distinction is the first thing to confirm on the Bulenox document. If the trail is measured from settled balance, a trader can hold a runner through an intraday retracement without touching the buffer. If it is measured from peak equity including floating profit, the trader is effectively trading a live floor and should take profit at defined levels.

Apex's version is explicit and it is the stricter of the two. On that side, the practical rule is to bank profit at a target rather than hold for a larger move, because the floor has already recorded the high. A trader who ignores this is not merely giving back gains; they are shrinking the buffer with a trade that ends flat.

The planning consequence is a taper. On a live trail, full size until half the target, half size until three quarters, and a third afterwards. On a settled trail, the taper can be gentler because the floor only moves once a day and the buffer is known before the session begins.

A $50,000 attempt, line by line

Start with a $2,500 threshold on $50,000. On the live-trail version, the failure point begins at $47,500. A position runs $900 into profit, lifting it to $48,400. The trade is closed at $300 profit after the retracement. The balance is $50,300, the floor is $48,400, and the buffer is $1,900 — smaller than the $2,500 the trader started with, despite a winning trade.

On the settled version, the same sequence leaves the floor at $47,500 during the session. At the close the balance is $50,300 and the floor moves to $47,800 if it trails the settled high. The buffer is $2,500, which is larger in relative terms than the starting figure because the balance rose and the floor rose by less.

The difference between the two outcomes is $600 of buffer on a $2,500 threshold — nearly a quarter of the account's room — produced by one trade that made $300. That is the clearest illustration of why the trail definition matters more than the headline threshold.

The sizing response follows directly. On the live version, the unit has to be recalculated after a winner, not only after a loss, because the buffer can shrink on a profitable trade. On the settled version, the unit can be set once a day from the previous close and held through the session.

Daily stops where they exist

A daily stop caps a session independently of the buffer. Where it exists, a bad day ends at a known figure and the account continues with the floor intact. Where it does not, the trail is the only limit, and the trail does not distinguish between one bad afternoon and five bad days.

Apex's standard evaluation has no separate daily stop, so the trader supplies one. A figure that keeps the worst day at a third of the buffer is the natural choice: with $2,500 of room, roughly $800, which at a $250 unit is three losses. That rule is stricter than any firm's published limit and it is what keeps a live trail from being consumed in a single session.

Bulenox applies a daily stop on some plans, which changes the plan rather than the arithmetic. Where it applies, the session ends at a figure and the trader cannot continue; where it does not, the self-imposed cap does the same work voluntarily. Either way, the number should be on the card before the open.

The basis matters on both sides. A limit measured on equity counts floating losses, so a position that is $600 underwater has consumed the allowance before it is closed. A limit measured on realised results permits a larger floating loss. Confirm which applies, because the difference is a whole risk unit on a single trade.

Promotion-driven billing

A fixed monthly subscription is easy to model: monthly cost times expected months times expected attempts. A promotion-dependent fee is harder, because the same product can be cheap in one quarter and ordinary in the next, and a trader who plans around the cheap version may be disappointed at renewal.

The practical approach is to price the worst case. Take the standard fee, not the promotional one, and run the arithmetic on that. If the plan only works at the discounted price, it does not work. A discount should improve an already viable calculation, not create one.

The second approach is to check the renewal terms. A promotional one-off fee that becomes a monthly subscription after the first month is a different product from a one-off fee that stays one-off. Read the checkout page rather than the landing page, and save it.

The third is to compare like with like across a year. Apex at an illustrative $150 per month plus $50 of data is $2,400 for a year of continuous attempts. A Bulenox one-off at an illustrative $400 per attempt is $1,200 for three attempts. The comparison is not between fee amounts but between annual costs at a realistic number of attempts.

There is a fourth consideration that rarely appears in a fee table: the cost of an abandoned account. A subscription keeps charging while an account sits untouched, so a trader who buys in January and returns in April has paid for three months of nothing. A one-off fee does not punish absence, which is a real advantage for anyone trading around a job, a holiday or an uneven schedule.

Contract sizing on either

The arithmetic is identical on both products because the instruments are the same. One Micro E-mini Nasdaq contract pays $2 per tick, so a 15-tick stop risks $30 per contract. One E-mini S&P contract pays $12.50 per tick, so an 8-tick stop risks $100. One Micro E-mini Dow contract pays $0.50 per tick, so a 20-tick stop risks $10.

With a $250 unit, the Nasdaq micro allows eight contracts, the S&P mini allows two, and the Dow micro allows twenty-five. A factor of twelve between the smallest and largest count for the same dollar risk is why sizing from contracts rather than from dollars is the most common error on a futures account.

Check the position cap before the risk budget. Many futures plans limit contracts per position or total exposure, and the cap can bind before the unit does on a small stop. A size that fits the budget and breaches the cap is a rule violation, not a size.

It is also worth checking whether the cap is applied per position or across the account. A plan that counts total exposure constrains a trader who runs two instruments at once even when each position is individually legal, and that constraint only becomes visible on the third trade of a busy session.

Add a slippage allowance. A stop order becomes a market order on trigger, and in a fast tape the fill can be several ticks beyond the level. On a 15-tick stop, three ticks of slip is a 20% overrun. On a live trail that overrun is felt immediately, because the floor has already recorded the high.

Minimum days and the calendar

Both products apply a minimum-day condition, so neither can be finished in a single good week. The rule exists to ensure the result is a process rather than one oversized trade, and it turns the calendar into a constraint to plan rather than a detail to discover at payout.

On Apex the calendar is billed, so every required session is a day of subscription. On Bulenox a one-off fee makes the same sessions free, which changes the optimal pace: patience costs nothing on one side and money on the other.

The correct plan counts the required sessions, maps them against availability, and spreads the target across them. A trader who reaches the target in week one and then waits out the minimum still has to keep the account alive during the wait, which is an underrated risk on a trailing product.

The wait is not passive. It is the best opportunity to build the distribution a consistency condition wants and to prepare the payout documents. Treating the required sessions as administration is what stops a completed target becoming a breached account.

The shared platform stack

Both firms list Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader, so the workflow question disappears. A trader can move between the products without changing anything, and a trader who has not used any of them faces the same learning curve on either side.

The choice within the list is about style. Tradovate is the lightest and fastest to learn, with a clean DOM. Rithmic is a data and routing layer valued for depth of market and execution speed. NinjaTrader is the programmable option with the largest ecosystem and the most complete order log.

Because the platforms are shared, the comparison is unusually clean: the only variables are the rules and the fee. That is rarer than it sounds. Most firm comparisons are contaminated by a platform migration that costs more than the rule difference being evaluated.

Data fees are the remaining variable. Futures data is usually billed separately and depth-of-market is the expensive tier. Confirm which feed the plan includes and which one the method needs, and add it to the monthly budget rather than discovering it at the first statement.

Payout conditions

Apex pays from the funded account after qualifying days, with the request made from the dashboard. Bulenox pays after qualification under its own programme terms. The difference that matters is the time from a profitable month to cleared funds and the conditions that can interrupt it.

Both review conduct before releasing profit. On a trailing product the statements show a floor that moved during the session, which can look unusual to a reviewer. A journal that records the buffer at the start and the end of each session answers that question before it is asked.

Documents and payment details sit on the critical path. Money normally returns along the same route it arrived by, so a replacement card forces a manual check. Complete the checks while the target is still open, and keep the fee receipt with them.

Ask whether the split is calculated on gross or net profit, and whether the funded stage keeps the evaluation's rulebook. Those two replies are worth more than the advertised percentage.

Retry maths over twelve months

Estimate the pass probability from your own record and divide the annual cost by it. At an illustrative $600 per attempt and a 30% pass probability, the expected cost per funded account on the subscription side is $2,000. At $400 per attempt and the same probability on the one-off side, it is $1,333.

The lever that improves both numbers is a smaller unit. Cutting the unit by a third usually turns a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown, and the fee is unchanged, so the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

The second lever is the plan variant. On the Bulenox side the daily stop applies to some plans, and a plan with a daily stop is easier to survive than one without, because the session ends at a known figure. On the Apex side the variant question is the same: confirm exactly which product the fee buys.

The third lever is the calendar. A subscription rewards a short attempt, so the plan should target a realistic duration. A one-off fee rewards a careful one, so the calendar can be longer without cost. Choosing the billing model that matches your natural pace is worth more than optimising the fee.

Releases and the overnight session

Both products trade US futures, so both face the same event calendar: central bank decisions, inflation prints, employment reports and, for some instruments, inventory data. The reaction is fast, the spread widens before the release, and the stop that looked safe at 09:25 is not the stop that fills at 09:30.

Restrictions vary by plan. Some prohibit opening positions in a window around a release, some prohibit holding through it, and some prohibit profiting from the resulting spike. The clause is short, rarely on the marketing page and enforced at review, which makes it a delayed failure with a complete audit trail.

The conservative plan is to be flat into the release and re-enter after the first half hour. A trader who holds should halve the position and widen the stop to a level the buffer can survive if it gaps — a stricter calculation on a live trail, because the gap can lift the floor and consume the buffer in the same move.

The overnight session is the second scheduled risk. It is thinner, spreads are wider, and the daily settlement can change margin without a trade. A position held across the settlement is the position that sets tomorrow's buffer and therefore tomorrow's unit.

Which futures trader belongs on which

Apex fits a trader who closes within a session, takes profit at levels and can watch the US regular session. The live trail rewards precision and punishes give-backs, and the subscription rewards finishing inside two months.

Bulenox fits a trader who wants a one-off option, a daily stop on the plan chosen, and a slower calendar. Where the daily stop applies, it suits a method with a small number of high-quality trades per session, because the day ends at a known figure rather than at the trader's discretion.

The tie-breaker is the billing structure matching the trader's pace. A trader who expects three attempts in a year should price them as three attempts on both products and compare the totals, not the headline fees. A trader who expects one clean pass should compare the three-month cost on each.

If the answer is still unclear, simulate both on the same hundred trades with the same dollar risk: apply the live trail to one and the settled trail plus daily stop to the other. Whichever survives with the smaller drawdown is the structure the method already fits.

What to check before paying

The first is the trail definition: whether the high is measured from equity including open profit, from settled balance, or at a scheduled point. The second is whether a daily stop applies to the exact plan and on what basis.

The third is the minimum-day condition and whether it applies to the evaluation, the funded account or the payout. The fourth is the fee structure at checkout, including whether a promotional price renews at a different rate.

The fifth is the position cap, in contracts or exposure. The sixth is the conduct clauses: news windows, order types, automation and third-party trading. Each is short, each is enforced, and none is reliably on the comparison page.

Save a dated copy of every answer. It takes ten minutes and it is the only document that settles a dispute six weeks later, when the terms page has been revised.

A thirty-day plan for either

The first week is a measurement exercise at half size. Log the buffer before each entry, note what the floor does after a winning trade, and record the spread you actually paid. By Friday the account's behaviour should be a table of numbers rather than an impression.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, and a hard stop after two losing trades. Recompute the buffer each morning and halve the unit when it falls below one twelfth of the starting threshold. On the live trail this is where compression is felt.

Week four is consolidation: a third of the unit, the result protected, and the payout file assembled. Pressing after the target is met is the classic way to hand back a finished attempt.

Across all four weeks, three numbers belong on a card: buffer, unit and session cap. The products differ in how they move the first one; neither can do anything about a trader who does not look.

Common questions

Do both firms trail the loss floor?

Yes, both use a trailing threshold. The important differences are whether the trail includes open profit and whether it updates continuously or at a scheduled point, so confirm the exact definition for the plan.

Does Bulenox have a daily stop?

It applies on some plans rather than all. Check the specific product before buying, because a plan with a daily stop ends a bad session at a known figure while one without it relies on the trail.

Which billing model is better?

It depends on pace. A subscription suits a trader who expects to finish inside two months; a one-off fee suits a slower attempt. Price the annual cost at a realistic number of attempts rather than comparing headline fees.

Are the platforms identical?

Both list Tradovate, Rithmic and NinjaTrader, so the workflow does not change. Data feeds may still differ in what is included, so confirm the feed before comparing monthly costs.

Can either be passed in a week?

No. Both apply a minimum-day condition, so the calendar is a constraint regardless of how quickly the target is reached. Spread the target across the required sessions.

How much should I risk per trade on $50,000?

With an illustrative $2,500 trail and a ten-loss tolerance, about $250 per trade. Add a slip buffer, and check the plan's contract cap before converting that figure into contracts.
