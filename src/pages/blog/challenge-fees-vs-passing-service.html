Challenge Fees vs Passing Service · reviewed October 2026

Challenge Fees vs Passing Service

The comparison is usually made as a price against a price: an evaluation fee of a few hundred dollars against a flat service fee. That comparison is wrong in both directions. The evaluation is not one fee but an expected cost that depends on how many attempts you need, and the service fee is not the whole cost either — it has a terms risk and a funding-conversion risk attached. This page prices both properly and shows the break-even point on a realistic retake rate.

Key takeaways

  • An evaluation fee is an expected cost, not a single price. Multiply the fee by the number of attempts you realistically need.
  • Subscription-based firms change the arithmetic: a slow trader pays monthly whether or not progress is being made.
  • A passing service's headline fee excludes the evaluation fee, the terms risk and the possibility of a reversed pass.
  • The comparison should be made against the value of the first payout, not against the fee.
  • Neither route is cheaper in general. The right answer depends on retake rate, rulebook fit and how you value certainty.

Two traders can pay the same $400 for the same challenge and have completely different costs. One passes first time and has paid $400 for a funded account. The other fails three times, pays $1,200, and passes on the fourth attempt. The product was the same; the cost was three times higher. Any comparison that treats the fee as a price has already lost that information.

The same problem runs the other way for services. A $220 fee looks small next to four attempts at $400. But the service fee is not the full cost either, because the evaluation still has to be bought, and because a pass produced by a third party carries a risk that a self-traded pass does not.

The comparison that matters

Price both routes as expected total cost to reach the same end state: a funded account that can pay out. That means including every cash outflow, weighting each by its probability, and adding the cost of the risks that are not invoices.

Cost lineSelf-traded routeService route
Evaluation feePaid per attemptPaid once, plus service fee
RetakesExpected across attemptsUsually covered by the service
SubscriptionsAccumulate with timeAccumulate while the service trades
Service feeNoneFlat, typically a few hundred
Terms riskLow — you trade your own accountMaterial where third-party trading is prohibited
Time costWeeks of your own attentionTime is the thing you are buying

The first four rows are money and can be modelled. The last two are the ones that decide whether the model is useful. A lower expected cost that carries a risk of total loss at payout is not lower in any meaningful sense, and an expensive route that reliably produces a payout can be the cheaper one per dollar of funded income.

Evaluation cost as an expected value

Take a firm with a $400 fee, a 10% target and no subscription. The single-attempt cost is $400. The expected cost depends on the probability of failing, because a failure means buying again.

Pass rate per attemptExpected attemptsExpected fee costMedian attempts
50%2.0$8001
35%2.9$1,1602
25%4.0$1,6003
15%6.7$2,6804
10%10.0$4,0007

Two things about this table are worth dwelling on. The first is the gap between expected and median attempts. At a 25% pass rate the expected number of attempts is four, but the median is three, because the distribution is long-tailed: a minority of traders take many attempts and pull the average up. Most individual traders experience something close to the median and therefore underestimate the average cost.

The second is the slope. Going from a 35% to a 25% pass rate raises the expected cost from $1,160 to $1,600, a 38% increase for a ten-point change. Small differences in rulebook fit — a trailing floor instead of a static one, a five-day minimum instead of none — move that pass rate more than most traders expect. The cheapest fee is not the cheapest product.

The pass rate itself is not published by any firm, and it is not a single number: it depends on the trader, the rulebook and the phase. The honest way to estimate it is your own history on similar rules, or a conservative assumption such as 25–35% for a first attempt at an unfamiliar structure. Using a 70% pass rate because it feels achievable is how the model stops being useful.

Subscription firms and the slow-trader trap

Subscription billing changes the shape of the cost. Instead of a fee per attempt, you pay for time, and the cost grows with how long you take rather than with how often you fail.

Billing modelCost driverWho it favoursWho it punishes
One-off fee per attemptNumber of failuresSlow, patient tradersTraders who fail repeatedly
Monthly subscriptionDays on the accountFast, decisive tradersTraders who take weeks to finish
Subscription with a resetMonths, plus retakesTraders who pass inside one cycleAnyone who needs a second month
Activation fee plus monthlyUpfront plus ongoingTraders who finish fast after fundingTraders who hold the account idle

A $120 monthly subscription looks cheaper than a $400 one-off fee until the third month. At four months it has cost $480, and a trader who is sitting at 6% of an 8% target with a minimum-day requirement still counting has no way to stop paying without abandoning the account.

This is where a passing service and a subscription interact badly. If the service trades slowly and the plan bills monthly, the service fee is only the visible cost; the subscription runs in the background for the duration. A service that quotes a flat fee without asking about the billing model has not priced the job.

The reverse also holds. A fast service on a subscription firm can produce a cheaper total than a slower service on a one-off firm, because the subscription stops when the pass lands. The duration, not the headline rate, is the variable that matters.

What a passing service actually costs

The service fee is one line of four. The others are easy to omit when comparing against an evaluation fee.

ComponentIllustrative amountWho bears it
Evaluation fee$400Buyer
Service fee$220Buyer
Subscription during the pass$0–$360Buyer
RetakesCovered by most servicesService
Total to a funded account$620–$980Buyer

Against a self-traded expected cost of $1,160 at a 35% pass rate, the service route is cheaper on cash. That is the honest arithmetic and it is why the product exists. The comparison only becomes interesting once the non-invoice risks are added.

The first is the terms risk. If the firm prohibits third-party trading, the pass can be reversed at funding or the payout withheld, and the $620–$980 is gone with no funded account at the end. That risk is not a percentage that can be averaged; for a given firm it is either zero, because the terms permit managed trading and the service discloses it, or it is a large probability of total loss. Averaging it hides the structure.

The second is the conversion risk. A pass is not a payout. The funded account still has to survive its first cycle, meet any seasoning or consistency condition and clear a review. A service that delivers a pass but no support afterwards leaves the buyer holding that risk alone.

Break-even on retake rates

Set the two routes equal and solve for the pass rate. With a $400 evaluation fee, a $220 service fee and no subscription, the service route costs $620. The self-traded route costs $400 divided by the pass rate.

100%$400$620Self-traded
65%$615$620Break-even
50%$800$620Service
35%$1,160$620Service

The break-even pass rate is around 65% for these numbers. That is a demanding threshold: it means passing two attempts in three. A trader who genuinely passes at that rate is unusual, and a trader who believes they do is usually estimating from a small sample.

The honest reading is not "the service always wins". It is that the service wins on cash for anyone whose realistic pass rate is below roughly two-thirds, and that this covers most first attempts at an unfamiliar rulebook. The question then becomes whether the terms risk is small enough to accept that saving.

The numbers change with the fee structure. On a $250 evaluation fee the break-even rate falls to about 60%. On a $600 fee it rises above 70%. Subscription billing shifts the calculation again, because the service's speed becomes a cost variable rather than a fixed one.

The risks that are not on the invoice

Four risks separate the routes, and only one of them is financial in a way a spreadsheet captures.

Terms risk. Covered above. Where third-party trading is prohibited, the service route carries a chance of total loss that the self-traded route does not. This is the single largest difference between the two and it is decided by the firm's terms, not by the service's price.

Skill risk. A self-traded pass teaches you something. The rulebook, the pacing, the drawdown management — all of it transfers to the funded account. A purchased pass transfers nothing, and the funded account that follows is still yours to trade. A trader who cannot pass an evaluation is unlikely to hold a funded account for long, which means the service buys the account but not the ability to keep it.

Concentration risk. A service trading many accounts the same way concentrates the firm's exposure, which is one reason firms restrict it. Where it is permitted, the service's own risk of a firm-level review is a risk the buyer inherits.

Counterparty risk. The service is a business that may stop operating, dispute the scope of a retake, or fail to deliver on a timeline. A flat fee paid in advance is a claim on that business. Ask what happens to an in-progress evaluation if the service is unavailable, and get the answer in writing.

Comparing against the payout, not the fee

Both routes are cheap relative to the thing they produce. A first payout on a $100,000 account at an 8% target and a 90% split is $7,200, and even after the retained balance it is several thousand dollars. Against that, the difference between a $620 route and a $1,160 route is a rounding error.

That framing is useful because it reframes the decision. The question is not "which is cheaper" but "which gives me the higher chance of reaching a payout I can keep". Cost is a second-order variable next to whether the funded account survives its first review.

It also exposes the case where neither route is sensible. A trader who pays $1,160 to reach a funded account and then breaches it in the first month has bought an expensive lesson in both scenarios. The fee comparison did not cause that; the absence of a plan for the funded account did.

A decision rule

The comparison collapses into four questions, in order.

  1. Do the firm's terms permit third-party trading? If not, the service route has a total-loss risk and the comparison is not about price.
  2. What is your realistic pass rate on this rulebook? Above about 65%, trade it yourself. Below that, the service is cheaper on cash.
  3. Which billing model does the firm use? On subscriptions, a slow service is more expensive than it looks; on one-off fees, retakes dominate.
  4. Can you hold a funded account once you have one? If not, neither route is worth buying, and the money is better spent on a smaller firm where the lesson is cheaper.

Written out, the rule is unglamorous: check the terms, estimate your own pass rate honestly, price the billing model, and decide whether the account is one you can keep. Most of the argument about which route is cheaper disappears once the first question has an answer.

Three firms, three billing models

Billing model changes which route is cheaper, and it changes it by more than the pass-rate assumption does. Three illustrative firms, all with a $100,000 account and an 8% target.

FirmFeeBillingSelf-traded, fast finishSelf-traded, slow finishService route
A$400One-off$400 (attempt 1)$400 (attempt 1)$620
B$120Monthly$120 (1 month)$480 (4 months)$460 (2 months incl. fee)
C$300 + $60/moHybrid$360 (1 month)$540 (4 months)$580

The table makes the point that a single comparison cannot cover three products. On firm A, a fast, competent trader pays $400 and a service at $620 is worse value. On firm B, the same trader pays $120 — a third of the one-off fee — and the service route looks expensive because the self-traded route is unusually cheap for a quick finish. On the slow path, firm B becomes the most expensive self-traded route in the table at $480.

The hybrid is the most common structure and the one that punishes indecision. The activation fee is sunk, the monthly charge continues while the account sits incomplete, and the trader has a strong incentive to finish quickly or abandon. A trader who is not confident of finishing inside two months should price the four-month column before buying.

The correct comparison is therefore not between the two routes in general, but between the two routes on the specific firm and the specific completion time. A service that finishes in six weeks is cheap on firm B and expensive on firm A. The same service fee is a different product depending on the clock it is racing.

The cost of a delayed pass

A pass that arrives three months later than expected is not a cheaper pass. It is the same pass with three months of subscription, three months of risk and three months of foregone payouts attached.

DelayExtra subscription at $120/monthForegone payouts at $2,000/monthTotal economic cost
1 month$120$2,000$2,120
2 months$240$4,000$4,240
3 months$360$6,000$6,360
6 months$720$12,000$12,720

The foregone-payout column dominates the subscription column by an order of magnitude. That is the economic argument for paying a higher service fee for a faster pass, and it is also the argument against a cheap route that takes an extra quarter to complete. A difference of $200 in fees is trivial next to a difference of two months in delivery.

Two caveats keep the table honest. The foregone payouts are not guaranteed; a funded account may not pay $2,000 in its first month, and a trader who would have breached it would have earned nothing. And speed has a cost in quality: a service that finishes very fast may use a style that is more likely to breach in the funded phase. The comparison is between expected outcomes, not between best cases.

What to ask a service before paying

  1. Do you check the firm's terms before accepting the job? Where third-party trading is prohibited, a quote is incomplete without this check.
  2. What exactly is covered? The evaluation fee, retakes, both phases, and any subscription during the pass. A flat fee that excludes the firm's own charges is not flat.
  3. What happens if the evaluation fails? Retake policy, time limits on the retake, and whether the service or the buyer bears the second fee.
  4. What happens after the pass? Whether any support continues into the funded account, and whether that support is included.
  5. How long does a pass usually take? A range rather than a promise, and the basis for it.
  6. What is your disclosure process? Whether the service documents the arrangement with the firm, and in what form.
  7. What is your history on the firms you recommend? Completed passes, reversals and refusals, with the reasons.

Questions two, three and seven are the ones that separate a service from a promise. A quote that does not name the firm's fees, does not state a retake policy and cannot describe a reversal is a quote for an outcome the seller has not priced.

The answers should be in writing. A chat reply is adequate if it is retained, and a written scope is better. In a dispute about what was included, the written scope is the contract, and the marketing page is not.

Why the cheapest quote is often the most expensive

The cheapest service quote is usually the one that excludes the evaluation fee, does not cover retakes, or assumes a fast pass on a subscription firm. Each exclusion moves cost to the buyer, and the moved cost is larger than the discount.

A $120 quote that excludes the $400 evaluation fee is a $520 product. A $220 quote that excludes retakes becomes a $620 product after one failure and $1,020 after two. A $180 quote that takes four months on a $120 subscription firm is $660 in firm charges before the service fee is counted. None of these is dishonest; each is a different scope, and the scope is the price.

The comparison that works is total cost to a funded account, with the retake risk and the completion time stated. That is a harder number to obtain, which is exactly why the cheap quote is usually quoting something else.

Retake policies in detail

"Retakes covered" is one of the most valuable phrases in a service quote and one of the least specific. Four policies hide behind it, and they produce very different costs.

PolicyWhat the buyer pays on a failureEffective value
Unlimited retakes, fee includedNothingHighest; the service absorbs the tail
One retake includedThe second evaluation feeModerate; covers the common single failure
Retake at a discountA reduced feeLower; the tail is still the buyer's
No retake, new purchaseThe full fee againLowest; equivalent to buying the evaluation yourself

The value of the first policy is not the average retake. It is the tail. A trader with a 35% pass rate has a 27% chance of failing twice in a row and an 18% chance of failing three times, and those outcomes are where the service earns its fee. A quote that covers one retake is worth much less than it appears, because the second failure — the expensive one — falls back on the buyer.

The policy should also be checked for scope. Does it cover both phases, or only the first? Does it cover a breach of a rule as well as a failure to hit target? Does it lapse after a period? A retake that expires in thirty days is not the same product as an open one, and a policy that covers phase one but not phase two leaves the largest single risk with the buyer.

The honest summary is that a service with unlimited retakes is buying the failure distribution from you at a fixed price, and a service with one retake is buying only the first failure. Both can be fair; they are not the same quote, and the difference should be visible before payment rather than after a loss.

Sunk cost and the third attempt

Every cost model on this page assumes a buyer who stops buying attempts once the expected cost overtakes the alternative. Most traders do not. Three failed attempts at $400 is $1,200 already spent, and the fourth attempt is rarely judged on its own merits; it is judged as the attempt that has to work in order to justify the first three. That is sunk-cost reasoning, and it is expensive precisely because it arrives after the point where the comparison should have been revisited.

The arithmetic that keeps the decision honest is simple: money already paid is gone, and the only question is whether another $400 has a better expected return than the alternative in front of you. Written that way, the third attempt and the first attempt cost the same, and neither is cheaper because of what came before it.

Three habits make that easier to apply:

  • Set an attempt budget before the first purchase. Decide the maximum number of evaluations you are willing to buy, and the total amount, while you are still calm. A budget of two attempts at $400 is a decision you can keep; a budget invented on attempt four is a reaction.
  • Re-price the alternative at every failure. After each failed attempt, recalculate the expected cost of continuing, including subscriptions and time, and compare it with the flat fee. The comparison changes as your own pass rate becomes better documented.
  • Separate the fee decision from the account decision. A funded account you cannot hold is not worth the cheapest route to it, and a pass that arrives in month four has cost three months of foregone payouts.

A flat service fee changes the shape of this problem rather than solving it. One payment with retakes covered means a failed attempt costs nothing extra in service fees, so there is no escalating invoice to defend. The evaluation fee and any subscription still exist, and the terms risk is unchanged, but the decision that sunk cost distorts most — buy another attempt or stop — disappears from the invoice entirely.

That is the least discussed part of the value: not the saving on the average attempt, but the removal of a decision traders routinely get wrong after two losses.

Common questions

Is a passing service cheaper than retaking a challenge?

Below a realistic pass rate of roughly 65% it usually is, on cash. Above that, retaking is cheaper in expectation. The comparison has to include subscription billing and the evaluation fee, not just the two headline prices.

What is the expected cost of passing a challenge yourself?

Divide the evaluation fee by your pass rate. At a 35% pass rate and a $400 fee, the expected cost is about $1,160 across attempts. At 25% it is $1,600. The median number of attempts is lower than the average because a minority of traders take many attempts.

Do passing services cover retakes?

Most reputable ones do, and that is a large part of what the fee buys. Ask what happens if the first attempt fails, whether a retake is included in the flat fee, and whether it applies to every phase.

Why do subscriptions make a challenge more expensive?

Because the cost grows with time rather than with failures. A slow trader on a monthly plan pays for every month on the account, including months spent waiting for a minimum-day count or a payout cycle.

What is the biggest hidden cost of a service route?

Terms risk. Where a firm prohibits third-party trading, a purchased pass can be reversed at funding or the payout withheld, and the evaluation fee plus service fee is lost. That risk is binary for a given firm rather than a small percentage.

Does a purchased pass prepare me for the funded account?

No. The funded account still has to be traded by you, and the skill that an evaluation builds does not transfer through a purchase. Buy the route that also leaves you able to hold the account.

Should I compare costs against the fee or the payout?

The payout. A first payout is several thousand dollars on a six-figure account, and the difference between the cheapest and most expensive route is a small share of it. Choose for the probability of a payout you can keep, not for the smallest invoice.

Are failed evaluation fees wasted?

They are a cost of the activity, and in many jurisdictions they are deductible where trading is treated as a business. They also produce information about rulebook fit, which is why firms with different structures teach different lessons.
