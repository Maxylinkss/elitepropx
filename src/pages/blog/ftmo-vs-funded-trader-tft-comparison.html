FTMO vs Funded Trader TFT: which rulebook fits your trading

These are close relatives: both evaluate forex and CFD on a static floor, both run a two-phase path, and both list MT4, MT5 and cTrader. The differences sit in the details that decide a payout — whether a consistency test applies, how the daily stop is measured, and how long the payout takes. FTMO adds a verification stage and a seasoning period; TFT applies a consistency condition. Figures describe each rulebook's published shape — confirm the plan terms before buying.

Reading two similar rulebooks

When two products share a market, a floor type and a platform list, the differences live in the administrative rows. Those rows are the ones traders skim, and they are the ones that decide whether a completed target converts into money.

FTMO describes a two-phase evaluation followed by a verification stage, on a static floor measured from the starting balance, with an equity-based daily stop and no consistency rule on the evaluation. Funded Trader TFT describes a two-phase evaluation on a static floor, with a daily stop and a consistency condition. The floor mechanics are effectively identical; the path length and the consistency row are not.

The third difference is timing. FTMO's first payout waits for a seasoning period after the funded account begins, then moves to a shorter schedule. TFT releases a profit split on a fixed cycle after qualification. The total elapsed time from first payment to cleared funds is the number to compare, and it is longer than either schedule implies.

A fourth difference sits in the platform list: DXtrade appears on the FTMO side and not on TFT's. That matters only to a trader who wants a browser-based terminal without expert advisor support, and it is a preference rather than a rule.

Two phases, with and without verification

FTMO's path is three stages: two evaluations and a verification stage, each with its own target and each measured against the same static floor. The verification stage exists to confirm the first result was a process rather than a favourable month, and it is where passes built on one oversized trade tend to fail.

TFT's path is two stages, with the consistency condition doing the work the verification stage does on the other side. A consistency test limits the share of profit that a single day may contribute, which is a different way of asking the same question: was this a process or a week?

The practical difference is where a trader fails. On FTMO, a trader who reaches the first target with a lumpy distribution can pass and then fail verification, having paid once and spent two months. On TFT, the same trader fails the consistency check immediately, which is cheaper and more informative.

Neither structure is easier. A three-stage path rewards repetition across a longer calendar, while a two-stage path with a distribution test rewards a smoother result inside a shorter one. A trader whose method is steady at a modest size suits either; a trader whose method wins in bursts suits neither without a change to size.

There is a cost difference hidden in the path length that rarely appears in a comparison. A three-stage path takes longer, and a longer path exposes the account to more news events, more weekends and more chances for a single bad session. The extra stage filters the trader, and it also filters luck in both directions.

Static floors and what they permit

A static floor measured from the starting balance does not move. On $100,000 with a 10% floor the failure point is $90,000 for the whole path, and profit adds room above it. A constant risk unit therefore stays valid from the first trade to the last, which removes one decision from every morning.

That comfort has a cost: the rulebook will not stop a slow bleed. An account can fall from $112,000 to $95,000 without any rule being broken, which is a 15% drawdown from the high and a 5% buffer from failure. Only a self-imposed limit prevents that.

TFT's static floor behaves the same way, and the same limit applies. Set a stop at a third of the floor from the equity high: if the account reaches $110,000, the stop is $106,700. That rule preserves gains the floor permits giving back.

The second self-imposed rule is a weekly cap. Four sessions at a personal limit can consume a large share of the floor before any single rule has been broken. A weekly cap of one third of the floor converts a slow bleed into a pause.

The third is a trigger for reducing size. Once the drawdown from the equity high passes a quarter of the floor, cut the unit in half and keep it there until the account makes a new high. That rule is mechanical, requires no judgement in the moment, and is the single most effective protection on a product with no trailing mechanic.

A $100,000 path on each

On FTMO, assume an 8% target for phase one and an 8% target for phase two, with a 10% floor. Phase one needs $8,000 and phase two needs another $8,000 measured from the new balance. A $500 unit with a 2:1 payoff returns $1,000 per winner, so each phase needs eight net winners.

On TFT, the same 8% targets apply but the consistency test shapes the route. If no single day may contribute more than a quarter of the total, no day can add more than $2,000 in phase one. At a $500 unit that is two winners in a day, which is a natural limit rather than a constraint for most methods.

The difference appears when a method produces a large day. On FTMO that day counts toward the target and can be verified later; on TFT it can satisfy the target and still fail the distribution. The same month passes one rulebook and fails the other without any change in skill.

Both paths therefore need a daily profit cap, not just a daily loss cap. Capping a winning day at two or three units flattens the distribution and costs almost nothing in expectation, which makes it the cheapest insurance in either product.

Daily stops and the equity basis

FTMO's daily stop is measured on equity, which means floating losses count as they occur. A position that is $2,000 underwater on a $100,000 account has consumed 40% of a 5% allowance before it is closed. That produces better behaviour and tighter statements.

TFT applies a daily stop without a stated basis in the summary. The question to settle is whether it is equity or realised, and at what time it resets. An equity basis counts open risk; a realised basis permits a larger floating loss and a different behaviour at the close.

A personal cap does the real work on both. With a $500 unit and a three-loss cap, the worst session is $1,500, which is 1.5% of the account and well inside any published limit. Three losses end the session, and a second session is allowed only if the weekly budget still has room.

The reset time matters for a swing method. A position carried across the roll can be attributed to either day, and the attribution decides whether a loss is a warning or a breach. If the rule is unclear, flatten before the roll rather than argue afterwards.

The consistency test

A consistency test exists to distinguish a process from a single position. It limits how much of the profit one day, or a few days, may contribute, and it changes the shape of a correct attempt: the target has to be reached through a series of ordinary sessions.

FTMO does not apply one to the evaluation, which leaves the distribution free until the payout review. TFT applies one, which makes the distribution a planning input from week one. A trader whose method produces occasional large days is affected by TFT and not by FTMO.

The test to run before choosing is the share of profit from the best day. Take sixty sessions, total the profit, and compute that share. Above a quarter, TFT's rule will bind and the plan needs a daily cap. Below a quarter, the rule is unlikely to change anything.

The second test is the best three days. A rule that caps single days rarely caps three, but a rule that caps a rolling window does. Confirm the exact formulation before assuming that a single-day cap is the whole test.

There is a practical way to build for the stricter reading. Set a daily profit cap at two units and a hard stop for the session when it is reached. That produces a flat distribution by construction, and it satisfies a rolling-window rule as easily as a single-day one, at the cost of a little opportunity.

Lot sizing and pip value

Forex sizing is pips × pip value × lots. On a standard lot of a USD-quoted major, one pip is about $10, so a 25-pip stop risks $250 and a $500 unit buys two lots. A 50-pip stop buys one.

That $10 figure only holds for a dollar-quoted major. Yen pairs move the value with the exchange rate, crosses convert twice, and metals and indices are quoted in ticks rather than pips. Using one number across the board is the fastest way to be wrong on exactly the instruments that move most.

As a check, multiply the stop in pips by the pip value and confirm the result equals the intended dollar risk. If it does not, the lot count is wrong, and the error is cheaper to fix on a calculator than on a statement.

Leave room for spread and slip. The fill, not the order level, is what the floor records, and on a thin pair a stop that fills late can cost one and a half units instead of one. Ten to twenty percent of headroom covers it without shrinking the position to nothing.

MT4, MT5 and DXtrade

Both firms list MT4, MT5 and cTrader, so the main workflow decision is identical. FTMO adds DXtrade, a browser-based terminal with a cleaner interface than MT4 and no installation, which suits a trader who wants execution without maintenance.

Within the shared three, the choice is about style. MT4 is the familiar hedging platform with the largest library of expert advisors. MT5 adds a modern backtester and more instruments but defaults to netting, which changes how multiple entries on one symbol are managed. cTrader is the level-based option with depth of market and precise order handling.

Automation raises a rulebook question rather than a platform one. Some plans restrict high-frequency activity or certain order types, and an expert advisor can breach those clauses without intent. Read the activity clauses, test on a demo under the same rules, and keep the logs.

Data is usually included on both, which removes a line item that futures evaluations add. The remaining platform cost is the terminal itself and the time spent learning it.

Fees and retry maths

Both charge once per attempt. At an illustrative $450, three attempts cost $1,350 regardless of duration, which makes patience affordable and puts the cost of failure on the retry count rather than the calendar.

The expected cost is the fee divided by the pass probability. On a three-stage path the probability is the product of the stage probabilities, which is lower than either alone: three stages at 45% each give roughly 9%, turning $450 into about $5,000 per funded account.

On a two-stage path with a consistency condition the arithmetic is similar but the failure points differ. A trader who passes targets but fails distribution pays for the same number of attempts with less information per attempt.

The lever that improves both figures is a smaller unit. A unit a third lower usually converts a normal losing run from a breach into a drawdown, and because the fee is fixed, the expected cost falls by more than the profit per trade.

It is worth putting a number on that. At a 10% floor, a $500 unit gives twenty losses of tolerance on $100,000. A $350 unit gives twenty-eight. The difference sounds academic until the method's longest losing run is measured: if it is nine, both survive; if it is fourteen, both survive; if it is twenty-two, only the second one reaches phase two.

Seasoning against a cycle

FTMO's first payout waits for a seasoning period that begins when the funded account does, not when the evaluation is purchased. The clock therefore starts after a process that may already have taken two months.

TFT releases a profit split on a fixed cycle after qualification, which is a shorter path in calendar terms. The total time from first payment to cleared funds is the number to compare, and it is usually longer than either page suggests.

Both review the record before releasing profit. Statements show what happened; a journal shows why. On a static floor the statements are simple, which makes the journal the only place where reasoning is visible.

Identity and payment verification sit on the critical path on both. Names, addresses and the funding instrument have to match, and a since-replaced card means a manual check. Clear them during the attempt rather than after the target.

The minimum withdrawal is the other schedule detail. A cycle that pays monthly but requires a balance above a threshold pushes the first payout later than the calendar suggests, which matters for a trader using the profit as income rather than as capital for another attempt.

Releases and weekend gaps

The scheduled-event calendar is the same for both products, and the price response is brutal. Minutes before a release the book thins, the spread doubles, and a stop placed at a comfortable distance becomes a fill at an uncomfortable one.

Each rulebook treats the window differently. One bans fresh entries inside a stated period, another bans carrying a position through the event, a third bans capturing the spike. The third is the hardest to interpret, because a reviewer can apply it trade by trade or to the account as a whole.

Going flat beforehand and returning once the first hour has passed removes the dispute entirely. Anyone who chooses to stay in should halve the position and move the stop to a level where even a gap leaves the floor untouched.

Weekend gaps are the recurring version of the same risk, because a two-month path contains eight or nine of them. Either reduce the unit for weekend exposure or flatten on Friday, and make the choice a rule rather than a mood.

Which trader fits which

FTMO fits a trader who wants a longer path, a verification stage that tests repetition, no distribution rule during the evaluation, and a platform list that includes a browser terminal. The static floor suits a method with deep retracements.

TFT fits a trader whose method is already smooth, who wants a shorter path, and who is comfortable planning to a consistency condition from week one. A trader who wins in bursts should either change size or choose the other product.

The tie-breaker is the shape of the profit. A steady method can use either and should choose on payout timing. A lumpy method should choose FTMO, because a distribution rule it cannot satisfy is a rule that ends the attempt regardless of skill.

If both fit, simulate each on the same hundred trades: apply the three-stage path with no distribution rule to one, and the two-stage path with a cap to the other. The one that survives with the smaller drawdown is the structure the method already suits.

One behavioural question settles it faster than any simulation. When you have a good day, do you stop or press? A trader who stops fits TFT's distribution rule and will find the verification stage easy. A trader who presses fits neither without a written cap, and the cap is cheaper to adopt than a retry.

What to confirm before paying

The first question is whether the consistency rule applies during the evaluation, during funding, or only at payout. The second is the daily stop's basis and reset time. The third is whether the floor resets after a payout.

The fourth is the payout timing in total: seasoning period, cycle, minimum withdrawal. The fifth is the fee and whether a retry is charged again.

Finish with the conduct section: event windows, which order types are permitted, whether automation is allowed, and whether a third party may trade the account. Short clauses, real consequences.

File every answer with its date. Months later the folder settles arguments that memory only prolongs.

A thirty-day plan for either

Begin at half size and record rather than target. Log the buffer before each entry, note what the floor does after a winner, and write down the spread paid. By the end of week one the account has a documented behaviour.

Weeks two and three are execution: normal unit, a fixed setup list, a daily loss cap and a daily profit cap. The profit cap is there for the consistency rule, not for risk, and it is the cheapest way to satisfy a distribution test.

Finish at a third of the unit, on the same instrument, with the payout file read twice. The last fortnight kills more attempts than the first, usually because the finish looks close enough to chase.

The plan fits on a card: buffer, unit, session loss cap, session profit cap and the weekly stop. Five figures, revised at the open.

Common questions

Do both firms use a static floor?

Yes. FTMO measures its floor from the starting balance and TFT describes a static floor, so profit adds room rather than moving the failure point. A constant risk unit stays valid.

Which one applies a consistency test?

TFT applies a consistency condition. FTMO does not apply one to the evaluation, though the payout review still examines the record.

What does the verification stage add?

A third measured stage against the same floor, which tests whether the first result repeats. It is the stage where passes built on one oversized trade are most likely to fail.

How does the daily stop basis differ?

FTMO's is explicitly equity-based, so floating losses count immediately. TFT's basis should be confirmed in the plan document, because equity and realised bases behave differently on the final trade of a day.

Which pays sooner?

TFT releases on a fixed cycle after qualification, while FTMO waits for a seasoning period after the funded account begins. Compare total elapsed time rather than the cycle alone.

Is DXtrade available on both?

No. DXtrade appears on the FTMO platform list; TFT lists MT4, MT5 and cTrader. It matters only for a trader who wants a browser terminal without expert advisor support.
