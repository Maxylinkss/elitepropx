Prop Firm Passing Service

A passing service trades an evaluation on your behalf for a flat fee and hands back a funded account in your name. That one sentence hides the parts that decide whether the arrangement is a good idea for you: who holds the login, what the rule audit actually checks, how position size is derived from the loss floor, what happens if the attempt fails, and what the handover looks like when the target is reached. This page covers all five, in order.

Key takeaways

  • The service trades the evaluation only. The funded account is yours, with no profit split to the provider.
  • It is a process, not a promise: rule audit, risk plan, execution, reporting, retry policy, handover.
  • The first question is whether your firm permits third-party trading. Get the answer in writing, naming the clause.
  • Set a written retry policy before paying. "Retries covered" must mean a further full attempt at no extra service fee.
  • The provider needs trading access during the evaluation only. It should never hold the funded login.
  • Red flags: guarantees, a refusal to name accepted firms, a profit split, or pressure to pay off-platform.
  • The service removes the learning opportunity. That is a real cost if you plan to trade the funded account yourself.

The product in one paragraph

A prop firm passing service trades an evaluation on the client's behalf for a flat fee and returns a funded account in the client's name. It is not a signal service, a managed fund or a guarantee. The service reads the rulebook, builds a risk plan, executes the evaluation and reports progress. The client keeps ownership of the account and the profit.

The arrangement is only legitimate where the firm permits third-party trading. That is the first question to ask, and the answer should be in writing. A service that treats the terms check as a formality is a service that has not understood the risk it is taking on your behalf — and the risk lands on your account, not theirs.

It helps to be blunt about what is and is not being sold. The fee buys work: reading, planning, executing and reporting. It does not buy a pass, because no provider can guarantee the behaviour of a market. A provider that sells an outcome has mispriced its own risk, and the correction usually arrives as an unpaid retry policy.

The workflow, stage by stage

The sequence below is what a professional arrangement looks like. Timings are typical rather than fixed, and they assume a firm that permits the arrangement and an evaluation with a standard profit target.

Two stages in that table carry most of the risk. Stage one decides whether the arrangement is legal under the firm's terms, and stage seven decides what you actually own at the end. A provider that handles both explicitly is behaving like a professional. A provider that treats the terms check as a formality and the handover as an afterthought has inverted the priorities.

What the rule audit checks

Prop firm rulebooks are short documents with long consequences. The audit exists so the attempt is planned against the actual numbers rather than the marketing summary, and it should cover every item below.

RuleWhy it changes the planQuestion to ask
Profit targetSets the distance to completion and therefore the number of trading days requiredIs the target a percentage of the initial balance or of a moving figure?
Maximum lossDefines the risk budget for the whole attemptIs it static, or does it trail?
Daily loss limitCaps how much can be lost in one session, independent of the total floorIs it calculated on balance or on equity, and does it reset at midnight server time?
Trailing behaviourA trailing floor follows unrealised profit and shortens the effective life of the accountDoes it trail on closed profit only, or on open positions too?
Consistency ruleCaps the best day as a share of total profit, often enforced at payoutIs it applied during the evaluation, at funding, or at the first withdrawal?
Minimum trading daysForces the attempt to spread across sessions even when the target is reached earlyDoes the funded phase carry its own minimum?
News restrictionsRemoves tradable windows and can invalidate trades held through an eventWhich events, and how long before and after?
Weekend and holding rulesDetermines whether positions can be carried through a closeIs the restriction on the evaluation, the funded account, or both?
Instrument and lot limitsExcludes markets or sizes that a plan may depend onAre there maximum lots or maximum open positions?
Prohibited practicesDefines what can void the account after the fact, including third-party tradingWhich clause governs third-party trading, and what is the stated consequence?

The last row is the one that decides whether the service route exists. The others decide how it is executed. Together they turn a challenge from a general ambition into a specific set of limits, which is the only form in which a risk plan can be written.

Save a dated copy of the terms. Rulebooks change between plan generations, and the version that governed your attempt is the version that matters. A dated copy, plus the checkout confirmation, plus the written terms check, is the complete evidence set if a payout is ever disputed.

How position size is derived

The single most common planning error on an evaluation is expressing risk in dollars instead of as a share of the loss floor. A $50,000 account with a 5% floor has a $2,500 budget; the same account at $100,000 has $5,000. Same percentage, different dollars, and the plan should be expressed as percentages so it transfers between sizes.

Worked example one — 1% risk per trade on a $50,000 account with a 5% floor. Each trade risks $500, which is 1% of the initial balance and 20% of the entire loss budget. Ten consecutive losses exhausts the floor. The plan therefore needs either a lower risk per trade or a stop condition that halts trading well before the floor is touched.

Worked example two — 0.5% risk per trade on the same account. Each trade risks $250, which is 10% of the budget and allows twenty consecutive losses before the floor is threatened. The trade-off is speed: at smaller size, reaching an 8% target takes longer, which raises the number of sessions and the exposure to news and weekend rules.

Risk per tradeDollar risk on $50,000Share of a 5% floorConsecutive losses to breachRealistic pace to an 8% target
1.0%$50020%10Fast, but fragile
0.75%$37515%13Balanced
0.5%$25010%20Slower, safer
0.25%$1255%40Too slow for a typical window

The plan also needs a stop condition — the point at which the attempt is abandoned rather than continued. A common rule is to stop when the account has lost 60% of the floor, because an account that is 3% down on a 5% floor cannot recover without taking risk that would breach. Ending an attempt deliberately at that point preserves the option to reset on your own terms rather than the firm's.

Who holds what, and when

Access is where most of the practical risk sits, and it is worth writing down explicitly before anything is handed over.

ItemEvaluation phaseFunded phase
Trading loginProvider, for executionYou only
Account owner of recordYouYou
Email on the accountYours, so firm notices reach youYours
Payout destinationNot set up yetYours, configured by you
Password changesChanged when access is returnedChanged at handover
Provider access after the passEndsNone

Three details in that table are non-negotiable. The account must be in your name, the email on the account must be yours so that firm notifications reach you directly, and the provider must lose access when the evaluation ends. A provider that keeps the funded login has retained control of your money, whatever the contract says.

On the security side, prefer temporary or limited credentials over a shared personal password, and change everything at handover. If the firm supports read-only or investor access for monitoring, use it rather than handing over your own account credentials. If it does not, at minimum use a password that exists for that account alone.

The third-party trading clause

This is the threshold question and the one most often glossed over in marketing. Firms differ enormously: some explicitly prohibit third-party or assisted trading, some prohibit account sharing while remaining silent on assistance, and some permit it outright. Enforcement is inconsistent between firms and sometimes between cohorts at the same firm, but the consequence when it is enforced tends to be the same: the account is closed and the payout is refused.

Terms positionWhat it means for the service routeRisk if ignored
Explicitly prohibitedDo not proceed. The route is unavailable at any price.Account closure and refused payout
Account sharing prohibited, assistance silentA legal reading may allow it, but the ambiguity belongs to youClosure at the firm's discretion
Explicitly permittedThe route is available; the remaining questions are cost and scopeLow, subject to the other rules

The honest position is that this risk belongs to the account holder. No service can transfer it, remove it, or indemnify you against it. Any provider that claims otherwise is describing a guarantee it cannot make. That is why the terms check has to come before payment, and why it should be written down: a dated note naming the clause you relied on is the only protection you have if the firm later changes its position.

How to vet a provider

You are buying several weeks of access to an account that holds your evaluation fee and, later, your payouts. That justifies a few questions, and the answers should be written.

QuestionA good answerA warning sign
Which firms do you accept?A named list with terms already checked"Any firm, guaranteed"
Who holds the login?The service during the evaluation onlyThe service keeps the funded account
What if the account breaches?Retry covered under the same feeA new fee, or no stated policy
Do you take a profit split?No — the funded account is yoursA percentage of funded profit
Can I see the terms check?A written confirmation per firmRefusal, or a generic claim
How is progress reported?Regular updates against target and floorSilence until the pass, or nothing at all
What is the timeline?A range, with the retry terms if it runs longA fixed date treated as a promise
How do I pay?A documented method that leaves a recordUntraceable payment, or pressure to pay now

Read the warning column as a set rather than a list. A single weak answer is a question to press on; three weak answers is a provider to walk away from. The pattern that should end the conversation immediately is a combination of a guaranteed outcome and a demand for a method of payment that leaves no record, because that pairing has no innocent explanation.

Retry policy mechanics

Retries are the most frequently tested term in the entire arrangement, because attempts fail for ordinary reasons. A policy worth accepting will answer four questions in writing.

  1. How many further attempts are covered? One, two, or unlimited within a stated period. "Unlimited" is only meaningful with a time limit and a definition of what counts as an attempt.
  2. Is the firm's reset fee included? Where the firm charges to restart, either the provider absorbs it or you do. The quote should say which.
  3. Within what window? A retry covered for thirty days is a different product from one covered indefinitely.
  4. What would void it? Attempts ended by a rule breach you caused, a credential you changed, or a plan you altered may fall outside the policy. That boundary should be stated before payment, not discovered afterward.

A provider whose retry policy cannot be written in four lines does not have a retry policy; it has an intention. Intentions are worth nothing at the moment you need them.

The handover at the pass

The pass is not the end of the arrangement, it is the transfer point. A clean handover has five parts, and all five should happen at the same time.

  • Access ends. Any credentials held by the provider are rotated, and the provider confirms it no longer has access.
  • The funded login reaches you. You should be able to log in, see the balance and the rules, and place or hold a position without the provider's involvement.
  • Payout details are set up by you. Your payout method, your identity documents, your bank or wallet. The provider should never be a party to your withdrawals.
  • The funded terms are re-read. Funded rules are not evaluation rules. Consistency requirements, minimum payout cycles and data fees usually appear only at this stage.
  • The evidence set is filed. Firm confirmation, the written scope, the terms check, and the pass result, kept together.

The fourth item is where many traders lose the account within a month. An evaluation can be passed under one rulebook and the funded account governed by another, with a consistency rule that only applies at payout. Reading the funded terms on the day of the handover, rather than the day of the first withdrawal, is the difference between a plan that works and a surprise.

Alternatives, and how they differ

A passing service is one of several ways to reach funded capital, and the honest comparison includes the options that are not the same product at all.

The distinctions matter because the risks are not equivalent. A signal service leaves the rule compliance with you, so it does not solve the problem that usually causes failure. Copy trading can breach a consistency rule without warning, because the strategy being copied has never read your firm's rulebook. Managed accounts and account sales raise questions the passing-service model does not, including who is legally responsible for the trading and whether the firm's terms permit the transfer at all.

Three firm archetypes, and how the plan changes

Rulebooks read as though they were written to a template, but the planning implications diverge sharply. Grouping firms into archetypes is useful because the risk plan that works for one is close to a different product for another.

ArchetypeDefining rulesWhat it does to the plan
Static-floor futures evaluationFixed maximum loss, single phase, target around 6%The floor is a fixed budget, so risk per trade can be set and held. The binding constraint is pace, not survival.
Trailing-floor futures evaluationMaximum loss trails unrealised profitThe effective budget shrinks as the account gains. Positions must be closed before giving back gains, which changes hold times and often removes the option of letting a winner run.
Forex evaluation with payout-stage consistencyTarget around 8–10%, daily loss limit, consistency applied at payoutPassing is not the finish line. The distribution of daily profits has to be planned from the first trade, because a single oversized day can block the first withdrawal months later.

The trailing archetype is the one that most often catches out a plan borrowed from elsewhere. A strategy that risks 1% per trade and holds winners through a retrace looks reasonable on a static floor and is genuinely dangerous on a trailing one, because the floor follows the unrealised peak. The same trades that produce the profit are the trades that raise the floor, so an open winner is simultaneously the best and the riskiest position in the account.

The consistency archetype is the one most often misunderstood. Traders treat it as a payout formality until the first withdrawal is refused because one day produced 40% of the total profit. Under a typical rule that caps the best day at a share of total profit, the remedy is not fewer good days — it is more ordinary ones, which means lengthening the evaluation deliberately rather than passing it as fast as possible. That is a counterintuitive instruction, and it is exactly the kind of thing a rule audit is supposed to surface before the first trade rather than after the pass.

Ask which archetype your firm is. The answer determines risk per trade, hold time, session choice and pace. A provider that prices one plan for every firm is implicitly assuming the rulebooks are interchangeable. They are not.

How assisted attempts fail anyway

Because the process removes the emotional failure modes, the remaining causes of failure are structural. Knowing them in advance is most of the defence.

Failure modeHow it happensHow the plan prevents it
Floor breach on a trailing rulebookOpen profit raises the floor; the position is closed lower than the new floorTighter take-profit levels and a rule to close before the peak is given back
Daily loss limit hit on one sessionTwo losses in the same day exceed the daily cap even though the total floor is intactA daily stop counted in units of risk, not in dollars, enforced before the limit is reached
Consistency failure at payoutThe single best day exceeds the permitted share of total profitProfit distribution planned across days from the first position
Minimum-day shortfallThe target is reached in fewer days than the firm requiresPace deliberately stretched across the required number of sessions
Rule change mid-attemptThe firm updates its plan or terms while the evaluation is runningA dated copy of the terms at the start, and a re-read at every payout-relevant step
Access problem at handoverCredentials, two-factor prompts or email ownership block the transferEmail on the account belongs to the client; access rotated at the pass

Notice that none of these are trading-skill failures. They are planning and administrative failures, which is the category a service is genuinely good at removing, and the reason the fee buys something real even though it cannot buy a result. A skilled trader loses evaluations to a trailing floor and a payout consistency rule as readily as an unskilled one does; the difference is that the skilled trader had the option of reading the rules first.

When a passing service is the wrong choice

There are four clear cases, and it is worth stating them plainly rather than leaving them implied.

Your firm prohibits the arrangement. Nothing else on this page applies. The consequence is a closed account and a refused payout, and the fee does not begin to cover that.

You want to learn the rulebook. The rule knowledge is the asset that survives the funded account. Buying a pass skips the part of the process that makes the funded account survivable, which is why some traders pass and then lose the account inside a month.

You already have a tested method and a high pass probability. Above roughly a 70% chance of passing first time, self-trading is cheaper on expected cost. The arithmetic is set out in the cost breakdown.

Your bottleneck is money, not time. If the fee is capital you need elsewhere, the correct decision is to trade a smaller account or a demo until it is not. No version of the expected-value calculation makes an unaffordable fee affordable.

Inside the remaining case — a trader with limited hours, a firm that permits assistance, and no interest in managing the evaluation personally — the arrangement is a straightforward trade of money for time, and it usually works out cheaper than the expected cost of repeated attempts. That is a narrow but genuine set of circumstances, and being precise about which side of the line you are on is the whole decision.

What the fee should and should not include

The scope of a flat fee is worth reading closely, because two quotes with the same headline number can cover very different amounts of work.

It should cover the rule audit, the risk plan, the execution, progress reporting and the retry policy stated in the quote. It should not include a share of funded profit, and it should not require you to hand over a personal account holding your own capital. The evaluation fee paid to the firm may or may not be included, and the quote should say which rather than leaving it to be inferred.

Retries are the part of the scope that matters most in practice. A provider charging a full fee per attempt is selling a per-attempt product with a flat-fee label; a provider covering retries has priced the probability of failure into the initial fee. Neither is automatically better, but the policy should be written down and the expected number of attempts should form part of the comparison rather than being assumed away.

Finally, ask what happens if the attempt runs long. A timeline that is a range, with a stated position on what happens when the range is exceeded, is a professional answer. A fixed date presented as a commitment is not, because the provider does not control the market, the news calendar or the minimum-day rule.

Reporting, and why the cadence matters

During the evaluation you are not trading, but you are still exposed: the account carries your name, and any rule breach lands on you rather than on the provider. That makes reporting part of the risk management rather than a courtesy.

Useful reporting has three features. It shows progress against the profit target and the distance to the loss floor, so you can see both sides of the attempt at once. It identifies which rules are binding, since a trailing floor or a daily limit changes the picture long before the target is reached. And it arrives on a predictable schedule, so silence is never ambiguous. A weekly update on a three-week evaluation is the minimum; anything less and you learn about a problem after the account has already ended.

Common questions

What exactly is a prop firm passing service?

A service that trades a prop firm evaluation on the client's behalf for a flat fee and returns a funded account in the client's name. It does not manage the funded account, does not take a profit share, and stops at the handover.

Is using one legal?

It depends on the firm's terms rather than on any general rule. The arrangement is legitimate only where the firm permits third-party trading, and the specific clause should be identified in writing before payment. A prohibited arrangement can lead to a closed account and a refused payout.

Does the provider own the funded account?

No. The evaluation and the funded account are opened in your name, on your email, and the payouts are configured by you. The provider's access should end at the handover. If a provider expects to keep the funded login, that is a different product with a different risk profile.

What should I verify before paying?

Seven things: the specific terms clause, the named list of accepted firms, the fee and what it includes, the retry policy in writing, the timeline, who holds the login during and after the evaluation, and whether any share of funded profit is taken. Get the answers in writing.

How long does it take?

Typically around two weeks, but the number is governed by the target and the minimum trading day rule rather than by the provider's speed. A firm requiring eight profitable days cannot be completed in five, however well the account is traded. Treat any fixed promise of a specific date as marketing.

What happens if the evaluation fails?

It depends on the written retry policy. A policy worth accepting covers a further full attempt at no additional service fee, states whether the firm's reset fee is included, gives a window, and defines what would void the cover. Ask for those four points before paying rather than after a failure.

Will the funded account survive after the handover?

That is your responsibility, and it is the stage most traders underestimate. Funded rules differ from evaluation rules: consistency requirements often apply only at payout, minimum cycles delay the first withdrawal, and data fees may resume. Read the funded terms at the handover rather than at the first payout request.

How is this different from a signal or copy-trading service?

A signal or copy service leaves rule compliance with you — you still carry the drawdown and the responsibility for consistency, news and minimum-day rules. A passing service takes on the execution and the rule audit. That is why the costs and the risks are not comparable.
